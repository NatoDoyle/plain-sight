# Competitors & Business

> The arena where rivalry is legitimate but the methods can cross into deception, theft, and fraud. Hard competition — better products, truthful comparison, aggressive negotiation, lawful hiring — is the healthy norm; the line is crossed when a competitor manufactures fear, fakes consensus, steals secrets, or negotiates in bad faith. Most business competition is fair; this dossier is about the exceptions.

## The arena

Businesses compete for customers, talent, capital, and reputation, and the incentives to win can tempt manipulation of buyers, partners, employees, and the market's perception. The asymmetries that make targets capturable: information gaps (you can't easily verify a rival's claims about your product), switching costs (fear of leaving a dominant vendor), dependence on partners who can act in bad faith, and the difficulty of detecting covert moves (astroturfing, pretexting). The defensive frame is unusually clean here because the **law itself draws the line**: competition is protected and encouraged; what's actionable is *deception* (false disparagement, fake reviews), *theft* (trade secrets), and *fraud* (bad-faith contracting). The goal is to recognize when a competitor has stopped competing and started cheating.

## Prevalence & base rates

There is **no reliable quantitative base rate** for how often businesses face manipulation by competitors (such figures are scarce and methodologically shaky — this dossier won't invent one). The qualitative reality: the overwhelming majority of business rivalry is legitimate, and antitrust/unfair-competition law is explicitly designed to **protect competition, not competitors** — intervening only at deception, theft, or fraud, not at vigorous (even brutal) fair rivalry. Treat "a competitor is winning / undercutting / poaching" as ordinary business, not manipulation, absent the specific illegitimate methods below.

## Top tactics in this arena

- **[[fud-competitor-tactics]]** — Fear, Uncertainty, Doubt (popularized by Gene Amdahl ~1975 describing IBM's tactics): sowing doubt about a rival's product/viability to freeze buyers, switching-cost terror, and **vaporware** (pre-announcing products to stall a competitor's sales — IBM's high-end System/360 announcements timed against Control Data's 6600 are the classic case).
- **[[fake-reviews-astroturfing]]** — manufactured grassroots support or reviews; sockpuppets, review farms, front groups that hide their sponsor (now squarely illegal — see Legal).
- **[[smear-campaigns]]** / commercial disparagement — false statements about a competitor's goods (lawful comparison is fine; *false* comparison is actionable).
- **[[bad-faith-argumentation]]** in negotiations and partnerships — bad-faith contracting, sham negotiations to extract information or stall, "nibbling," and manufactured leverage.
- **[[veiled-threats]]** — litigation threats, supplier/channel pressure, "it would be a shame if…" against smaller rivals or partners.
- **[[phishing-pretexting]]** / espionage-adjacent social engineering — pretexting for competitive intelligence (the **HP pretexting scandal, 2006** is the textbook example), fake job interviews to extract information, and trade-secret theft.

## Arena-specific dynamics

Sustained competitor manipulation tends to combine reputation attacks ([[smear-campaigns]], [[fake-reviews-astroturfing]]) with market-perception manipulation (FUD/vaporware) and, in the gravest cases, information theft (pretexting → trade secrets). Where a "partnership" or acquisition talk is really a vehicle to extract secrets or freeze a rival, the [[con-anatomy]] arc (trust-building → extraction) maps onto B2B deception.

## Red-flag checklist

- A competitor's claims about *your* product are specific, damaging, and false (not mere puffery).
- Sudden waves of suspiciously similar negative reviews, or "grassroots" opposition with hidden backers.
- "Partnership"/diligence requests that probe for trade secrets far beyond the stated purpose.
- Pre-announced competing products that never quite ship, timed to freeze your sales (vaporware).
- Unsolicited contacts (fake recruiters, "researchers," "customers") fishing for confidential information.
- Bad-faith negotiation: moving terms, manufactured deadlines, leverage that appears only after you've committed.

## Legal protections & reporting

*US, jurisdiction-labeled; not legal advice — and some of this is fast-changing.* **False advertising / commercial disparagement:** the **Lanham Act §43(a) (15 U.S.C. §1125(a))** creates a federal civil claim for false/misleading commercial statements about your or a competitor's goods — but **only competitors (not consumers) have standing**; truthful comparative advertising is legal, false comparison is actionable; state trade-libel torts also exist. **Fake reviews/astroturfing:** the FTC's **Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials** (16 CFR Part 465, effective Oct 21, 2024) bans fake/AI-generated reviews, review suppression, and undisclosed insider reviews, with civil penalties (~$50,000+/violation, inflation-adjusted). **Trade secrets:** the **Defend Trade Secrets Act (2016)** (federal civil), the **Uniform Trade Secrets Act** (state), and the **Economic Espionage Act (1996)** (criminal) — *poaching employees is generally legal; stealing trade secrets is not*. **Non-competes are in flux:** the FTC's 2024 nationwide ban was **vacated in court (2024) and abandoned by the FTC (2025)** — there is **no federal non-compete ban; enforceability is governed by state law** (e.g., California bans most), so verify current local law.

## Exit & resources

Unlike interpersonal arenas, "exit" here is competitive and legal response: **document** the conduct ([[documentation-practices]]), preserve evidence (suspicious reviews, communications, timelines), and route to counsel for Lanham Act / trade-secret / unfair-competition claims. Report fake reviews and deceptive practices to the **FTC (reportfraud.ftc.gov)**; report suspected trade-secret theft / economic espionage to the **FBI**. Harden against social engineering ([[verification-rituals]], [[phishing-pretexting]]): verify unusual information requests out-of-band, and train staff that "researchers"/"recruiters"/"customers" fishing for internal detail may be pretexting.

## Caveats

- **Most competition is legitimate and good.** A rival with a better product, lower price, smarter marketing, or the ability to hire your people away is *competing*, not manipulating ([[epistemic-guardrails]]). The law protects this; so should your judgment.
- **Hard ≠ dirty.** Aggressive negotiation, truthful comparative advertising, public competitive intelligence, and lawful recruiting are normal hardball — not fraud. The line is **deception, theft, or fraud**, not intensity.
- **"FUD" and "astroturf" are themselves weaponizable labels** — dismissing a competitor's *legitimate* criticism or a *genuine* grassroots backlash as "just FUD" or "astroturfing" is its own bad-faith move. Reserve the labels for actual deception with evidence.
- **No invented numbers:** be wary of anyone (including this KB) citing precise prevalence stats for competitor manipulation — they're not reliably established.

## Sources

1. FUD / vaporware: popularized by Gene Amdahl (~1975) re: IBM; vaporware market-freezing (IBM System/360 Model 91 vs. Control Data Corp). "Nobody got fired for buying IBM" is an anonymous industry adage (no confirmed origin). Folk/industry history.
2. Astroturfing: term coined by Sen. Lloyd Bentsen (1985). FTC Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials, 16 CFR Part 465 (effective Oct 21, 2024) — bans fake/AI reviews, suppression, undisclosed insider reviews; per-violation civil penalty inflation-adjusted (~$50K+). Established (regulatory).
3. Lanham Act §43(a), 15 U.S.C. §1125(a) — federal false-advertising/disparagement claim; competitor (not consumer) standing; truthful comparative advertising lawful. Established law (US).
4. Trade secrets: Defend Trade Secrets Act (2016, federal civil); Uniform Trade Secrets Act (state); Economic Espionage Act (1996, criminal). Non-compete status: FTC 2024 rule vacated (*Ryan, LLC v. FTC*, 2024) and abandoned by FTC (2025) — no federal ban; state law governs. US; jurisdiction-/date-specific; not legal advice.
5. HP pretexting scandal (2006) — private investigators used pretexting to obtain phone records of board members and journalists; ~$14.5M California settlement; spurred anti-pretexting law. Documented case.
