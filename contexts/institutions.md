# Institutions & Gatekeepers

> The arena of expert power: doctors, lawyers, financial advisers, schools, and bureaucracies you depend on but cannot easily evaluate or escape. The structural asymmetry — they know, you don't; they gatekeep what you need — *creates the opportunity* for exploitation, betrayal, and dismissal. But most professionals act in good faith, and the hard task here is separating genuine abuse from ordinary expert limits, bad outcomes, and institutions lawfully pursuing their own interests.

## The arena

Institutions hold two things over those who depend on them: **information** (expertise the layperson can't verify) and **access** (to health, justice, money, credentials, employment). Economists call medical/legal/financial/repair services **credence goods** — you often can't judge quality even *after* you receive them (Darby & Karni, 1973) — which structurally enables **over-treatment, over-charging, and steering**. Layered on this is **institutional betrayal**: the institution you depend on failing or harming you, especially after you report wrongdoing, and **institutional DARVO** — when an institution Denies, Attacks, and Reverses Victim and Offender to protect itself (Jennifer Freyd's extensions of [[darvo]]). The defensive frame: the asymmetry is real and worth guarding against, but it is *opportunity, not proof* — the goal is recognizing exploitation of the gap, not treating every professional as a predator.

## Prevalence & base rates

There's no clean prevalence figure, and the honest base rate is essential: **most doctors, lawyers, advisers, and institutions are competent and act in good faith.** The credence-goods asymmetry creates *opportunity* for abuse, and documented patterns exist (over-treatment, conflicts of interest, dismissal of certain patients), but a bad outcome, a good-faith disagreement, a denied claim, or an institution following its own legitimate rules is **not** by itself abuse, betrayal, or "gaslighting" (see Caveats — this is the arena most prone to over-labeling).

## Top patterns in this arena

- **Information-asymmetry exploitation** ([[information-asymmetry]]): the credence-goods problem — over-treatment, unnecessary procedures/services, over-charging, and steering you toward what profits the provider.
- **Institutional betrayal & institutional [[darvo]]** (Freyd): institutions denying and reversing victim/offender when held accountable — disbelieving reporters, retaliating against whistleblowers, protecting the institution over the harmed.
- **Medical dismissal / "medical gaslighting"** ([[gaslighting]] — *contested extension*): symptoms minimized, psychologized, or disbelieved — disproportionately for women, racial minorities, and chronic-pain/chronic-illness patients. (See Caveats: the harm is real; the "gaslighting" label, which implies *intent*, often overstates it.)
- **Financial steering / conflicts of interest:** the **fiduciary vs. suitability** gap — investment advisers owe a fiduciary duty (Investment Advisers Act 1940), while brokers historically owed only "suitability," now "best interest" (SEC Reg BI, 2020) — a *weaker* standard; plus churning (excess trading for commissions) and undisclosed high-fee products.
- **Legal process abuse:** **SLAPP** suits (Strategic Lawsuits Against Public Participation — litigation to silence/intimidate via cost and delay), vexatious litigation, and abuse of process.
- **Gatekeeping by attrition:** denial, delay, and bureaucratic [[circular-conversation]] that exhaust a claimant until they give up.

## Arena-specific dynamics

The recurring dynamic is **the asymmetry exploited plus the institution closing ranks**: you can't evaluate the service, you depend on access, and when you push back the institution may deploy [[darvo]] (deny/attack/reverse) and [[smear-campaigns]] against a complainant or whistleblower. Predators inside institutions also exploit institutional trust — the [[grooming-sequence]]'s "grooming of the environment" (a trusted reputation that pre-discredits disclosures) is an institutional dynamic.

## Red-flag checklist

- You're discouraged from seeking a second opinion, outside records, or independent advice.
- Recommended services conveniently maximize the provider's revenue; conflicts of interest go undisclosed.
- Your documented concerns vanish, get reframed as your problem, or trigger retaliation.
- "Trust me, you wouldn't understand" substitutes for transparent explanation.
- A complaint is met with the institution attacking *your* credibility rather than addressing the issue (institutional DARVO).
- Legal threats or process used to exhaust/silence you rather than resolve a dispute (SLAPP).

## Legal protections & reporting

*US, jurisdiction-labeled; not legal advice.* You have more recourse here than in most arenas — use the **regulators and complaint channels**: **Medical** — state medical boards (discipline; public actions via the FSMB), hospital patient advocates/ombudsmen, your **HIPAA right to access your records** (45 CFR 164.524), and second opinions. **Legal** — state bar disciplinary boards; in many states, **anti-SLAPP statutes** (a special motion to strike, often with fee-shifting — coverage varies widely, no federal statute). **Financial** — **FINRA** (broker complaints/arbitration), **SEC** (tips/complaints), **CFPB** (consumerfinance.gov/complaint — banks, cards, loans), and state securities regulators; insist on **fiduciary** advisers where it matters. **General** — ombudsmen, regulators, and documentation are your leverage against the asymmetry.

## Exit & resources

The counters are structural: **get a second (independent) opinion** — the single best defense against credence-goods exploitation; **get and keep your own records** ([[documentation-practices]], HIPAA access); **verify credentials and conflicts** independently ([[verification-rituals]]); insist on **fiduciary** financial advice; and **escalate in writing** through the regulator/ombudsman channels above, which create a record the institution must answer. For disputes, consider an independent professional (a second doctor, an outside lawyer, a fee-only fiduciary planner) rather than relying solely on the institution that holds the conflict.

## Caveats

- **Most professionals and institutions act in good faith and competently.** The asymmetry creates *opportunity* for abuse; it does not mean abuse is the norm ([[epistemic-guardrails]]). A poor outcome, an honest disagreement, a denied (but legitimate) claim, or an institution following its own lawful interests is not "abuse," "betrayal," or "gaslighting."
- **"Medical gaslighting" — real harm, imperfect label:** symptom dismissal and diagnostic bias (especially against women, minorities, and chronic-illness patients) are real and documented. But "gaslighting" implies *deliberate* reality-distortion, while most dismissal stems from **bias, time pressure, diagnostic uncertainty, or error — not intentional manipulation** (Durbhakula & Fortin, *JGIM* 2023, propose "diagnostic misattribution"). Hold both: take the harm seriously *and* don't impute malice that usually isn't there. The construct of *intentional* medical gaslighting is `contested`; *biased dismissal* is `supported`.
- **The labels are weaponizable both ways.** Institutions can dismiss legitimate complaints as "you don't understand," and individuals can mislabel ordinary professional limits as "abuse"/"gaslighting." Reserve the strong terms for demonstrable patterns (conflicts of interest, retaliation, intentional misrepresentation, institutional DARVO).
- **Credence-goods exploitation is a *structural opportunity*, not a personal tactic** — the economics, not a diagnosis of any individual professional.
