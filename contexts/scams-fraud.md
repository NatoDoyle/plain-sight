# Scams & Fraud

> The arena of strangers (and fake friends) engineering you out of your money. Losses are enormous and rising, the tactics are professionally designed, and — the single most important reframe — **getting scammed is not a sign of stupidity.** Scams exploit universal human psychology (trust, urgency, fear, hope); smart, educated, careful people are defrauded every day, and shame is the scammer's most useful ally because it keeps victims silent.

## The arena

Fraud is a global, industrialized business — including literal scam compounds — targeting anyone reachable by phone, text, email, app, or dating site. The capturing levers are the KB's core mechanisms weaponized at a distance: manufactured [[scarcity-urgency]] ("act now"), borrowed [[authority]] (fake banks/agencies), [[trust-mechanics]] (impersonation, fabricated relationships), and [[hope-greed]] (impossible returns). The defining defensive facts: (1) the harm is financial *and* emotional, sometimes catastrophic; (2) the victim pool is everyone, not just "the gullible elderly"; and (3) the universal counter is the same across every scam type — **slow down and verify through an independent channel** ([[verification-rituals]]).

## Prevalence & base rates

Reported losses are large and growing — and these are *reported* figures (the true totals are far higher, since most fraud goes unreported out of shame). **Cite by agency and year; do not merge the datasets** (different scope/methodology):
- **FTC (Consumer Sentinel, 2024):** ~**$12.5 billion** reported lost to fraud (+25% over 2023) [1].
- **FBI IC3 (2024):** ~**$16.6 billion** in reported losses (+33%), ~860,000 complaints [1].
- **By dollars lost, investment/crypto fraud leads** (FTC 2024: ~$5.7B; pig-butchering drives much of it); **by number of reports, imposter scams and online shopping lead** [1].

**Who's targeted — correct the stereotype:** *younger* adults (esp. 20s) report *losing money* to fraud at higher *rates*, while *older* adults lose **more money per incident** (median loss rises steeply with age); elder aggregate losses are huge and badly under-reported (FTC's 2025 estimate of the true cost to older adults spans a wide $10B–$80B+ range) [1]. So "only gullible old people get scammed" is false — the scam pool is everyone ([[elder-targeting]] covers the elder-specific dynamics).

## Top tactics in this arena

- **Trust/identity fabrication:** [[mirroring-false-identity]] (catfishing, fake personas), [[charm-offensive]], [[pity-plays]] (the hard-luck opener).
- **Impersonation & pretexting:** [[phishing-pretexting]] — fake bank/government/tech-support/boss, urgency + authority across channels.
- **Urgency & pressure:** [[manufactured-urgency]] ("your account will be suspended"), demands for irreversible payment (gift cards, wire, crypto).
- **Greed/affinity hooks:** [[future-faking]] (guaranteed returns), [[affinity-fraud]] (exploiting shared faith/ethnicity/community), [[ponzi-psychology]].
- **Cold reading & persuasion:** [[cold-reading]] (psychics, "recovery experts," some "investment gurus").
- **AI-enabled (the major emerging trend):** [[ai-enabled-manipulation]] — voice cloning (a 3-second clip enables a convincing "grandchild in trouble"), deepfakes, AI chat personas in romance/investment scams.

## Arena-specific dynamics

The full scam arcs live in their own files: the general [[con-anatomy]] (put-up → play → rope → tale → **convincer** → send → touch → blow-off), the [[romance-scam-arc]] (incl. pig-butchering), and the [[mlm-lifecycle]]. A recurring late-stage dynamic is the **recovery scam**: prior victims are re-targeted by fake "recovery" services promising to retrieve lost funds for an upfront fee (advance-fee fraud, traded on "sucker lists") — *legitimate agencies never charge to recover your money.* Many scams also run [[isolation-tactics]], [[gaslighting]], and [[love-bombing]] — the same coercive toolkit as abusive relationships.

## Red-flag checklist

- Contact you didn't initiate, invoking urgency + a problem or a prize.
- Any demand for payment in **gift cards, wire transfer, or crypto** (these are scam-defining).
- Pressure to act *now*, in secret ("don't tell your bank/family").
- An online-only relationship that turns to money or "investment" before you've met.
- Guaranteed/too-good-to-be-true returns; a platform only your contact recommends.
- A small early "win" or withdrawal that precedes a request for much more (the convincer).
- Being asked to verify via a number/link *they* gave you (verify independently instead).

## Legal protections & reporting

*US, jurisdiction-labeled; not legal advice.* Report fraud to the **FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov** (1-877-382-4357) and the **FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center at ic3.gov**; identity theft to **identitytheft.gov**. Specialized help: **AARP Fraud Watch Network helpline 877-908-3360** (free, non-members welcome, emotional support + guidance); **BBB Scam Tracker** (report/search; reports accepted even with no money lost); **DOJ National Elder Fraud Hotline 1-833-372-8311** (case-manager support). Irreversible rails (wire/crypto/gift card) are usually unrecoverable, so speed of reporting to your bank/card issuer matters; dispute card charges promptly. Statutes vary; fraud is criminal everywhere, but cross-border recovery is often impractical — prevention beats recourse.

## Exit & resources

If you've been scammed: **stop all contact and payments immediately**, report (above), alert your bank/card issuer, and **do not engage any "recovery" offer** (it's the next scam). Change passwords and enable MFA if credentials were exposed. The defensive habit that prevents most of this is procedural — independent verification and a cooling-off pause on any urgent money request ([[verification-rituals]], [[detection-heuristics]]). See Safety notes for the emotional toll.

## Caveats

- **Being scammed is not stupidity.** Scams are professionally engineered to exploit universal psychology; the victims are disproportionately *busy, trusting, and in-good-faith*, and include the highly educated and financially sophisticated. Self-blame is both wrong and counterproductive (it suppresses reporting and aids re-victimization).
- **Not every bad deal, loss, or unmet expectation is fraud.** A risky investment that lost value, a disappointing purchase, a business that failed, or hard bargaining are not scams ([[epistemic-guardrails]]). Fraud requires *deception* — misrepresentation of material facts designed to take your money.
- **"Scam" is also a weaponizable label** — used to smear legitimate businesses, competitors, or disliked counterparties. Reserve it for actual deception, and verify before accusing.
- **The over-detection cost is real but lopsided here:** healthy skepticism toward *unsolicited, urgent, irreversible-payment* requests is almost always warranted; the rule "verify before you pay" rarely produces a costly false positive.

## Safety notes

`safety: crisis-escalation`. Fraud — especially romance and investment fraud — can be financially and emotionally devastating, and **suicidality among defrauded victims is documented**. If you or someone you know is in crisis after a scam, this is a moment for compassion and support, never "you should have known." **US: 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline** (call/text 988). Report and seek support (FTC, AARP helpline 877-908-3360, DOJ Elder Fraud Hotline 1-833-372-8311); for sextortion within a scam, see [[romance-scam-arc]]'s safety guidance (don't pay/comply; preserve evidence; report to the FBI; "it's never too late to tell"). Respond to the person's safety and shame first.
