# Boundary Scripts

> The actual words for setting and holding a limit under pressure — ready-made language for a stressed person who needs a sentence *now*. The governing principle: **a boundary is about what *you* will do, not about controlling the other person** — and you do not owe anyone the justification they're demanding. **Safety first: with an abusive or controlling partner, direct assertion can escalate danger — read "When NOT to use it" before anything else.**

## What it counters

The action layer once you've decided where your limit is — the declared counter for the obligation-and-guilt tactics ([[guilt-tripping]], [[emotional-blackmail]], [[guilt-leverage]], [[pity-plays]]), the erosion tactics ([[boundary-testing]], [[moving-goalposts]]), labor-and-fairness offloading ([[weaponized-incompetence]]), and pressure tactics generally where the counter is a clean, repeatable "no." It follows [[detection-heuristics]] (you've noticed something's off) and [[verification-rituals]] (you've checked), and supplies the language those decisions need. The core reframe — borrowed from Cloud & Townsend — is that a boundary defines *your own* behavior and response, not a demand that the other person change [6]; that is exactly what makes it un-manipulable.

## How to execute

These are templates to adapt, not scripts to recite robotically. The throughline: **state your position once, plainly, and stop explaining.**

**The foundation — don't JADE.** Do not **J**ustify, **A**rgue, **D**efend, or **E**xplain [4]. Over-explaining hands the other person material to attack and reframes your decision as a debate you can lose. State the limit; resist the pull to add reasons. **"No is a complete sentence."** (Popularized by Anne Lamott, 2005; the saying predates her — original author unknown — so it's offered as folk wisdom, not authority [5].) You have the right to **offer no reasons or excuses** and to **say no without guilt** (two of Smith's Bill of Assertive Rights) [1].

**Broken Record** [1] — for repeated pressure, pick your one-line position and repeat it, calm and unchanged, without rising to new bait:
> "I understand, and the answer is still no." → "I hear you, and I'm not able to do that." → "Like I said — no."

**Fogging** [1] — when criticism is used to move you, agree with any *grain of truth* without conceding the demand, which removes the fuel:
> "You may be right that I've been less available lately." *(then stop — no concession follows)*

**Negative Assertion** [1] — own a genuine fault flatly, without the apology-spiral a manipulator wants to mine:
> "You're right, I did forget. That was my mistake." *(no grovelling, no over-correction)*

**Negative Inquiry** [1] — when criticism is vague or manipulative, ask *for more*, which defuses it and surfaces the real demand:
> "What is it about how I handled that that bothers you?"

**DESC** [2] — to request a change cleanly (Bower & Bower): **D**escribe the behavior factually → **E**xpress your feeling ("I" statement) → **S**pecify the change → state **C**onsequences:
> "When plans get cancelled an hour before [Describe], I feel disrespected [Express]. I'd like at least a day's notice [Specify]. If that works, I'll keep planning things with you [Consequences]."

**DEAR MAN** [3] — the DBT version for a harder ask (Linehan): **D**escribe, **E**xpress, **A**ssert (ask clearly / say no clearly), **R**einforce — delivered while staying **M**indful (stay on the goal; broken-record past sidetracks), **A**ppearing confident, and willing to **N**egotiate. (DEAR is *what you say*; MAN is *how you carry it*.)

**Hold the consequence, not the other person.** A boundary you state is only real if it governs *your* action: "If the yelling continues, I'm going to end the call" — and then you do, regardless of their response [6]. You are not trying to make them stop; you are deciding what *you* will do when they don't.

## When to use it

In **normal-range relationships and lower-stakes manipulation** — a guilt-tripping relative, a boundary-testing colleague, a pushy salesperson, a friend who offloads. These scripts are designed for situations where the other party is annoying, entitled, or manipulative but **not dangerous**, and where the worst case of holding firm is friction, sulking, or an argument — not retaliation.

## When NOT to use it

**This is the most important section. Read it before using any script above.**

- **Not with an abusive or coercively-controlling partner without a safety plan first.** Asserting a boundary, autonomy, or a "no" can read to a controlling person as a *loss of control* — and loss of control is the documented trigger for the most dangerous escalation. **Separation and asserting independence are the peak lethality window in abusive relationships** (a large share of intimate-partner homicides occur at or after separation, and violence commonly spikes for months afterward) [7]. In this situation, **safety planning comes before boundary-setting** ([[dv-safety-planning]]), and a low-provocation disengagement posture ([[gray-rock]]) is often safer than confrontational scripts. **US:** National Domestic Violence Hotline 1-800-799-7233 / thehotline.org · **UK:** 0808 2000 247 · standing list in `README.md`.
- **Not as a tool to control or punish.** A "boundary" that is really a demand on someone else's behavior ("my boundary is you can't see your friends") is [[therapy-speak-weaponization]], not a boundary. The test: does it govern *your* action, or *theirs*?
- **Not where the relationship requires repair, not limits.** With a safe partner in ordinary conflict, sometimes the move is a vulnerable conversation, not a wall.

## Failure modes & risks

- **The extinction burst (expect it in safe relationships; heed it in unsafe ones).** When you stop reinforcing a behavior — stop giving in to the guilt-trip, the pressure, the tantrum — that behavior reliably gets *worse before it gets better*: more frequent, more intense, more desperate, *then* it declines [8]. **In a safe relationship, this is predictable pushback, not evidence your boundary is wrong — hold the line and it passes.** **In a potentially dangerous relationship, the same escalation is a danger signal, not a cue to "hold firmer"** — fall back to safety planning. Knowing which situation you're in (see "When NOT to use it") determines how to read the burst.
- **Over-explaining yourself back into the trap.** The most common failure is JADE-ing — adding one more reason, which reopens the debate. The script fails the moment you start justifying.
- **Boundaries without consequences you'll actually keep.** Stating "if X, I'll do Y" and then not doing Y teaches that your limits are negotiable ([[boundary-testing]] thrives on this). Only name consequences you're willing to follow through on.
- **Brittleness / rigidity.** Scripts delivered as cold recitations in a relationship that needs warmth can manufacture conflict. Assertiveness is a tool, not a personality — calibrate to whether the person is manipulating or just human.

## Safety notes

**If you may be in an abusive or coercively-controlling relationship, safety planning comes before any boundary script in this file.** Asserting limits, autonomy, or leaving can be read by a controlling partner as loss of control — the documented trigger for the most dangerous escalation, with lethality peaking at and after separation [7]. Do not treat an escalating reaction as a cue to "hold firmer"; treat it as a danger signal. Build a plan with people trained for it: **US** — National Domestic Violence Hotline 1-800-799-7233 / thehotline.org; **UK** — 0808 2000 247; full list in `README.md`; protocol at [[dv-safety-planning]]. A low-provocation [[gray-rock]] posture is often safer than confrontational scripts until you can exit safely. The assertiveness scripts above assume it is *safe to assert* — that assumption is exactly what fails in coercive control.

## Evidence

Graded `supported`, with an important scope limit. **Assertiveness training** (the tradition Smith's techniques come from) has meta-analytic and RCT support — but the evidence is largely for improving the *trainee's* outcomes (reduced social anxiety and depression, greater self-efficacy and assertive behavior), **not** for "these scripts make a determined manipulator comply." Do not overclaim efficacy against bad actors; the value is that you act according to your own judgment instead of being steered. DESC [2] is a widely-used clinical/organizational tool; DEAR MAN [3] is part of Linehan's manualized DBT (the parent treatment is well-validated; component-specific trials are thinner). JADE [4] and "no is a complete sentence" [5] are `folk` recovery-community heuristics — clinically sensible, no formal trials. Cloud & Townsend's [6] "govern your own response" principle is a popular self-help framework but echoes mainstream clinical practice. The **extinction burst** [8] is established behavioral psychology; the **DV-separation lethality** finding [7] is well-supported in the homicide/femicide literature.

## Caveats

A boundary is not a weapon and not a guarantee — it controls *your* conduct, not the outcome, and a manipulator may not respect it (which is itself information). The scripts assume you are *safe to assert*; that assumption fails precisely where the stakes are highest, which is why the safety carve-out is load-bearing rather than a footnote. And the line between a real boundary and a controlling demand dressed as one ([[therapy-speak-weaponization]]) is *whose behavior it governs* — keep yours pointed at your own actions. (Distinguished from [[gray-rock]]: boundary-setting is *active* limit-statement; gray-rock is *passive* emotional disengagement — a temporary retreat for when you can't safely assert or exit, not a boundary in itself.)

## Sources

1. Smith, M. J. (1975). *When I Say No, I Feel Guilty.* Bantam — systematic assertiveness: **Broken Record**, **Fogging**, **Negative Assertion**, **Negative Inquiry**, and the **Bill of Assertive Rights** (incl. the right to offer no reasons/excuses and to say no without guilt). Foundational assertiveness-training text.
2. Bower, S. A., & Bower, G. H. (1976). *Asserting Yourself: A Practical Guide for Positive Change.* Addison-Wesley — the **DESC** script (Describe, Express, Specify, Consequences).
3. Linehan, M. (DBT Skills Training). **DEAR MAN** (Describe, Express, Assert, Reinforce; Mindful, Appear confident, Negotiate) — interpersonal-effectiveness module. DBT is well-validated overall; component-specific evidence is thinner.
4. **JADE** (Don't Justify, Argue, Defend, Explain) — Al-Anon / 12-step recovery slogan, ubiquitous in codependency and estranged-family communities. Folk heuristic; no empirical base.
5. "**No is a complete sentence**" — popularized by Anne Lamott, *Plan B* (2005); the saying predates her and the original author is unknown (cf. M. LeBoutillier's 1995 book of the same title). Folk aphorism; do not attribute as Lamott's coinage.
6. Cloud, H., & Townsend, J. (1992). *Boundaries.* Zondervan — a boundary is a "property line": you control your own choices, feelings, and responses, not the other person; a boundary shifts the cost back to the person responsible. Popular self-help; principle echoes clinical practice.
7. Intimate-partner-homicide / separation-lethality literature (e.g., J. C. Campbell et al., Danger Assessment research; Websdale): risk of lethal violence spikes at and after separation and when an abuser perceives loss of control. Well-supported in the femicide literature. (US/general; not legal advice.)
8. Extinction burst — established behavioral psychology / ABA: when previously reinforced behavior stops being reinforced, it temporarily escalates in frequency/intensity/duration before declining; larger when prior reinforcement was consistent and the stop abrupt.
