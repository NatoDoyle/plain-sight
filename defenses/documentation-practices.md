# Documentation Practices

> Keep a dated, factual record — a log, saved messages, screenshots — of what actually happened. A contemporaneous written record is the antidote to gaslighting (you can't be talked out of what's on the page), the answer to "he said / she said," and the evidence base for a protective order, HR case, or custody fight. **Two rules govern everything: store it where the other person can't find it (discovery can escalate danger), and know your state's recording-consent law before you hit record.**

## What it counters

The most widely-applicable defense in the knowledge base — over twenty tactics route here. Chiefly: [[gaslighting]] and [[history-rewriting]] (a record fixes reality against revision), [[darvo]] and [[victim-posturing]] (it settles credibility contests), [[coercive-control]] and [[financial-abuse]] (evidence for legal/protective action), and the workplace patterns [[mobbing]], [[pip-weaponization]], and [[credit-theft-visibility]] (a paper trail for HR/legal).

## How to execute

- **Keep a dated, factual log.** For each incident: date, time, location, who was present, **verbatim quotes** where you can, and how you responded. Write it **within a day**, while memory is fresh.
- **Save the artifacts**: texts, emails, voicemails, screenshots; photograph injuries and property damage with timestamps. For financial abuse, keep statements, transaction records, and signed documents, plus a dated list of suspicious transactions.
- **Be factual, not editorial.** "He said X at 9:14pm" is more credible (and more useful) than "he was abusive." Specifics persuade; characterizations don't.
- **Preserve originals and metadata** for any legal use; note **patterns over time**, which is often what proves the case.

## Recording conversations & the law

*US, jurisdiction-labeled; not legal advice — check your state, and the other party's.* Federal law and a **majority of states (~37 + D.C.) are "one-party consent"** (you may record a conversation you're part of). But roughly **a dozen states require "all-party consent"** — **California, Florida, Pennsylvania, Illinois, and Washington are solidly among them** (the full list is genuinely contested at the edges — Connecticut, Oregon, Michigan, Nevada have nuances). An **illegal recording can be inadmissible *and* a crime** (with civil liability), and the **strictest law applies across state lines**. Before recording someone, confirm your state's rule; when in doubt, get legal advice.

## When to use it

As soon as you recognize a **pattern** — even before you've decided whether to act on it. Standard in [[gaslighting]], coercive control, workplace mobbing/PIP, custody disputes, and elder/financial abuse.

## When NOT to use it

Documentation is almost always safe to *keep* — the danger is in *how* and *where* (see Safety notes). The one thing to avoid is **recording where it's illegal** in your jurisdiction, which can backfire legally.

## Failure modes & risks

- **Discovery → escalation (the safety risk).** If an abuser finds your records, it can provoke retaliation or violence (see Safety notes).
- **Insecure or illegal capture.** Records on a monitored/shared device leak ([[stalkerware-monitoring]]); illegally-obtained recordings can be thrown out and prosecuted.
- **Rumination.** Obsessive documentation can keep you tethered to the harm and feed anxiety — it's a tool for a purpose, not a way of life.

## Evidence

Graded `supported`: documentation is standard, cross-domain advice from DV advocates (National DV Hotline; NNEDV Safety Net), employment lawyers (contemporaneous records "carry substantially more weight than memories reconstructed months later"), and elder/financial-abuse resources. The anti-gaslighting mechanism is sound — an external, dated record resists the directional reality-control gaslighting depends on. The "obsessive documentation feeds rumination" and "a record shows conduct, not intent" points are reasoned cautions, not trial findings.

## Caveats

- **Secure storage is non-negotiable** (Safety notes) — the discovery risk is the whole reason this carries a safety flag.
- **Recording law is jurisdiction-specific and this is not legal advice** — check before you record.
- **A meticulous record proves conduct and pattern, not the other person's intent** ([[epistemic-guardrails]]); it documents what happened, which is usually what matters.

## Safety notes

If you may be living with or leaving an abuser, **store documentation where they cannot find it** — discovery can escalate danger:

- Use a **separate, abuser-unknown account** (a documentation-only email), a **password-protected** file, or printed copies hidden outside the home; **never** keep it on a shared or company device.
- **Give a copy to a trusted person or a DV advocate** for safekeeping.
- For device/account safety (especially if [[stalkerware-monitoring]] is possible), use **NNEDV's Safety Net / techsafety.org** guidance — set up new accounts not linked to old ones. *(Note: NNEDV's DocuSAFE app has been discontinued; use techsafety.org's current tip sheets, not that app.)*
- **US — National DV Hotline:** 1-800-799-7233 · text START to 88788 · thehotline.org. Coordinate documentation with a safety plan ([[dv-safety-planning]], [[no-contact-exit-planning]]).
