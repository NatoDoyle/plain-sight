# DV Safety Planning

> A personalized, practical plan for staying as safe as possible in, leaving, and after an abusive relationship. It is **individualized — make it with a trained advocate**, who can run a danger/lethality assessment and tailor it to your situation. Two things to hold onto: certain factors (prior strangulation, a gun in the house, threats to kill, leaving) sharply raise lethality risk; and **the responsibility for the violence is always the abuser's, never yours.**

> **In immediate danger, call 911.** **US National DV Hotline: 1-800-799-7233** · text **START** to **88788** · chat **thehotline.org** (24/7, free, confidential; TTY 1-800-787-3224). **loveisrespect** (teens/young adults): **1-866-331-9474** · text **LOVEIS** to 22522. Crisis: **988**. **UK — Refuge:** **0808 2000 247**.

## What it counters

The canonical safety layer beneath [[coercive-control]] and the [[abuse-cycle]] — pointed to by the tactics that carry physical danger: [[financial-abuse]], [[stalkerware-monitoring]], [[reactive-abuse-provocation]], [[self-harm-threats]], and the rest of the partner-abuse cluster. It pairs with [[no-contact-exit-planning]] (the exit) and [[documentation-practices]] (the record).

## How to execute

A safety plan covers **three situations** (National DV Hotline framework), because needs differ across them:

1. **While still in the relationship** — identify the safer rooms (those with an exit; **avoid kitchens, bathrooms, garages, and rooms with weapons**), recognize escalation cues, set a **code word** with children/trusted people that means "call for help / I need to leave," and rehearse how you'd get out.
2. **Preparing to leave** — assemble a **go-bag** (IDs and key documents: birth certificates, Social Security/passport, immigration, custody, medical/financial records; money or independent funds; medications; keys; a few clothes; important numbers), kept where the abuser won't find it or with a trusted person; line up somewhere to go; quietly arrange support.
3. **After leaving** — change locks, phone number, routines, and routes; inform your workplace/school; consider a **protective order**; vary patterns an abuser could predict.

**Tech/digital safety** runs through all three: phones and accounts may be monitored ([[stalkerware-monitoring]], "Find My"/family-locator misuse), so use a **safe device the abuser hasn't had access to** and create new accounts not linked to old ones — guidance from **NNEDV's Safety Net / techsafety.org** (UK: refugetechsafety.org).

## The Danger Assessment (lethality factors)

Jacquelyn Campbell's **Danger Assessment** is a validated tool that weighs the factors most associated with intimate-partner homicide. Advocates use it to gauge risk; the high-weight factors are worth knowing [1][2]:

- **Prior non-fatal strangulation/choking** — a major predictor (one study: ~6.7× the odds of attempted and ~7.5× the odds of completed homicide) [2].
- **Abuser's access to a firearm**, and prior **threats with a weapon**.
- **Explicit threats to kill**; **escalating frequency or severity** of violence.
- **Separation / leaving**, especially from a highly controlling partner.
- **Forced sex**, **extreme jealousy and control**, the abuser's **threats of or attempts at suicide**, and — importantly — **the victim's own belief that the abuser could kill them** (intuition is a documented signal).

The presence of these factors is a reason to involve an advocate and treat planning as urgent — not a reason to panic, and not a checklist you score alone.

## When to use it

Anyone experiencing or preparing to leave [[coercive-control]] or partner abuse — and preventively, when red flags are escalating. It applies regardless of gender, orientation, or marital status.

## Caveats

- **Make it with a trained advocate.** A generic plan is a starting point; a personalized one (with a danger assessment) is the real tool. Advocates are free and confidential (Safety notes).
- **Store the plan and go-bag where the abuser can't find them**, and avoid leaving a digital trail (searches/links may be monitored).
- **Leaving is the highest-risk window** — the plan must cover the exit *and* after, not just "get out" ([[no-contact-exit-planning]]).
- **Not legal advice**; protective-order law is jurisdiction-specific.
- A safety plan **reduces but cannot eliminate** risk; it is harm-reduction, and **the abuser is responsible for the abuse** ([[epistemic-guardrails]]).
- **Don't cite the folk numbers**: the popular "75–77% of DV homicides occur at separation" figure lacks a solid source (Campbell's data show ~44% of victims separated) — the *direction* is real, the percentage isn't a constant.

## Safety notes

This whole file is safety guidance; the resources are at the top. The essentials: **call 911 in immediate danger**; reach a **trained advocate** (National DV Hotline **1-800-799-7233** / text START to 88788 / thehotline.org) for a free, confidential, personalized plan; handle digital steps from a **safe device**; and remember the lethality factors above are a reason to get help fast. You are not overreacting, and it is not your fault.
