# Helping Others

> When someone you love is being manipulated, scammed, abused, or recruited, the instinct is to tell them: "leave him," "it's a scam," "they're using you." It almost always **backfires** — pushing triggers reactance and entrenches them. What works is the opposite: **stay connected, don't attack the manipulator, ask questions instead of arguing, and keep the door open** so they have somewhere to land. And the hard safety rule: **never confront the abuser — it can escalate the danger to the person you're trying to help.**

## What it counters

The isolation that [[coercive-control]], [[cult-conversion-funnel]], and [[romance-scam-arc]] depend on — by keeping a trusted outside relationship alive (the [[betrayal-blindness|outside perspective]] manipulation works to remove). It supports the target toward [[dv-safety-planning]], [[cult-exit-support]], or [[no-contact-exit-planning]] on *their* timeline.

## How to execute

- **Don't lead with "leave / it's a scam / they're using you."** Direct confrontation provokes **psychological reactance** (Brehm) — a threatened person defends their choice and digs in. The boomerang is the default outcome of telling.
- **Don't attack the manipulator or group.** It triggers defense of them, and (with cults especially) *confirms the group's "outsiders will attack us" script*, pushing the person further in. Criticize the *behavior's effect* on them, gently, not the person they're attached to.
- **Ask, don't tell** (a motivational-interviewing stance — Miller & Rollnick): resist the "righting reflex" to fix and argue; ask open questions, reflect what you hear, and let *them* voice their own doubts (people believe what they hear themselves say). Support their autonomy.
- **Stay connected and keep the door open.** Be the relationship that's still there when they're ready — repeated, patient availability beats one big confrontation.
- **Offer specifics, not ultimatums**: "I'm here, whatever you decide"; "I noticed X and it worried me"; a place to land; help to [[documentation-practices|document]]; resources/hotlines.

## When to use it

Whenever someone in your life shows signs of being manipulated or abused and you want to help without driving them away — friends, family, colleagues.

## When NOT to use it

- **Don't confront, threaten, or tip off the abuser** (Safety notes) — it endangers the target.
- **Don't issue ultimatums** ("me or them") — they usually fail and can sever the very lifeline the person needs.
- **Don't take over.** Deciding *for* them is *rescuing*, not helping (see below).

## Failure modes & risks

- **The rescuer trap** (Karpman's drama triangle): jumping in as Rescuer disempowers the person, recreates a controlling dynamic, flips you into "persecutor" when you push, and burns you out. (A useful clinical *heuristic*, not hard theory.)
- **Reactance backfire**: the more you argue, the more committed they become.
- **Helper danger / burnout**: confronting an abuser can endanger *you* too; and over-functioning as a savior is unsustainable — mind your own limits.

## Evidence

Graded `supported`. **Psychological reactance** (Brehm, 1966) is an established, well-replicated mechanism behind backfiring persuasion (the "boomerang effect") [1]. **Motivational interviewing** (Miller & Rollnick) — resist the righting reflex, support autonomy, evoke the person's own change-talk — has a strong RCT base [2]. The **don't-attack / stay-connected** guidance is the consensus of DV-advocate and cult-exit (Hassan) practice [3]. The **Karpman Drama Triangle** is a widely-used *clinical heuristic* with only limited empirical operationalization — present it as a lens, not established theory. The separation-danger basis for the safety rule is Campbell's femicide research [4].

## Caveats

- **You can't force someone to leave or "see it"** — pushing triggers reactance; respect their autonomy and timeline.
- **They usually know their abuser's danger better than you** — follow their lead on safety and timing; leaving is the highest-risk window.
- **Helping ≠ rescuing** (*rescuing*): the goal is *their* restored agency, not your win or your takeover.
- **Some situations need professionals** (DV advocates, therapists, exit-counselors), not friends alone ([[epistemic-guardrails]]).

## Safety notes

**Never confront, threaten, or warn the abuser** — it can escalate danger for the person you're helping (and for you). Instead:

- **Support the person and connect them to experts**: National DV Hotline **1-800-799-7233** / text START to 88788 / thehotline.org; for a friend exiting a group, [[cult-exit-support|ICSA]]. **988** for crisis. **UK — Refuge** 0808 2000 247.
- **Don't pressure a faster exit than is safe** — leaving is the highest-risk moment and must be planned with an advocate, on their timeline ([[dv-safety-planning]], [[no-contact-exit-planning]]).
- **Assume your messages to them may be seen** (shared accounts, [[stalkerware-monitoring|monitored devices]]) — keep written contact neutral and let an advocate advise on safe channels.
- If you believe someone is in immediate danger, call **911**.

## Sources

1. Brehm, J. W. (1966). *A Theory of Psychological Reactance* — threatened freedom → reactance and the "boomerang effect"; coercive persuasion entrenches. Reviews: Rosenberg & Siegel (2018); Reynolds-Tylus (2019). Established.
2. Miller, W. R., & Rollnick, S. *Motivational Interviewing* — "resist the righting reflex," support autonomy, evoke change talk (RULE). Strong RCT base (SAMHSA TIP 35). Established.
3. Don't-attack / stay-connected helping guidance: DV-advocate sources (One Love; National DV Hotline) and cult-exit practice (Hassan, *Freedom of Mind* — keep communication channels open; attacking the group backfires). Practitioner consensus.
4. Karpman, S. (1968), the Drama Triangle (Rescuer/Victim/Persecutor) — clinical heuristic, limited empirical operationalization (Lac & Donaldson, 2020). The safety basis (separation danger): Campbell et al. (2003), *Am. J. Public Health* 93(7). Clinical / established (the safety point).
