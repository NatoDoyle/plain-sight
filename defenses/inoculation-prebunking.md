# Inoculation & Prebunking

> Forewarning is forearming. Expose someone to a *weakened* dose of a manipulation technique plus a refutation — *before* they meet the real thing — and they build resistance, like a vaccine builds antibodies. Learning *how the trick works* in advance is the most generalizable protection there is (this whole knowledge base is, in effect, technique inoculation). Honest limit: the effect is real but **modest, it fades without boosters, and it must build discernment — not a blanket cynicism that distrusts everything.**

## What it counters

The preventive layer in front of recognition — especially against information-environment manipulation: [[propaganda-devices]], [[disinformation-playbooks]], and the recruitment funnels of [[cult-conversion-funnel]] and [[radicalization-pipeline]]. It generalizes best against *techniques* (emotional manipulation, false dilemmas, scapegoating) rather than specific claims, and feeds [[detection-heuristics]].

## How to execute

- **Prebunk, don't just debunk.** Warn and refute *before* exposure — prevention scales and avoids the backfire/entrenchment that after-the-fact correction can cause. Once a falsehood lands, it's far harder to dislodge.
- **Two ingredients** (McGuire): a **forewarning** ("people will try to manipulate you with X") that raises your guard, plus a **refutation** that pre-arms you with the counter ("here's the trick, and here's why it's misleading").
- **Inoculate against the *technique*, not just the claim.** Learning the manipulation *moves* — manufactured urgency, false dichotomy, scapegoating, emotional loaded-language, fake experts — transfers to novel content; memorizing one debunked claim doesn't.
- **Make it active**: games and exercises where you *spot* or even *simulate* the technique (e.g., the "Bad News" game) beat passive reading; teaching the techniques to others reinforces them.

## When to use it

Proactively and at population scale — media-literacy and scam-awareness *before* people are targeted; warning a community about a circulating scam type; and as the design principle behind any defense agent or curriculum (teach the techniques, not just the cases). Most effective on people **not yet deeply committed** to the belief being protected.

## When NOT to use it

It's not a tool to *deploy on* someone mid-manipulation (that's recognition + disengagement). And it isn't a substitute for the harder defenses where there's a live threat — a person being coercively controlled needs [[dv-safety-planning]], not a prebunking video.

## Failure modes & risks

- **Decay.** Effects fade within days to weeks without **booster** re-exposure — a one-off inoculation isn't durable.
- **Cynicism instead of discernment (the important one).** Some interventions raise skepticism of *everything* — true information included — rather than improving the ability to tell true from false. Blanket distrust is itself the goal of "firehose" disinformation, so prebunking must sharpen discrimination, not just lower trust across the board.
- **Limited reach with the already-committed**, and modest effect sizes overall — it's a layer, not a cure.

## Evidence

Graded `supported`, **not established** — strong theory and replication, with genuinely open questions on durability and real-world effect. **Inoculation theory** is well-founded (McGuire, 1961/1964). Modern **prebunking** has substantial support: van der Linden & Roozenbeek's "Bad News" game (2019) and **scalable short-video inoculations** tested in seven preregistered studies including a **YouTube field experiment (n≈22,632)** improved recognition of manipulation techniques (Roozenbeek et al., 2022, *Science Advances*); a meta-analysis (Banas & Rains, 2010) confirms inoculation reliably beats controls. **The honest caveats:** effects are small-to-moderate and **decay** without boosters (Maertens et al., 2025); and the **real-world translation is contested** — reanalyses argue some game effects reflect a **conservative response bias (blanket skepticism), not improved discrimination** (Modirrousta-Galian & Higham, 2023), and a same-design study found **no discrimination gain** in an Indian sample (Seabrooke et al., 2024/25), while a health-media-literacy intervention raised skepticism of *both* accurate and inaccurate news (2024). Counter-evidence exists (Guess et al., 2020, found literacy tips improved discernment). Net: a promising, theory-grounded layer whose magnitude and durability are still being worked out.

## Caveats

- **Build discernment, not distrust.** The success criterion is telling true from false *better* — not trusting everything less. An intervention that just makes people cynical has done disinformation's work for it ([[epistemic-guardrails]]).
- **Pair with boosters / repetition** — treat it as ongoing, not one-and-done.
- **It's preventive, not curative** — it doesn't replace recognition, verification, or (for live threats) safety planning.
