# Manipulation Audit

> A structured way to answer "am I being manipulated, or is this just normal friction?" — run as a deliberate checklist rather than left to a gut you can't fully trust. The catch it's built around: **manipulation corrupts the very self-assessment you'd use to detect it** (that's what gaslighting is for), so the load-bearing step is **getting an outside perspective**. It assesses the *situation and its effect on you*, never a diagnosis of the other person.

## What it counters

The deliberate, escalated pass that [[detection-heuristics]] hands off to: it's aimed at chronic, hard-to-see patterns — [[gaslighting]], [[coercive-control]], and anything where [[betrayal-blindness]] keeps you from naming what's happening. Outputs route to [[verification-rituals]], [[boundary-scripts]], or (for abuse) safety planning.

## How to execute

Run these questions on the **pattern over time**, not a single bad day. Write the answers down — memory is exactly what gets eroded.

1. **Felt-sense inventory.** How do I *consistently* feel during and after contact — confused, drained, guilty, anxious, walking on eggshells, "crazy"? Persistent post-interaction fog is a signal.
2. **The "no" test (the strongest single marker).** Can I say no, disagree, or set a boundary *without* being punished, escalated against, or worn down? Honest influence accepts "no"; manipulation doesn't ([[manipulation-vs-influence]], criterion 5).
3. **Symmetry / cui bono.** Is influence mutual, or one-directional? Who reliably benefits, and at whose cost?
4. **Autonomy test.** Over time, am I *freer and more myself* — or more constrained, more isolated, smaller (Stark's shrinking "space for action")?
5. **Apply the five criteria** of [[manipulation-vs-influence]] to the *behavior*: covertness · exploiting fear/guilt/attachment vs. appealing to reasons · benefit asymmetry · targeting your vulnerabilities · response to "no." Score the conduct, not the soul.
6. **Innocent-explanation / base-rate check.** What are the non-manipulation explanations (stress, mismatch, ordinary selfishness), and how likely are they? ([[epistemic-guardrails]] — guard against both over- and under-reading.)
7. **Outside perspective (do not skip).** What would a trusted, *uninvolved* person say? Say it out loud to someone not entangled in it.
8. **Document and review.** Revisit your notes in a month — patterns you'd otherwise rationalize away become visible on the page.

A useful benchmark: the Duluth **Power & Control Wheel** (coercion, intimidation, isolation, minimizing/denying/blaming, economic abuse…) versus its mirror **Equality Wheel** (negotiation, respect, trust, honesty, shared responsibility); loveisrespect frames the same continuum as **healthy → unhealthy → abusive** [1].

## When to use it

When the felt-sense keeps flagging something you can't pin down; when deciding whether to stay, leave, escalate, or let it go; periodically in a relationship/job that leaves you doubting yourself.

## When NOT to use it

As a substitute for **professional or crisis help** in suspected abuse (see Safety notes), or as a weapon ("I audited you and you're a manipulator"). It is for *your* clarity and decisions, not to litigate someone's character.

## Failure modes & risks

- **Defeated by gaslighting.** If the manipulator's voice is already in your head, your internal answers are compromised — which is exactly why step 7 (outside perspective) is non-negotiable, not optional [2].
- **Over-detection.** Run as a witch-hunt, the audit "finds" manipulation everywhere; the base-rate and innocent-explanation steps exist to prevent that.
- **Self-blame spiral.** The audit assesses the *situation*, not your worth; finding you've been manipulated is not a verdict on you ([[why-smart-people-fall]]).

## Evidence

Graded `supported` as a **synthesis** of recognized tools — there is **no single validated "manipulation audit" instrument** (stated plainly, like [[detection-heuristics]]). It draws on practitioner-standard frameworks (Duluth wheels; the loveisrespect spectrum) and Stark's coercive-control model [1][3], and on clinician-administered IPV **screens** (HITS, WAST, Composite Abuse Scale) — but those are *screening* tools (and even there, "no single tool has well-established psychometrics," Rabin et al., 2009; USPSTF recommends clinicians screen and refer), not self-diagnosis [3]. Self-report has real limits (recall bias, gaslighting-corrupted self-assessment), which is why the outside-perspective and professional steps carry the weight.

## Caveats

- **Avoid both errors** ([[epistemic-guardrails]]): over-detection (paranoia, labeling) is as harmful as under-detection (minimizing real abuse). Require conjunction + persistence + the base-rate check.
- **Not a diagnosis of the other person** — assess the situation and its effect on you; describe behavior, never pin a disorder on someone.
- **The symmetry trap**: apply the five criteria to *both* parties, including yourself — "you're manipulating me" is itself a classic manipulator's move ([[manipulation-vs-influence]]).
- **Distinguish [[normal-relationship-conflict]]**: ordinary friction with repair, respect for "no," and preserved autonomy is not manipulation.

## Safety notes

If the audit points toward abuse or coercive control, this is a moment to bring in support, not just more self-reflection — and **safety planning comes before boundary-setting or leaving**, because risk often *peaks at separation* ([[dv-safety-planning]], [[no-contact-exit-planning]]):

- **US — National DV Hotline:** 1-800-799-7233 · text START to 88788 · thehotline.org. **loveisrespect** (teens/young adults): 1-866-331-9474.
- **UK:** Refuge / National DV Helpline 0808 2000 247.
- A DV advocate or trauma-informed therapist can help you read the situation and plan safely.
