# No-Contact & Exit Planning

> Cutting off contact and leaving is often the right answer — and **the most dangerous moment**. With a controlling or violent partner, the period around separation is when risk of serious or lethal harm spikes, so the order matters: **plan first, with help, before you go.** "No contact" both removes the abuser's access (safety) and breaks the trauma bond (recovery) — but the transition is the hazard, and it must be planned, not announced.

## What it counters

The pull that keeps people tethered to an abuser: [[trauma-bonding]] and [[intermittent-reinforcement]] (the bond no-contact is designed to break), [[hoovering]] (the "I've changed / one more chance" re-engagement), and the manufactured exit costs of [[escalation-entrapment]]. It is the exit half of [[coercive-control]] and [[abuse-cycle]] recovery, paired with [[dv-safety-planning]].

## How to execute

**Plan the exit before you make it** (with a DV advocate where abuse is involved — see Safety notes):

- **Gather quietly, in advance**: IDs and key documents (birth certificates, Social Security/passport, immigration, custody, financial/legal papers), independent **money/access to funds**, **medications**, keys, and your [[documentation-practices|records]] — into a **go-bag** kept where the abuser won't find it, ideally with a trusted person. *(In an emergency, skip this — getting out safely beats gathering things.)*
- **Line up somewhere safe to go** and how you'll get there.
- **Digital safety**: separate accounts and passwords and check for location-sharing / [[stalkerware-monitoring]] — but do this **from a safe device** (a friend's phone, a library computer), because removing monitoring can tip off and escalate the abuser. Lock your phone.
- **Legal**: consider a protective/restraining order (WomensLaw.org for state-specific help).

**The no-contact rule itself**: once out, **block on every channel, hold no "closure" meeting, and don't answer hoovering.** Removing the option removes the temptation — and, with a dangerous ex, removes their access to you.

## When to use it

For **abusive, coercively controlling, or genuinely toxic** relationships where contact is harmful and change isn't happening. The full version (total cutoff) when it's safe and possible; the **lowest-contact** version when it isn't (below).

## When NOT to use it

- **Not as a first resort for ordinary conflict** (*normal relationship conflict*): healthy relationships are repaired with boundaries and dialogue, not cut off at the first difficulty. No-contact is a serious step for serious situations.
- **When full no-contact is impossible** (co-parenting, shared finances): use **parallel parenting**, [[gray-rock]], and **business-only, documented communication** (the BIFF approach — Brief, Informative, Friendly, Firm; app-based, timestamped tools) — aim for *lowest-contact*, not zero.

## Failure modes & risks

- **Announcing it.** Telling a dangerous abuser you're leaving — or giving them "one more conversation" — is the classic escalation trigger. Plan silently.
- **The separation spike.** Risk of serious violence is highest around leaving (see Evidence) — an unplanned exit can be more dangerous than a planned delay.
- **Hoovering pulls you back.** The post-no-contact "I've changed" wave is predictable; breaking it once is the hardest and most important part.

## Evidence

Graded `supported`. The **separation-violence spike is well-documented**: Campbell et al.'s multisite femicide study found **estrangement — especially leaving a highly controlling partner — is an independent risk factor for intimate-partner homicide** (about 44% of femicide victims were separated; the UK Femicide Census likewise finds a large share killed during/after leaving, many within the first months) [1]. Treat the *direction* as established and **avoid any single precise figure** — the widely-circulated "75–77% of DV homicides happen at separation" stats are advocacy numbers without a settled source. The DV-advocate **exit-planning and digital-safety practices** are practitioner-standard [2]. The breakup-recovery "no-contact rule" (to break a [[trauma-bonding|trauma bond]] and resist hoovering) is clinical/popular-psychology guidance, not experimentally validated — sound, but grade it as practice, not proof.

## Caveats

- **Safety planning precedes the exit** — this is the whole point of the file (Safety notes).
- **Work with a DV advocate**, who can run a danger assessment and personalize the plan; don't improvise a high-risk exit alone.
- **No-contact is a tool for harmful relationships**, not a way to avoid every hard conversation ([[epistemic-guardrails]]).
- **The abuse is the abuser's responsibility, never yours**, and a plan reduces risk without eliminating it — it is harm-reduction, not a guarantee.

## Safety notes

**Leaving is the highest-risk window.** If a partner is controlling or has been violent, plan the exit *before* acting, with help:

- **US — National DV Hotline:** 1-800-799-7233 · text START to 88788 · thehotline.org (free, confidential, 24/7) — advocates do **personalized safety/exit planning** and danger assessment.
- **loveisrespect** (teens/young adults): 1-866-331-9474 · text LOVEIS to 22522. **988** for crisis. **UK — Refuge:** 0808 2000 247. **Immediate danger: 911.**
- Don't announce your departure; store your plan/go-bag where the abuser can't find it; handle digital steps from a safe device ([[dv-safety-planning]]).
