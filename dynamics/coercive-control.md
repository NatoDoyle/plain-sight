# Coercive Control

> The architecture of domination: not a series of "incidents" but an ongoing condition of unfreedom, built from isolation, surveillance, micro-regulation of daily life, degradation, and credible threat. Its harm is to your **liberty and personhood**, not only your body — and that is why it can devastate even when there is little or no physical violence.

## Definition

Coercive control (Evan Stark, 2007) is **a sustained pattern of domination through which one person strips another's autonomy and entraps them — via isolation, surveillance, micro-regulation, deprivation, degradation, and credible threats**, rather than through discrete violent episodes [1]. Stark's paradigm shift: the dominant "violent incident model" wrongly equates abuse with assaults gauged by injury, and misses the *ongoing condition* — abuse that is "neither primarily domestic nor necessarily violent," more akin to **kidnapping, hostage-taking, or indentured servitude** than to a fight [1]. He names it a **"liberty crime"**: the injury is to autonomy, dignity, and "space for action." This reframing is now embedded in law in several jurisdictions (see Legal status). The defensive importance is twofold: it lets a target name domination that leaves no bruises, and it correctly locates the harm in the *pattern*, so the victim stops asking "was that incident bad enough to count?" — because the answer is the cage, not any single bar.

## The pattern

Coercive control is an *architecture*, not a sequence — though it usually develops gradually, often opening with charm or [[love-bombing]] before the controls tighten. Stark and the Duluth "Power and Control Wheel" document its recurring components:

- **Isolation** — severing the target from friends, family, work, money, and information, so the controller becomes the sole frame of reference ([[isolation-tactics]]).
- **Surveillance / monitoring** — tracking movements, phones, messages, spending; "beeper games" in Stark's era, [[stalkerware-monitoring]] now.
- **Micro-regulation** — rules governing everyday life: what to wear, eat, say, where to go, who to see, how to keep house.
- **Degradation** — humiliation, name-calling, and rituals that erode self-worth (often via [[gaslighting]] and shaming).
- **Deprivation & economic control** — restricting money, food, sleep, transport, medication; sabotaging work ([[financial-abuse]]).
- **Intimidation & credible threats** — the menace that enforces the rest, often deniable ([[veiled-threats]], [[emotional-blackmail]]).

The hub of the Duluth wheel is **power and control**; physical and sexual violence form the rim that backs the other spokes with force [1].

## Composed of

Coercive control is built from many KB tactics. Its structural pillars not catalogued elsewhere are declared here as constituents: [[financial-abuse]] (economic deprivation/sabotage) and [[stalkerware-monitoring]] (the surveillance apparatus). Its other core components connect through their own trajectories — already declared in those files as `escalates-to`/`enables` edges toward coercive control: [[isolation-tactics]], [[gaslighting]], [[veiled-threats]], [[emotional-blackmail]], and [[silent-treatment]]. It `exploits` [[fear-exploitation]] — the credible threat is the backbone that makes the micro-rules self-enforcing, so that eventually the controller need not act, only imply.

## Escalation markers & timeline

It typically *tightens* over time: rules multiply, the permitted world shrinks, monitoring intensifies, and the target's sense of a separate self erodes. Markers: needing permission for ordinary choices; tracking your every movement; finances controlled or sabotaged; friends and family fallen away; walking on eggshells around an ever-changing rulebook; losing the sense of what *you* think or want. **The most dangerous escalation is at challenge or exit:** coercive control is among the strongest predictors of intimate-partner homicide, and lethal risk spikes precisely when the controller perceives loss of control — i.e., when the target asserts autonomy or tries to leave [4] (see Safety notes).

## Exit windows

Leaving coercive control is both the goal and the peak-danger moment, so timing and planning are everything. Because the controller has usually engineered isolation and economic dependence, exit generally requires *rebuilding external resources first* — a trusted contact, independent money, documents, a destination — almost always with a domestic-violence advocate ([[dv-safety-planning]]). Documentation matters here in a way it doesn't for one-off harms: because coercive control is a *pattern*, contemporaneous records (incidents, rules, messages, finances) are what make it visible to police, courts, and advocates where it is criminalized ([[documentation-practices]]). Do not announce the plan to the controller.

## Counter-strategies

- **Name the pattern, not just the incidents** ([[detection-heuristics]]): the defining recognition is the *cage* — the sum of rules, isolation, and monitoring — not whether any single act "counts." Naming it as coercive control is what reframes "maybe I'm overreacting" into "this is a recognized pattern of abuse."
- **Document the pattern** ([[documentation-practices]]): keep a private, safely-stored record — dates, rules, threats, financial control. It counters the deniability the controller relies on and is often legally decisive where coercive control is an offence.
- **Rebuild external resources covertly** ([[isolation-tactics]] counter): re-establish a trusted outside contact and independent access to money/ID before any move — the isolation is load-bearing for the control, and breaking it (carefully) is the first structural step.
- **Plan exit with specialists** ([[dv-safety-planning]]): because separation is the lethality peak, exit should be planned with a DV advocate — never a spontaneous confrontation. **Do not confront the controller** with "I know what you're doing"; that forfeits safety for a moment of clarity.

## Legal status

*Informational, not legal advice; jurisdiction- and date-specific (verify current law).* The coercive-control framing has been written into law:
- **England & Wales** — Serious Crime Act 2015, **s.76** ("controlling or coercive behaviour in an intimate or family relationship"; in force 29 Dec 2015; "serious effect" = causing fear of violence on ≥2 occasions, or serious alarm/distress with substantial adverse effect on day-to-day activities; max 5 years). Broadened by the Domestic Abuse Act 2021 (s.68; "personally connected" extended; in force 5 Apr 2023, not retrospective).
- **Scotland** — Domestic Abuse (Scotland) Act 2018 (in force 1 Apr 2019; a single "course of behaviour" offence folding physical/sexual/psychological abuse and coercive control together; gender-neutral; often called a model statute).
- **Ireland** — Domestic Violence Act 2018, s.39 (commenced 1 Jan 2019).
- **Australia** — e.g. New South Wales, s.54D Crimes Act 1900 (commenced 1 July 2024; current/former intimate partners); other states evolving.
- **United States** — a patchwork, mostly *civil/family* law (e.g., California Family Code §6320, 2020; Connecticut "Jennifer's Law," 2021); **Hawaii** is the notable criminal-standalone exception.

## Caveats & false positives

- **Not ordinary relationship conflict** ([[normal-relationship-conflict]]): couples disagree, negotiate, and sometimes control *specific* things (a shared budget, a parenting rule) by mutual agreement. Coercive control is distinguished by the **global pattern of domination, fear, and lost autonomy** — one person's will systematically overriding the other's across life domains — not by the presence of any rule, request, or argument.
- **Not "situational couple violence"** ([[situational-couple-violence]]): Johnson's typology distinguishes coercive-controlling violence ("intimate terrorism") from situational couple violence — conflict that escalates in the moment *without* an underlying campaign of control. SCV is the more common type in community samples and is more gender-symmetric; it can still be serious, but it is **not** coercive control, and conflating the two over-detects (and muddies the statistics). The marker is the *control campaign*, not the violence count ([[epistemic-guardrails]]'s intimate-terrorism vs SCV note).
- **Anyone can be a victim or perpetrator.** Stark theorized coercive control as gendered (men's control of women, rooted in structural inequality), and in practice it is heavily gender-asymmetric — but the *behavioral pattern* occurs in same-sex, female-perpetrated, and family (elder, parental) configurations too. The KB describes the pattern; it does not gate who can be harmed by it.
- **One controlling act is not a regime.** A single jealous demand or a possessive moment is rung-1 evidence, not coercive control; the diagnosis is the *sustained, multi-domain pattern* that produces entrapment ([[epistemic-guardrails]]).

## Safety notes

Coercive control is `severity: critical`: it is among the strongest single predictors of intimate-partner homicide, and **lethal risk peaks when the controller senses loss of control — challenge, autonomy, or separation** [4]. The future agent (and any reader) must treat suspected coercive control as a situation to **escalate, not just analyze**: route to specialist help, do not advise confrontation, and support a *planned* exit. **US:** National Domestic Violence Hotline 1-800-799-7233 / text START to 88788 / thehotline.org · **UK:** Refuge 0808 2000 247 · **Ireland:** Women's Aid 1800 341 900 · **Australia:** 1800RESPECT (1800 737 732) · immediate danger: local emergency number. Protocol at [[dv-safety-planning]].

## Evidence & debates

Graded **`supported`**: Stark's framework is widely adopted across DV research, policy, and now statute in multiple jurisdictions [1] — more than clinical or folk. It is not `established` in a hard-science sense: it is a sociological/criminological framework with live debates about *measurement* (how to operationalize and prove a "pattern") and *gender symmetry* (Stark's gendered etiology vs. evidence that the behavioral pattern crosses configurations). Michael Johnson's typology [2] is the key disambiguating contribution (intimate terrorism vs situational couple violence), and the Duluth model [3] supplies the practitioner taxonomy. The honest synthesis: a robust, law-shaping reframing of abuse as a liberty crime, whose main open questions are measurement and the scope of its gendered theory — not whether the pattern is real.
