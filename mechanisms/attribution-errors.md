# Attribution Errors

> Every act needs a cause, and minds assign causes cheaply: their behavior is who they *are*, yours is what you were *up against*, the sufferer must have done something, and the offer is worthless because your opponent made it. This family is mostly **observer-side** — it decides what everyone around you concludes about you. Which is why the smear, the reversal, and "why didn't you just leave?" all run downstream of one lever: whoever controls what the audience can see of the situation controls the character the audience infers.

## The lever

Four clusters, one question: *why did that happen?*

**Reading other people.** The **fundamental attribution error** (Ross's 1977 coinage: underestimating situational and overestimating dispositional causes of behavior) and its narrower descendant **correspondence bias** — Gilbert & Malone's definition, worth quoting because the precision is the whole point: "the tendency to draw inferences about a person's unique and enduring dispositions from behaviors that can be entirely explained by the situations in which they occur" [2]. Jones & Harris supplied the paradigm case: readers attributed pro-Castro attitudes to essay writers *even when told the position had been assigned* [1]. The mechanism is a staircase — dispositional inference is fast and near-automatic, situational correction is effortful and collapses under cognitive load [2] — which means a tired, rushed, or emotionally loaded audience is a maximally dispositional audience. The lab effect is one of the sturdiest in the discipline: Many Labs 2 replicated a correspondence-bias paradigm at **d = 1.82** (95% CI 1.76–1.87), among the largest in a 28-effect battery where only 15 replicated at p < .05, with "little heterogeneity" between WEIRD and less-WEIRD samples [3]. The popular cultural gloss is wrong and worth killing: Krull and colleagues found "significant correspondence bias effects... in both cultures. Moreover, no cultural difference emerged" [4]. What does vary is *responsiveness to situational salience* — Koreans and Americans both showed the bias in the no-choice condition, but Koreans' bias shrank when constraints were made salient and Americans' did not [4]; and spontaneous explanation style diverges developmentally, growing more dispositional with age in US samples and more contextual in Hindu Indian samples [4]. The construct also has live critics: Sabini and colleagues argue the person/situation dichotomy is incoherent as usually drawn (fear of embarrassment is both a "situation" and a disposition-like force), and that what the classics really demonstrate is how badly people underestimate face-saving motives [5]; Gawronski's title says the rest — "the fundamental attribution error is dead, long live the correspondence bias" [5]. Honest split: **the inference pattern is established; the sweeping "people are dispositionists" reading is contested.**

**Trait ascription bias** (evidence: supported — thin, and this file says so) is the self/other version: I am variable and situation-responsive, you are a fixed type. The flagship is a single N = 56 study, and its actual result complicates the slogan — participants judged their friends' behavior as *more consistent* than their own while ascribing *higher trait intensity* to themselves [9]; the related Jones–Nisbett trait-ascription studies and Sande's "multifaceted self" work (people claim both poles of a trait dimension for themselves, one pole for others) are the rest of the base [9]. No meta-analysis exists, the parent asymmetry collapsed (below), and the label is largely a bias-codex construction. Keep claims minimal.

Two members scale the same inference to groups. The **group attribution error**: observers read a group's *decision* as each member's personal attitude, ignoring the procedure that produced it — Allison & Messick showed participants a policy outcome reached by a leader, a 90% vote, or a bare majority, and they associated the individual views with the group outcome in all three cases [10]; the follow-up found people infer attitude *change* when only the decision rule changed [10] (evidence: supported — clean paradigm, modest literature, no meta-analysis and no adversarial replication history; the widely repeated moderator list — that the error runs strongest for groups seen as dissimilar, monolithic, and adversarial — traces to a citation that does not support it, and is not carried here, see Evidence base). The **ultimate attribution error** (evidence: supported — partial): Pettigrew's group-level extension, where prejudiced perceivers attribute outgroup negatives dispositionally and explain outgroup positives away as "the exceptional case," luck or special advantage, unusual effort, or situational demand [11]. Hewstone's review of 19 studies is the honest verdict — "the evidence provides only limited support for Pettigrew's (1979) 'ultimate attribution error'." What reliably holds is an ingroup-serving asymmetry: more internal attribution for ingroup than outgroup positive acts, outgroup failure charged to lack of ability while outgroup success is explained away, and a general preference for ingroup-serving accounts of group differences [11]. Hewstone prefers **intergroup attribution bias** as the label, and Pettigrew's specific prediction — that the pattern tracks individual prejudice level — did not hold [11].

**Protecting the self.** The **self-serving bias** is the family's strongest member and one of the largest meta-analytic effects in social psychology: across 266 studies and 503 independent effect sizes, people made more internal, stable, and global attributions for positive than negative events at **d = 0.96**, "present in nearly all samples" [7]. Its moderators are load-bearing for this KB. Culture scales it without abolishing it — Asian samples d = 0.30, Western 0.70, US 1.05 [7]. Psychopathology attenuates it (0.48 vs 1.28), and the **depression figure is d = 0.21 — attenuated, not reversed** [7]; the "depressed people run it backwards" claim comes from the older selected-sample depressive-attributional-style literature and should not be stated as the meta-analytic finding. Children and older adults show the largest biases [7]. And self-threat *magnifies* it [7] — meaning the bias peaks exactly during accountability conversations, on both sides. [[projection-blame-shifting]] carries the tactic-grade deployment; this is its engine.

The **actor-observer bias** is this file's humility exhibit, and it should be told straight. The textbook claim — actors cite situations, observers cite dispositions — was described for thirty years as robust and pervasive. Malle's meta-analysis of 173 published studies found "average effect sizes from d = −0.016 to d = 0.095": essentially nothing [6]. The asymmetry survived only under narrow conditions (highly idiosyncratic actors, hypothetical events, intimate actor–observer pairs, free-response coding) and, decisively, only by valence: "the asymmetry held for negative events, but a reverse asymmetry held for positive events. This valence effect may indicate a self-serving pattern in attribution, but across valence, no actor-observer asymmetry exists" [6]. In other words the survivor is the self-serving bias in a different costume. Malle's replacement account keeps a real actor–observer difference but relocates it: actors and observers differ in *which kind* of explanation they give (reasons vs causal histories of reasons), not in a person/situation tug-of-war [6]. Do not write "people naturally excuse themselves and blame others' character" as a law. Write the negative-event version — which is, conveniently, the conflict case.

The **defensive attribution hypothesis** (evidence: supported) covers responsibility judgments distorted by the observer's need to feel safe: better a blameworthy agent than random chance, unless the agent could be me. Walster's severity effect started it and replicated inconsistently thereafter; Shaver added the relevance/similarity moderator; Burger's meta-analysis of 22 studies found "a statistically significant but weak tendency to attribute more responsibility" for severe than mild accidents — while the *interaction* was the real finding: observers personally and situationally similar to the actor attributed *less* responsibility as severity rose, dissimilar observers more [8]. A later meta-analysis confirms the severity direction is consistent across methods but small and dependent on which judgment is asked for [8]. **The robust core is the similarity moderator** — severity raises blame mainly when observers feel unlike the person it happened to. That is precisely the joint victim-blaming machinery grips.

**Defending the world-model.** The **just-world hypothesis** / **just-world belief** (evidence: established for the derogation phenomenon): people need the world to be one where people get what they deserve, so unresolvable innocent suffering gets resolved cognitively instead. Lerner & Simmons' observers, watching a peer apparently receive shocks, derogated her character — but the boundary conditions are the useful part: derogation appeared when observers could neither stop nor compensate the suffering and expected it to continue, announced compensation eliminated it, and the "martyr" suffering for the observers' own benefit was derogated most [12]. Hafer & Bègue's review confirms the threat-reduction framework and criticizes the post-1980 literature as unsystematic — carry that qualifier [12]. The measurement work supplies the defensive payload: belief in a just world splits into two dimensions, and only one is dangerous. **BJW-self** ("the world is fair *to me*") "most strongly and consistently predicted decreases in depression and stress, and increases in life satisfaction" — a coping resource. **BJW-others** ("the world is fair in general") is the dimension that carries harsh social attitudes and victim derogation [13].

**System justification** (phenomena supported; mechanism contested) is the institutional-scale version: processes "contributing to the preservation of existing social arrangements even at the expense of personal and group interest," offered to explain "the participation by disadvantaged individuals and groups in negative stereotypes of themselves," smoothed by complementary stereotypes like "poor but happy" and "rich but miserable" [15]. The dispute is about *why*, and the critics state it cleanly: "The debate... does not pivot on whether system justification occurs — we all agree that system justification does occur. The issue is why it occurs?" [15c]. The social-identity alternative (SIMSA) re-derives the same findings from passive acceptance of social reality, ingroup bias at the superordinate level, and hope for future ingroup advancement — no dedicated system-justification motive required — and the signature status-legitimacy prediction has repeatedly failed in large cross-national tests [15]. Defensively the mechanism war is moot: every account predicts that people, including the harmed, can be recruited into defending the arrangement harming them.

**Moral luck** (evidence: supported — qualified, and mostly observer-side) is philosophy's name for blame that tracks outcomes the agent did not control: the drunk driver who kills and the one who gets home chose identically [14]. Empirically the structure is precise. Cushman's two-process finding: "judgments of the wrongness or permissibility of action... rely principally on the mental states of an agent, while judgments of blame and punishment are found to rely jointly on mental states and the causal connection of an agent to a harmful consequence" [14]. But the blame asymmetry is largely a between-subjects artifact — Kneer & Machery found that within-subjects, with room to compare cases, "the vast majority of people judge a lucky and an unlucky agent as equally blameworthy," that between-subjects outcome effects are "mediated by negligence ascriptions" and reduce to different probability judgments, and that "punishment judgments are significantly more outcome-dependent than wrongness, blame, and permissibility judgments" [14]. The manipulation-relevant residue: **punishment prices by outcome, and bad outcomes retro-license inferences of recklessness** — not a general law that people blame by luck.

**Devaluing the adversary.** **Reactive devaluation** (evidence: supported — thin): a proposal's perceived value drops because of who offered it. The canonical demonstration had US respondents rate an identical nuclear arms-reduction proposal favorably when attributed to Reagan or to unspecified policy analysts and far less favorably when attributed to Gorbachev — roughly 90% / 80% / 44% rated it favorable to the US or even-handed, as reported in Ross & Stillinger 1991 [16]. The fuller experimental writeup (Stillinger, Epelbaum, Keltner & Ross) remained an unpublished Stanford manuscript, so the figures stand on the published report and no independent verification of the design exists. The archival replication is stronger: an actual Israeli-authored peace plan was rated less favorably by Israeli Jews when attributed to Palestinians than to their own government, with devaluation heavier among hawks [16]. A companion finding names the sibling effect — proposals were valued more highly as hypotheticals than once actually offered [16]. Candidate mechanisms (zero-sum inference, construal change, concession devaluation) remain underdetermined, there is no meta-analysis, and most of the work comes from one research lineage.

## Evidence base

The file grade reflects the family's core (correspondence bias, self-serving bias, just-world derogation); members carry their own grades above. This cluster has an unusually wide evidence spread and must not be presented as uniformly established.

- **Survived cleanly:** correspondence bias (Many Labs 2, d = 1.82, minimal WEIRD/non-WEIRD heterogeneity) [3]; self-serving bias (266 studies, 503 effects, d = 0.96, with a coherent moderator structure) [7]; just-world victim derogation (60 years, replicated boundary conditions, a *Psychological Bulletin* review) [12]; the defensive-attribution similarity moderator (meta-analytic) [8].
- **Collapsed under meta-analysis:** the classic **actor-observer asymmetry** — 173 studies, overall d ≈ 0, surviving only as a valence effect that Malle himself reads as self-serving attribution [6]; and **moral luck for blame** — the asymmetry largely vanishes within-subjects and is mediated by negligence/probability inference; only punishment is robustly outcome-dependent [14].
- **Renamed and downgraded:** the **ultimate attribution error** — "only limited support"; the ingroup-serving pattern holds, the full four-part error rarely materializes, the prejudice-level prediction failed, and "intergroup attribution bias" is the accurate label [11].
- **Mechanism disputes that don't change the exploit:** system justification (SJT vs SIMSA — both camps agree the phenomenon occurs) [15]; the FAE construct critiques (Sabini's incoherent dichotomy; Gawronski's theory-based correction), which reframe *why* the inference happens without disturbing that it does [5].
- **Thin, and load-bearing nowhere:** trait ascription bias (one N = 56 study plus adjacent work; no meta-analysis) [9]; group attribution error (clean demonstrations, modest literature, never adversarially tested) [10]; reactive devaluation (one lineage; the flagship study unpublished) [16].
- **Popular retellings this file contradicts:** "East Asians don't show the FAE" (false in standard paradigms — no cultural difference emerged) [4]; "depression reverses the self-serving bias" (it attenuates to d = 0.21) [7]; "severity raises blame" as an unconditional law (weak; the similarity interaction is the finding) [8]; "actors blame situations, observers blame character" (d ≈ 0) [6]; and the group attribution error's standard moderator list — "strongest for outgroups seen as dissimilar, monolithic, and adversarial" — which is routinely attributed to Worth, Allison & Messick 1987, a paper whose verified title and locator concern a different question (how a group decision reshapes perception of one's own and others' attitudes) and whose abstract is not retrievable from any indexer; the claim is plausible and may well be true, but this file cuts it rather than carrying an unsourced moderator [10].
- **Sampling caveat:** the correspondence-bias replication is unusually culture-robust [3][4], but just-world, defensive attribution, system justification, and reactive devaluation rest on overwhelmingly Western samples, with reactive devaluation's best field data drawn from two specific conflicts [12][15][16].

## How it's weaponized

The recurring exploit: **the operator controls what the audience can see of the situation, and the audience's own inference machinery converts the fragment into your character — or into your desert.**

- **Character assassination by context-stripping** (correspondence bias) — the move is to supply a *behavior sample* with the provocation off-camera: the target crying, snapping, drinking, or shouting after months of pressure, exhibited as who they are. [[reactive-abuse-provocation]] is the engineered version (provoke, film, present the reaction as disposition); [[pip-weaponization]] is the institutional one (a single incident or an engineered-to-fail process filed as trait proof). The receiving side is measured: exposure to DARVO reduced perceived victim credibility and increased victim blame, and a second experiment (N = 360) found brief education about the pattern mitigates the effect [17] ([[darvo]], [[smear-campaigns]], [[victim-posturing]], [[mobbing]]). The tell is grammatical: character vocabulary displacing event vocabulary — "she *is* unstable" instead of "what happened was."
- **The asymmetric ledger** (self-serving bias, plus actor-observer's negative-event residue) — their harms always have circumstances, yours always have character, sustained over years and immune to counter-example. Measured in offenders: 1,267 male and 159 female convicted IPV offenders minimized, denied, and attributed more blame for the offense to their partner than to themselves [18]. And since self-threat amplifies the bias [7], the ledger tilts hardest at exactly the moment accountability is raised. Tactic home: [[projection-blame-shifting]].
- **Victim-blaming machinery** (just-world + defensive attribution) — "why didn't you leave?", "you should have known better," "she knew what she was doing." Fraud victims are "constructed as greedy and gullible and there is an overwhelming sense of blame and responsibility levelled at them," with ridicule isolating victims and suppressing disclosure [19]; sympathy is gated on resembling an "ideal victim" [19]. The operator's contribution is **engineered dissimilarity**: foreground the victim's choices, greed, or naivety so the audience can think *I'd never have clicked that link* — because blame lands hardest from observers who feel unlike the person it happened to [8], and rises with the severity of what happened [8]. The downstream cost is structural: roughly 11% of surveyed adults report losing money to fraud while only about 4% report it [19], so the machinery reproduces the conditions for the next victim.
- **Scapegoating at group scale** (group + intergroup attribution) — propaganda supplies a diet of outgroup negative acts framed as essence and pre-packages Pettigrew's discount moves for any counter-evidence ("he's one of the good ones," "they only got it through connections") [11]; the explanation cannot lose ([[propaganda-devices]], [[disinformation-playbooks]], [[radicalization-pipeline]]). The family-scale isomorph is [[golden-child-scapegoat]]: the scapegoat's successes are luck, their failures are character.
- **Manufactured consensus** (group attribution error) — audiences read a visible group *output* — a committee decision, an HR outcome, a review average, a trending hashtag — as the sincere personal verdict of every apparent member, without auditing the procedure that produced it [10]. The regulatory anchor: the FTC's rule on consumer reviews and testimonials, 16 CFR Part 465 (effective Oct 2024), bans fake and AI-generated reviews and fake social-media indicators (US jurisdiction) [19] ([[fake-reviews-astroturfing]]). Against an individual it is an isolation amplifier: "everyone at work thinks you're the problem," concluded from an outcome one or two people drove ([[isolation-tactics]], [[mobbing]]). Scoping honesty — the pure "everyone believes what I believe" illusion is the *false consensus effect*, which lives in [[mind-reading-illusions]]; what this file owns is the group-output-to-member-attitude inference.
- **"The system is fair, so your complaint is false"** (system justification) — institutions facing internal accusations recruit members' status-quo defense: complainants become disloyal, oversensitive, or self-interested, because crediting the complaint would indict the legitimizing story [20]. The tell is timing — legitimacy talk surges precisely when evidence of harm surfaces, offered *in place of* engagement with the evidence. Complementary stereotypes get sold inside exploitative arrangements ("scrappy and free," "we're a family here") so the exploited defend the arrangement [15] ([[we-are-family-rhetoric]], [[mlm-lifecycle]], [[pip-weaponization]]). The high-control-group version runs the same engine privately: events staged to look providential, successes proving the doctrine, failures proving your insufficient faith [20] ([[spiritual-abuse]], [[thought-terminating-cliches]], [[cult-conversion-funnel]]).
- **Outcome pricing, both directions** (moral luck) — reckless conduct that happened not to injure is exhibited as proof of good character ("nothing happened, you're overreacting" — the deniability [[veiled-threats]] is built for, the arithmetic behind [[non-apology-patterns]] minimization); and conversely, a target's ordinary decision that turned out badly gets relabeled as vice — the documented consumer version is investment decisions judged less moral and re-described as "speculation" or "gambling" rather than "investment" purely on how they turned out, with participants switching both the moral verdict and the label once told the outcome was bad [14].
- **Mediation sabotage and source-laundering** (reactive devaluation) — a workable settlement can be killed simply by arriving labeled as the adversary's: "that schedule is your ex's lawyer talking," "this was your mother's idea," and terms you would have signed stop being readable on their merits ([[triangulation]], [[bad-faith-negotiation]]). Whoever benefits from the conflict continuing benefits from that label sticking. The mirror version arrives as a demand relayed by someone you trust ([[proxy-recruitment]]) — so unexplained source-switching, in either direction, is itself a tell.
- **"I know you better than you know yourself"** (trait ascription — thin evidence, carried here as a recognition pattern, not an evidenced mechanism) — interpretive authority as role assignment: the target is a predictable type while the operator reserves complexity and context for themselves. Predictable objects don't get consulted; complex subjects do ([[therapy-speak-weaponization]], [[gaslighting]]).
- **Flattering-attribution sales** — the con that hands you the attribution you already want: this win was your skill, so reinvest; your doubts are other people's jealousy. The sharpest version targets the bias blind spot ([[optimism-overconfidence]]) — people see bias readily in others and not themselves [20] — so "you're far too sharp to be scammed" is a scam marker, not reassurance ([[flattery-ego]], [[hope-greed]]).

## Ethical use vs exploitation

Character judgment is not a malfunction: choosing friends, hiring, and warning someone about a documented pattern all require inferring dispositions, and this KB is itself a machine for pattern-based inference about behavior. Situational explanation is equally legitimate — mitigation, context, and mercy are moral achievements, not excuses. The line ([[manipulation-vs-influence]]): exploitation **controls the situational record** (stages the provocation, circulates context-stripped samples, narrates from the only vantage point), **engineers dissimilarity** so blame will land on the harmed party, **fuses legitimacy with the specific claim** (answering "did this happen?" with "our process is fair"), **prices by outcome rather than conduct**, or **poisons a proposal by attributing it to the adversary**. The disclosure test: would the account survive if the audience saw the entire situation, knew who assembled the sample, and knew what the assembler gains from the inference?

## Recognition

- **Being summarized.** "That's just who he is." "You *are* like this." "I saw it with my own eyes" — one decontextualized clip treated as a census of your character. The narrator saw one situation, once, and the situation was doing most of the work.
- **The ledger that never symmetrizes.** "I snapped because I'm exhausted; you snapped because you're hostile." "I earned this, you got lucky." Their context always counts and yours never does — over months, immune to counter-example. Any single instance is normal; the one-way flow is the marker.
- **The audit lands on you.** "Why didn't you just leave?" "What did you do to set him off?" "People don't get fired for nothing." "I'd never have fallen for that." Blame arriving fastest for the *worst* outcomes, and sympathy conditional on your having been a perfect victim.
- **"Everyone agrees."** "HR sided with him — the whole department thinks you're a problem." "The family decided." You never learn the decision rule, who actually spoke, or how many abstained.
- **The group that can't win.** Negative acts prove essence, positive acts get discounted — "one of the good ones," "they only got it because of quotas." As the target: your successes are asterisked, your mistakes are essential.
- **Legitimacy offered instead of an answer.** "That's just how things work here." "If it were really unfair someone would have done something." "We take this very seriously; our process is fair." The strange relief of deciding the unfixable thing is actually fine.
- **Blame priced by the dice.** "Nothing happened, so you're overreacting." "You could have killed someone!" "How could you not have known?" — the same conduct, repriced by how the outcome happened to land.
- **The offer that soured overnight.** "If they're offering it, there's a catch." The terms you'd have accepted yesterday feel suspicious today and the only thing that changed is whose name is on them.
- **Felt-sense indicators:** being flattened — their self-account has chapters and exceptions, their account of you has one adjective; the second injury of reporting harm and finding the audience auditing *you*; compulsive private review of your own conduct ("what did I do to deserve this") after abuse or a scam; the sudden aloneness when one institutional outcome arrives feeling like a unanimous personal verdict from everybody in the building.

## Resistance

1. **Situation-first questioning.** Before crediting any character claim: "what was happening right before?" — then ask who supplied the situational information and what they gain from the inference. Refuse to grade character from a single sample, especially one that is secondhand, emotionally loaded, or staged by the narrator ([[documentation-practices]] preserves context precisely because the exploit is context deletion).
2. **The two-column test** ([[manipulation-audit]]). Write each disputed incident twice — once as each party would explain it. Someone who cannot produce even a hypothetical situational account of *your* behavior while always producing one for their own is running the asymmetric ledger; that pattern, on paper, is checkable in a way that memory is not.
3. **Audit your own ledger against the base rate.** At d = 0.96 your account is flattering you too, and the bias grows under threat [7] — the symmetric obligation in [[epistemic-guardrails]]. The inverse also matters: a scrupulously evenhanded, self-blaming account is not automatically honesty; flattened attribution co-travels with depression [7] and with months of absorbed blame-shifting.
4. **Ask for the decision rule.** "Who decided, by what procedure, and who actually spoke?" Majority votes hide minorities, leader decisions hide everyone, negotiated compromises reflect nobody, and unanimity is the only rule that licenses the member-attitude inference [10]. For online consensus, check provenance before crediting it ([[verification-rituals]], [[detection-heuristics]]).
5. **Judge conduct ex ante.** "What did they know and choose *before* the dice landed?" Cushman's split makes this operational: ask the *wrongness* question, which tracks intent, rather than the punishment question, which tracks outcome [14]. Apply it to yourself first — targets torture themselves over outcomes where the choice was ordinary and only the luck was bad.
6. **Blind-evaluate offers.** Write down your assessment of the terms, then learn who wrote them; ask "would I take this exact deal if a mediator had proposed it?" Partisan identification amplifies devaluation, so "how adversarial am I feeling right now?" is a live calibration question [16]. Mediators' single-text procedures exist for exactly this reason — that is the defensive use of the effect, not a trick.
7. **Give the audience something to do.** The cleanest lever in this file is Lerner & Simmons' compensation condition: derogation stopped when observers could compensate the victim [12]. Disclosure that names a concrete way to help protects the discloser better than exhibiting suffering alone ([[helping-others]]). And naming the reversal pattern in advance demonstrably reduces the credibility damage it does [17] ([[inoculation-prebunking]]).
8. **Separate the two questions.** "Is this institution broadly legitimate?" and "did this specific harm occur?" are different questions, and fusing them is the exploit [15]. Say so out loud when a legitimacy answer is offered to a factual one.

## Caveats

- **Dispositional attributions are frequently correct — and this KB depends on that.** Single acts correlate weakly with traits, but *aggregates* across occasions become highly reliable [21]; personality judgments by acquainted others achieve real accuracy, and informant reports predict behavior about as well as self-reports [21]. A pattern observed repeatedly, across different situations, over time, against the person's own incentives, IS trait evidence — that is not the fundamental attribution error, it is the behavior-pattern standard the whole knowledge base runs on. The bias is **single-instance dispositionism**, plus dispositional inference from constrained or engineered situations. "My friends all independently see the same thing in him" is data. This caveat is expressly bounded by the next one.
- **Cross-neurotype misattribution: consistency arising from neurotype is not disposition.** The bullet above licenses reading a cross-situational, over-time pattern as character — which is exactly the shape autistic and ADHD behavior takes, because a neurotype is stable by definition. Flat or mismatched affect read as coldness, executive-function failure read as not caring, delayed or literal processing read as evasion, blunt phrasing read as hostility, a meltdown or shutdown read as aggression or stonewalling: each is consistent across situations and over time, and none of it is a character verdict. Milton's **double empathy problem** relocates the failure in the *mismatch between* neurotypes rather than in one party's deficit [22] — which means the confident neurotypical read of an autistic person is itself the less reliable instrument, not the corrective. Before converting any consistent pattern into a verdict about who someone is, ask whether *neurodivergent-communication* already explains it, and check the thing neurotype does **not** explain: whether the harm runs one direction and reliably benefits the same party. That test cuts both ways — an audience can be recruited into a dispositional read of a neurodivergent target ("everyone can see what he's like"), and a real diagnosis can be offered to pre-excuse a pattern of harm that tracks benefit rather than neurology.
- **The Hanlon's-razor seam** (*incompetence-not-malice*). This file's caution cuts both ways. Over-attributing harm to design when disorganization, poor skill, or ordinary self-interest explains it is the mirror error — and "they didn't mean it" and "they're a monster" are *both* dispositional claims. The tiebreaker is not vibe or apology quality but the shape of the pattern: genuine incompetence is symmetric and costs the incompetent person too, while the harm here runs one direction and reliably benefits the same party.
- **"You're victim-blaming" and "you're making excuses for them" are symmetric weapons.** Each is sometimes an accurate correction and sometimes a way to end an inquiry. Asking what happened is not blaming; naming a real, controllable causal contribution in a prevention context is not derogation — but that licence stops at the door of anyone still in the situation, where the only controllable contribution that matters is the operator's, and "what could you have done differently" delivered to someone under active control is part of the harm rather than an aid to it ([[coercive-control]]). The bias signature is *character* derogation, blame exceeding causal contribution, and judgments calibrated to observer comfort rather than facts — not the mere act of examining conduct.
- **Self-serving bias is culturally scaled, and its absence is not health.** Asian d = 0.30 vs US d = 1.05 means present everywhere, sized differently [7] — so both "he never takes responsibility" and "she blames herself for everything" need cultural and clinical context before becoming evidence about a person. Depression attenuates the bias to d = 0.21 [7]; an unusually self-blaming account may be a harm signature rather than ground truth about fault.
- **Believing the world is fair is not the problem; believing it about other people is.** BJW-self predicts lower depression and higher life satisfaction and functions as a coping resource; BJW-others is the dimension carrying harsh attitudes and victim derogation [13]. Don't strip a survivor's belief that things can be fair for them — just notice when a just-world reflex is billing them for someone else's conduct. And "everything happens for a reason" said by a grieving person about their own loss is meaning-making; *direction* is the marker.
- **Accepting an arrangement can be accurate.** Some hierarchies are legitimate, some constraints are real, and strategic patience is not false consciousness — treating any acceptance of inequality as capture is exactly the concept-creep [[epistemic-guardrails]] prohibits, and it is half of what the SIMSA critique formalizes [15].
- **Discounting an adversary's offer is often rational.** Adversaries do have opposed interests, and an offer does carry information about the offerer. The bias signature is the controlled-experiment one: identical content valued differently purely by attributed source [16].
- **Several members here are thin, and thin members carry no load.** Trait ascription rests on one small study, the group attribution error has never been adversarially tested, and reactive devaluation's flagship demonstration is unpublished [9][10][16]. Weak entries are vocabulary for noticing something, never grounds for a conclusion about a real person.
- **Safety routing.** "Why didn't you leave?" is this file's most-quoted phrase, and it is usually asked of someone who may still be in danger. Understanding the observer bias behind the question does nothing about the danger: where control, threats, stalking, or violence are present, this analysis hands off to [[coercive-control]], [[dv-safety-planning]], and the crisis resources in `README.md`. This file carries no safety flag; those do.
- **Seams.** [[confirmation-bias]] owns favoring belief-consistent evidence; [[pattern-illusions]] owns seeing structure in randomness; this file owns the *causes assigned* to behavior and outcomes. [[mind-reading-illusions]] owns false consensus, naïve realism, and the transparency illusions — what you think others think; [[self-evaluation-illusions]] owns illusory superiority and the comparative self-assessment family. The **bias blind spot** — seeing bias readily in others and not in yourself, cited here only where it is the sales hook — is homed in [[optimism-overconfidence]] and further discussed in [[why-smart-people-fall]]; this file borrows it rather than owning it. Hindsight and memory-side outcome bias have their own codex home. [[bias-codex-index]] maps the remainder.
