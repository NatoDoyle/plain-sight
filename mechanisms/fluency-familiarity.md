# Fluency & Familiarity

> Your mind reads *how easily something is processing* — how smooth, how familiar, how quickly it comes together — and hands back a verdict about the world: that's true, I like that, I already knew that, that's worth more. The ease is real; the verdict is a misattribution. And you never experience the ease, only the verdict. That is why this lever cannot be felt being pulled.

## The lever

**Ease read as evidence.** **Processing fluency** is the subjective experience of how easily a mental operation runs, and Alter & Oppenheimer's review is the unification: manipulations as different as semantic priming (*conceptual* fluency), visual clarity (*perceptual* fluency) and phonological simplicity (*linguistic* fluency) produce "remarkably similar judgmental consequences" — chiefly that the fluent item is judged **more true** [1]. The mechanism is misattribution: the ease belongs to the *presentation*, and gets charged to the *content*. Schwarz's wrinkle keeps this from being a law of nature — what a given ease *means* depends on the naive theory the person brings to it, which is why the same fluency can read as truth, liking, familiarity, or expertise depending on the question asked [1]. The defensively load-bearing property, and everything below follows from it: **the misattribution is not introspectively available.** Nobody experiences "that processed easily, therefore it's true." They experience "that's true." There is no felt difference between a conclusion reached and a conclusion re-encountered. The umbrella is a theoretical unification, not a finding — grade it `supported`, and note that at least one tribe refuses to sit still (see mere exposure, below) [12].

**Believing: the illusory truth effect.** The single most manipulation-relevant member, reproduced in the lab for nearly fifty years, though its corpus is thin on *external* replication and preregistration (see Evidence base) [5]. Hasher, Goldstein & Toppino had students rate sixty plausible statements across three sessions two weeks apart; confidence in the *repeated* false statements climbed 4.2 → 4.6 → 4.7 on a seven-point scale while confidence in non-repeated statements stayed flat [4]. Three modern results give the effect its defensive shape. (1) **Knowledge does not protect.** Fazio et al. showed repetition moving people who *demonstrably knew the correct answer* — participants who knew the pleated skirt worn by Scots is a kilt still drifted toward the repeated "a sari is the name of the short plaid skirt worn by Scots." Their term is **knowledge neglect**: the failure to *consult* stored knowledge in the face of a fluent experience [6]. Knowing better is not the same as checking, and repetition attacks the checking. (2) **Implausibility is not a firewall.** Pennycook, Cannon & Rand found repetition raising perceived accuracy of fake-news headlines after a single exposure, persisting a week, surviving fact-check tags and ideological mismatch — but reported extreme implausibility ("the earth is a perfect square") as a boundary; two of the same authors then largely dissolved that boundary, arguing it is a psychometric artifact of the rating scale rather than a psychological block, and a preregistered test found repetition raising truth ratings even for blatantly implausible statements [7]. Read the fences honestly: those judgments all stayed *below the scale midpoint*, and about half of participants showed no effect or a reversed one [7]. Repetition does not flip absurd into believed; it drifts things within range. (3) **Dose-response is logarithmic** — across studies running up to 9 and 27 repetitions, "the largest increase in perceived truth came from encountering a statement for the second time," with shrinking returns after; rumour work replicates the log shape, finds it *mediated by processing fluency*, and finds skepticism moderating only slightly [8]. Nobody needs a firehose. A colleague who plants the same insinuation twice has banked most of the available effect. Magnitude discipline: the current meta — 182 studies, 366 effect sizes, N = 31,184 — puts the effect at **g = 0.37 after correcting for small-study bias**, with substantial heterogeneity and moderators including item type, first-exposure instructions, first-exposure presentation duration, and the **presence of veracity cues** [5]. Small per exposure, serious at industrial volume, and not mind control. One correction cuts the other way: lab procedures usually *warn* participants that some statements are false, and removing that warning **more than doubles** the effect — published sizes systematically understate the unwarned world you actually live in [9]. Downstream, repetition also lowers the bar for *transmission*: seeing a fake headline once or four times made people judge publishing and sharing it less unethical **even when it was clearly labeled false and they disbelieved it**, and they shared repeated headlines more; repetition-driven sharing is mediated by perceived accuracy [10].

**Liking: the mere exposure effect.** Zajonc's claim was that "mere repeated exposure of the individual to a stimulus is a sufficient condition for the enhancement of his attitude toward it" — no argument, no reward, no requirement that you recall the exposure [11]. (His opening anecdote, the Oregon State student who attended a speech class inside a black bag for two months while the room moved "from hostility toward the Black Bag to curiosity and finally to friendship," is illustration, not data.) Bornstein's 1968–1987 meta established the phenomenon across visual, auditory, olfactory and gustatory material and across subliminal and clearly-perceptible exposures; the frequently-quoted overall correlation could not be verified against the primary this sweep and is therefore not printed here [11]. The modern anchor is Montoya et al. — 268 curve estimates from 81 articles — and it complicates the folk version in two ways that matter. The curve is an **inverted U** (positive slope, negative quadratic): more exposure buys more liking up to a point and then costs it, which is why real campaigns rotate creative instead of looping one asset. And the pattern was conditional — visual but not auditory stimuli, exposure durations under 10 s or over a minute, ratings taken after all stimuli. The authors' own verdict: "existing models for the mere exposure effect do not adequately account for the findings" [12]. So: phenomenon `established`, explanation open. One contested sub-claim deserves care because it is repeated everywhere: Bornstein & D'Agostino found *larger* effects for 5-ms unrecognized exposures than 500-ms recognized ones, and proposed discounting (when you can attribute the warmth to the exposure, you subtract it); a later direct test of that model found the opposite — the mere-exposure effect appeared only where recognition was *highest*, and liking and recognition were positively correlated [13]. The safe claim is narrow: exposure need not be remembered to work. It is not "subliminal advertising works." What *is* well demonstrated is that familiarity **outlives its source**: nonfamous names read once were judged famous 24 hours later but not immediately, and Jacoby et al. are explicit that recollecting the source is "a separate act" that does not happen spontaneously [14].

**Form: rhyme, and the disfluency mirror that broke.** The **rhyme as reason effect** is the cleanest demonstration that surface form buys judged truth: extant rhyming aphorisms ("What sobriety conceals, alcohol reveals") were rated more accurate than semantically equivalent non-rhyming twins ("...alcohol unmasks") — and, crucially, the advantage was **attenuated when people were cautioned to separate poetic qualities from semantic content** [15]. That attenuation is the empirical warrant for this file's best counter and for the "de-fluency the familiar" move already prescribed in [[thought-terminating-cliches]]. Grade it `supported`, not `established`: a single well-known 2000 paper, no registered replication located, an unexcluded rival account (rhyming aphorisms may feel true because they *sound like accumulated folk wisdom*), and domain-specific nulls reported in advertising research [15]. Now the mirror image, and the file's honesty exhibit. If fluency inflates belief, deliberate **processing difficulty** (the **processing difficulty effect**, or **disfluency effect**) should improve thinking — a genuinely interesting idea that produced two famous findings and then failed, by two separate routes. Strand one: hard-to-read fonts were reported to activate analytic reasoning and improve performance on counterintuitive math problems; pooling the original with **16 replication attempts** found "no effect on solution rates" and "no evidence of a disfluent font benefit under any conditions," and an independent multi-study follow-up returned decisive evidence against, ruling out numeracy as the missing moderator [16]. Strand two: disfluent fonts were reported to improve classroom retention; a direct replication was null, the purpose-built "memory font" Sans Forgetica failed repeatedly (including a study where the *generation* effect appeared robustly in the same experiments while the font effect was absent — the labs could detect a real effect when one was there), and in one vulnerable population disfluency actively **harmed** sight-word learning [17]. That strand is genuinely unsettled rather than closed: a null meta-analysis of text-based learning was itself shown to contain coding errors leaving its recall results in doubt, and blur-based disfluency still produces memory benefits in recent work [17]. Two lessons the KB carries out of this: never offer "make it harder to read" as a defense, and note the direction of the residual risk — disfluent presentation has been shown to *increase* false recall, not attentiveness [17].

**Self-made: generation, IKEA, and the thin one.** The **generation effect**: words produced by the participant are remembered better than the same words merely read — across cued and uncued recognition, free and cued recall, **and confidence ratings** [18]. Self-produced material is not just recalled better; it is held harder. Meta-analysis puts it at **.40 across 86 studies and 445 effect sizes**, "a benefit of almost half a standard deviation," with substantial moderator variability; the modern meta (126 articles, 310 experiments) identifies **generation constraint** — how tightly the task pins down what you can produce — as a significant moderator [18]. That moderator is the exploitation story in one word: a heavily constrained generation task still produces the effect *while leaving the generator feeling they arrived under their own power*. Fences: it needs words you already know, it shrinks or vanishes when learning conditions are compared between subjects, it is abolished by encoding/retrieval mismatch (transfer-appropriate processing, owned by [[memory-retrieval-quirks]]), and it buys memory for the generated item **at a cost to the surrounding context** — self-generated conclusions are remembered better while the circumstances that produced them are remembered worse. That is precisely the profile that makes self-persuasion hard to audit afterwards. The **IKEA effect** — self-assembly raises valuation of the resulting object — survived meta-analysis (k = 55, N = 5,454, d = 0.57, robust to customization and tangibility; publication-bias correction not confirmed in what the sweep retrieved) [19]. Its sharpest feature is the original boundary: "labor leads to love **only when labor results in successful completion**" — the effect dissipated when participants failed to finish or destroyed their creations [19]. The mechanism is contested (a conceptual replication says psychological ownership; a developmental series finds the bias emerging at age 5, *not* moderated by amount of effort invested, and concludes existing theories are inadequate), and one clean extension null exists [19]. **Not invented here** is the weakest member by a wide margin, and belongs here only by family resemblance — the inverse of generation and IKEA: if self-made things are overvalued, externally-supplied ones are undervalued. The founding study is a correlational field study of 50 R&D groups in which performance rose to ~1.5 years of team tenure and declined noticeably by five, tracking falling communication with outside sources — insularity in long-tenured teams, not a demonstration that people reject ideas *because* they are external [20]. Its own field says so: three decades later a review still described the antecedents and consequences as not clearly understood, and later authors split the label into six sub-syndromes [20]. Graded `folk` as a cognitive bias (the management evidence is `supported` — a 565-project survey finds prevalence, negative effects, and perspective-taking as a countermeasure — but it is attitude survey work in firms, not an individual-level paradigm) [20].

## Evidence base

- **Illusory truth — `established`** (the frontmatter grade reflects the established members — illusory truth, mere exposure, generation — not the fluency umbrella, which this file grades `supported`). 1977 original [4]; meta-analysed across 182 studies at g = 0.37 corrected [5]; survives demonstrated prior knowledge [6], implausibility [7], conspiracy content and cognitive reflection [7], partisanship [7], and monetary incentives for accuracy (preprint) [10]; replicates in naturalistic text-message and fake-news paradigms [7]. Honest dents: the corpus is WEIRD, convenience-sampled, trivia-heavy, short-delay, non-standardized, and thin on external replication and preregistration [5]; the older meta reports the between-items effect as larger than within-items, implying two paradigms tapping partly different processes, and no pooled *d* from it is printed here because it could not be verified [5].
- **Mere exposure — `established` as a phenomenon, `contested` as an explanation.** Zajonc 1968 [11]; Bornstein's meta of the 1968–1987 literature [11]; 268 curve estimates confirming an inverted U with real moderator restrictions, and the authors' own statement that existing models (fluency misattribution included) do not adequately account for the findings [12]. The recognition/discounting sub-claim is `contested` — the classic finding and a later direct test point opposite ways [13].
- **Processing fluency (umbrella) — `supported`.** Robust member effects; the unification is a proposal, and fluency is a famously elastic post-hoc explanation. Behavioral measures also lack the resolution to separate fluency from familiarity, so "this felt easy, therefore fluency moved me" is not directly observable even in the lab — treat this file's felt-sense markers as prompts for suspicion, never as diagnostics [1][12].
- **Rhyme as reason — `supported`.** One well-known study with a replicable-sounding attenuation condition, no registered replication located, an unexcluded folk-wisdom rival account, and reported nulls in advertising [15].
- **Processing difficulty / disfluency — `contested`, trending refuted for the font manipulation.** Reasoning strand: 17 pooled samples (the original plus 16 replications), no effect, no rescuing moderator [16]. Learning strand: direct replication null, Sans Forgetica serially failed, harm in one vulnerable population — but the null meta-analysis has a published error critique and blur-based effects are still reported, so "refuted" overstates it [17]. Also note disfluency's documented downside: more false recall, not more care [17].
- **Generation effect — `established` for memory** (.40, 86 studies; modern preregistered work finds it robust in the same experiments where disfluency does nothing) [18][17]. The *persuasion* extension — that self-generated **conclusions** are believed harder, not merely recalled better — is an inference this file labels as such; the weight for it sits with [[commitment-consistency]] and [[dissonance-exploitation]], not with the memory literature [18].
- **IKEA effect — `supported`.** Meta-analytic d = 0.57 across 55 studies, plus conceptual and developmental replications; but the mechanism is unsettled, the completion boundary was not reproduced in one conceptual replication, one extension is a clean null, bias-correction is unconfirmed, and the effect comes from a consumer-psychology genre whose showpieces replicate poorly as a class [19].
- **Not invented here — `folk`** as a cognitive bias; `supported` as an organizational pattern. No experimental individual-level paradigm, no meta-analysis, a correlational founding study, and construct proliferation [20]. Manipulation relevance is low and this file says so rather than manufacturing one.
- **Applied bridges, labeled.** Grooming-as-familiarity and MLM/crypto onboarding-as-IKEA-effect are **mechanism-level extrapolations with no direct study** [21][19]. The "do your own research" funnel has one hard finding behind it, and its mechanism is *data voids*, not fluency [22].

## How it's weaponized

The recurring exploit: **manufactured ease presented as accumulated evidence** — one interested source, repeated or polished until it feels like many sources, or like something you worked out yourself.

- **Repetition at industrial volume.** The claim is not argued, it is *scheduled*, across surfaces engineered to look independent — the firehose model in [[disinformation-playbooks]], the device catalog in [[propaganda-devices]], the astroturfed chorus in [[fake-reviews-astroturfing]] that buys familiarity and apparent consensus in one purchase ([[social-proof]] owns the consensus half). Repetition volume and source diversity are different quantities; manipulation collapses them.
- **Slogan and cliché design.** Short, concrete, rhythmic, repeated verbatim, immune to paraphrase — fluency in weapon form. [[thought-terminating-cliches]] owns the tactic and its counters; this file is the *why* underneath it [1][4][15].
- **Normalization by airing.** [[overton-window-shifting]] routes repetition-as-acceptability, and its fence is correct and inherited here rather than re-argued: mere exposure is strongest for **neutral** material and weak-to-reversible for already-disliked material, so the heavy lifting in normalizing genuinely objectionable content is done by norm erosion and apparent consensus, not by exposure [12]. Note the open tension the KB flags rather than resolves: repetition appears to move *believing* most on claims you initially reject, while moving *liking* least on things you initially dislike.
- **Interpersonal repetition, in the middle zone.** Repetition does not make you believe the earth is a square. It makes you believe "everyone knows she's unstable," "this is standard in the industry," "I've told you about that fee a hundred times" — unverifiable, technical, or merely unfamiliar claims, which is exactly where a manipulator's claims about your memory, your finances, your reputation and your group live ([[circular-conversation]], [[smear-campaigns]], [[gaslighting]]). And the transmission finding makes gossip self-propelling: people pass on repeated material they *disbelieve*, having quietly rated it less unethical to share [10].
- **"Do your own research" funnels.** The target is handed a question, a starting point and a search term rather than a conclusion, then congratulated for figuring it out themselves. The hard evidence here is specific: across five experiments, **searching online to evaluate false articles increased the probability of believing them**, concentrated among people whose searches returned low-quality results — the mechanism is *data voids*, informational spaces where the only corroboration is junk [22]. Stack on top the labeled inference that self-assembled conclusions are held harder [18], and you get the shape used by [[cult-conversion-funnel]] and [[radicalization-pipeline]]: correcting the belief later reads as an insult to the target's judgment rather than a factual update.
- **Familiarity manufactured by harmless contact.** In the grooming literature the relevant stage is trust development — accumulated benign, individually deniable contact preceding any request ([[grooming-sequence]] owns the sequence and its safety apparatus). The honest statement of what the fluency literature contributes: it explains why the familiar adult reads as safe *to the adults around the child*, and nothing more. Experimentally, observers shown vignettes of grooming stages **could not rate any stage as more likely to precede abuse** [21], and the behaviors that best *differentiate* offenders cluster at desensitization, not warmth. This file supplies no detection signal here, by design.
- **Effortful onboarding.** The target is made to do work early — the starter kit, the first inventory order, the modules, the wallet-and-seed-phrase ritual, the personalized dashboard. Presented as onboarding; the design signature to recognize is *effortful but reliably completable* [19]. [[mlm-lifecycle]] documents the buy-in and inventory-loading sequence; [[commitment-consistency]] owns effort justification and sunk cost, and this file routes there rather than duplicating the escalation machinery. Note the honesty flag: no study tests the IKEA effect on MLM inventory or crypto onboarding — this is the KB's extrapolation, and the better-evidenced neighbors carry the weight.
- **Polish as an argument.** Perceptual fluency means a clean deck, a confident delivery, a well-designed invoice or login page raises judged truth without touching content [1] — which is why generated fluent text at scale ([[ai-enabled-manipulation]]) and pixel-accurate lures ([[phishing-pretexting]]) are cheap wins, and why "I've heard of them" functions as a false credential in [[affinity-fraud]] and [[advertising-marketing]] alike. False fame is real: a name met once, recalled 24 hours later as reputable, source long gone [14].

## Ethical use vs exploitation

The same levers carry honest communication. Public-health messages, safety briefings, and instructions worth remembering are repeated *because they are true and matter*; spaced practice and self-testing are genuine learning tools ([[memory-retrieval-quirks]]); a plain, well-designed document is a courtesy; letting a learner or customer reach the conclusion themselves is good teaching and good selling; and onboarding sometimes just requires work. The line ([[manipulation-vs-influence]]): exploitation **manufactures the repetition** (one interested party staged across many apparently independent surfaces), **substitutes form for evidence** (the rhyme, the polish, the confident cadence carrying weight the content cannot), **pre-constrains the search space** while calling the result your own research, or **extracts effort that serves the seller** and then cites that effort as a reason you cannot leave. The disclosure test is unusually clean here: an honest source can answer "where did this first come from, and who else says it independently?" without irritation, and does not lose the argument when you restate its slogan in flat, ugly prose.

## Recognition

These are prompts for suspicion, not diagnostics — the misattribution is not introspectively available; the structural tells below are the actual test.

- **"I keep hearing this, so there must be something to it."** The claim arrives smooth, without friction and without an argument attached — and you cannot say who first made it.
- **"I know that's not right, but it still lands."** The signature of knowledge neglect [6], and the experience readers most often mistake for their own stupidity. You can state the correct fact and feel the false one at the same time.
- **The click of a rhyming slogan** — a phrase that feels *settled*, as if it were true before anyone said it. Aphorisms, jingles, mottos, mission statements, the family saying, the group's stock answer.
- **"I figured this out myself, nobody told me."** A conclusion that feels earned, defended unusually hard, remembered along with the *effort* but not the *sources* — especially if someone handed you the question, the search terms, and the compliment about your independent thinking.
- **"I've heard of him."** Warmth or credibility attached to a name you know nothing about; familiarity that outlived its source [14]. Also: warmth toward someone whose actual track record with you is thin but whose presence has been frequent — which is also what an ordinary new friendship feels like; the question is whether anything but frequency is behind the warmth (*informed-preference*).
- **"After everything I've put into this."** Your valuation tracking your own labor rather than the thing's performance — and effort invoked at exactly the moment you consider leaving [19].
- **"I don't believe it, but it's worth passing on."** The moral bar dropping while belief stays flat — the engine of gossip you would never endorse [10].
- **Typical phrases (from whoever is doing the repeating):** "Everyone knows that." "It's common knowledge." "People have been saying this for years." "I've told you this a hundred times." "As we always say…" "Don't take my word for it — go look it up yourself." "You built this; nobody can take that from you." "Just try it for thirty days and see."
- **Structural tells, which are the actual diagnostics** (the feeling is unavailable to introspection): Can you name the source? Is the claim *repeated* or *supported* — what was the evidence the *first* time? Has it now been said exactly **twice**? (Don't wait for a pattern; most of the effect is banked by the second exposure [8].) Did the claim arrive dressed — rhyme, cadence, a clean slide, a confident voice? Did you generate the conclusion, or were you steered into generating it? Does your valuation track your investment rather than the results?
- **Escalation signs:** the same three claims from more mouths in more settings; the claim starts appearing in *your* mouth; you find yourself defending a position whose original evidence you can no longer reconstruct; effort demands ratchet while returns stay vague.

## Resistance

1. **Expect falsehoods before you enter the field** ([[inoculation-prebunking]]). Warning people that some of what they are about to see is false cuts the truth effect to less than half, and the protection persists 3–6 days [9]. Practical form: before a sales meeting, a group session, or a scroll, say to yourself that some of this is false. This is the single best-evidenced counter in the file.
2. **Rate as you go, don't absorb and sort later.** Focusing on accuracy at the moment of exposure — actually judging each claim true or false — wiped out the later illusion, though **only for material you already know something about** [9]. Where you know nothing, this buys nothing; use step 3 instead.
3. **Perform source recollection deliberately** ([[verification-rituals]], [[documentation-practices]]). The question is not "is this true?" — that invokes the corrupted feeling — but "**who told me this, when, and how do they know?**" Source recall is a separate act that does not happen on its own [14]. Writing it down converts a fluency judgment into a checkable record. Source-monitoring failure is the most age-graded part of this mechanism — it increases with age — so repetition-based claims land hardest exactly where source recall is weakest, which is why the written record matters more, not less, with age ([[elder-targeting]]).
4. **Separate form from content, explicitly** [15]. Restate the slogan in flat, ugly prose and see whether you still agree: "What sobriety conceals, alcohol reveals" → "drunk people say what they actually think" → sometimes, partly. Paraphrase is the enemy of a fluent surface, and this attenuation is a finding, not a folk remedy.
5. **Encode claims as somebody's opinion**, not as free-floating content. Material encoded as opinion showed no illusory-truth effect, and for some opinion statements a reversed one [9]. Attaching a name to a claim as you receive it is cheap and appears to defuse it.
6. **Count independent sources, not repetitions** — and read *laterally*. Searching a false claim's own terminology drives you into the data void that made it stick; research the *source*, not the claim [22]. "I've heard it from six places" is worthless until you can show the six were not one.
7. **Reduce exposure rather than resist it.** The effect is not under voluntary control and grows across delay [14]. Where the repetition field belongs to one person, the reliable move is leaving it ([[gray-rock]], [[no-contact-exit-planning]], an audited information diet), not out-arguing it.
8. **Price your labor separately from the asset.** For anything you built into: *would I buy this today, at this price, already assembled, knowing what I know now?* Hours spent are gone either way and are not part of the thing's value to anyone but you — and effort cited as a *reason to continue* is the tell that it was extracted rather than invested ([[escalation-entrapment]]).
9. **What does not work, stated plainly:** knowing about the effect [6]; being paid for accuracy [10]; being a generally skeptical person [8]; fact-check labels on the item [7]; disbelieving it [10]; and **making the material harder to read** [16][17]. This is a set of procedures, not an identity.

## Caveats

- **Familiarity is usually a valid signal, and the field says so.** People accept incoming information by default "because most claims in our environments are true" [2]; the fluency→truth link is *learned* precisely because fluency and truth are positively correlated in ordinary environments — participants taught a reversed correlation reversed the effect [3]. Things you have met repeatedly usually are common, usually are true, and usually are safe. A reader who leaves this file distrusting everything familiar has been made **worse** at judging truth, not better.
- **Repetition itself is never the tell.** True things are repeated too — safety rules, public-health guidance, the reminders a loving partner gives. The diagnostic is repetition **from a single interested source without a retrievable first origin and without independent corroboration**, not repetition. And a person who repeats themselves is not thereby manipulating you: anxious people, tired people, and people who feel unheard all repeat themselves.
- ***Informed-preference* is not mere exposure.** Liking something because you have genuine experience of it — the café whose coffee you have actually tasted a hundred times, the colleague whose work you have actually watched — is preference built on evidence. Mere exposure is the *contentless* case: warmth with no accumulated information behind it. The distinction is the presence or absence of a track record, not the number of encounters.
- **Magnitude discipline.** g = 0.37 corrected, judgments of implausible content staying below the midpoint, roughly half of participants showing no or reversed effects on absurd claims [5][7]. "Illusory truth" does not explain why someone you disagree with holds a view, and this file must never be used that way ([[epistemic-guardrails]]). Symmetrically: "that's just the illusory truth effect" is itself a dismissal move — a psychology citation used to override a specific, checkable claim ([[gaslighting]]'s reality-denial pattern in academic dress).
- **The mere-exposure fence cuts against over-detection.** Exposure works on neutral material, has an inverted-U ceiling, and can *reverse* for material you already dislike [12]. So "he kept showing up" is not evidence of anything, and repeated contact with someone you disliked to begin with should, per the literature, make things worse rather than build warmth.
- **No grooming detection signal.** This file supplies none, by design. Observers cannot identify grooming stages prospectively [21], warmth and contact are indistinguishable from ordinary kindness at the level of any single act, and reading this section as a reason to suspect coaches, teachers, and relatives would make the KB a false-positive engine. Concerns about a child route to [[grooming-sequence]] and to reporting, not to bias-spotting.
- **The applied stories are labeled extrapolations.** No study connects the IKEA effect to MLM inventory, crypto onboarding, or any coercive-commitment context; no experimental study connects the generation effect to cult conversion; advertising's use of mere exposure has *mixed* effectiveness evidence [12][19]. The KB proposes these bridges and says so, and rests the real weight on [[commitment-consistency]], [[loss-aversion-endowment]], and the data-voids finding [22].
- **The disfluency collapse is the file's humility exhibit** — and it is live in the wild: "we made it harder on purpose, it's better for you" is a real pedagogy and wellness-product justification resting on a claim that mostly did not survive [16][17]. A file arguing that ease is exploited owes it to the reader to report that the mirror claim was tested at scale and failed.
- **The not-invented-here look-alike.** A group that reflexively rejects outside input may be under milieu control ([[isolation-tactics]], [[information-asymmetry]]) — or may just be a normal organization with normal NIH, or may be right about the dependency risk. Its most manipulation-relevant use runs the other way: "you're just being NIH about it" is a dismissal of substantive technical or safety objections, a cousin of [[tone-policing]] and [[bad-faith-argumentation]].
- **Literature limits.** The illusory-truth corpus is Western, convenience-sampled, trivia-heavy, short-delay, and unstandardized, with little external replication [5]; mere exposure's *explanation* is openly unsettled [12]; the fluency umbrella is elastic enough to explain anything, which is a reason to distrust it when it explains everything [1].
- **Seams.** [[availability-salience]] declares the boundary from its side: it owns *what comes to mind first and what grabs attention* — noticing; this file owns *what feels true because it processes easily* — believing-because-easy. Availability asks "what came to mind?"; fluency asks "how did it feel to think it?" — and repetition is the input driving both, which is why "say it constantly" appears under both headings; the contested ease-of-retrieval paradigm sits on the seam and stays there. [[memory-fallibility]] keeps its narrower and genuinely different claim — repetition producing a false *episodic memory of having been told* ("I definitely told you about the fee") — while this file is the mechanism home for repetition producing a *truth judgment*: same repetition, two downstream misattributions. [[memory-self-editing]] already ceded illusory truth here. [[commitment-consistency]] owns effort justification and sunk cost; this file owns only labor→valuation. [[loss-aversion-endowment]] owns mere ownership. Two fluency-adjacent codex neighbors live elsewhere: familiarity's effects on *category* judgments belong to [[stereotyping-essentialism]], and its effects on felt *duration* to [[time-distortions]]. "Frequency and the conference of referential validity." *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior* 16(1), 107–112 (sixty plausible statements, three sessions two weeks apart; repeated-false confidence 4.2 → 4.6 → 4.7 on a 7-point scale, non-repeated flat).
5. Ye, S., Attali, D., Ghazi, M., Cachia, A., Cassotti, M., & Borst, G. (2026). 6. Fazio, L. K., Brashier, N. M., Payne, B. K., & Marsh, E. J. (2015). "Knowledge does not protect against illusory truth." *JEP: General* 144(5), 993–1002 ("knowledge neglect"; kilt/sari item).
7. Pennycook, G., Cannon, T. D., & Rand, D. G. 8. Hassan, A., & Barber, S. J. (2021). 9. Jalbert, M., Schwarz, N., & Newman, E. J. (2020). M., Eliseev, E. D., & Marsh, E. J. (2020). *Cognition* 194, 104054 (accuracy focus at exposure eliminates the later illusion, but only where relevant knowledge is held); Riesthuis, P., & Woods, J. 10. Effron, D. A., & Raj, M. (2020). "Exposure and affect: Overview and meta-analysis of research, 1968–1987." *Psychological Bulletin* 106(2), 265–289 (classic meta across modalities and subliminal/supraliminal exposures — **the widely-quoted overall effect size could not be verified against the primary and is not printed**; Bornstein 1993 also reports outgroup exposure reducing prejudice).
12. Montoya, R. M., Horton, R. S., Vevea, J. L., Citkowicz, M., & Lauber, E. A. (2017). 13. Bornstein, R. F., & D'Agostino, P. R. (1992).
