# Framing & Anchoring

> Judgments are made *relative to reference points* — and whoever supplies the first number, the comparison set, or the gain/loss frame has chosen your reference point for you. Most pricing psychology is exactly this: deciding what your decision will be measured against.

## The lever

Two of the most robust findings in judgment research. **Anchoring**: estimates assimilate toward initially presented values — even arbitrary ones (a wheel of fortune moved estimates of UN membership) [1], and the literature review verdict is that anchoring holds across domains, tasks, and contexts [2]. In negotiation, first offers strongly predict final settlements [3]. **Framing**: logically equivalent descriptions flip preferences — the Asian-disease problem made majorities risk-averse under a "lives saved" frame and risk-seeking under a "lives lost" frame [1]. Add **comparison-set control**: introducing an asymmetrically dominated decoy shifts choice toward its target [4] — though honesty requires the boundary condition: decoy effects are strong in stylized numeric formats and weak, absent, or reversed with real perceptual goods [4]. The unifying point: none of these change the options — they change the *measuring stick*.

## The relative-judgment family

Four neighboring biases run on the same machinery — judgment is comparative all the way down — each with its own exploitation signature:

- **Contrast effect** — the same thing judged after different neighbors *is* a different experience: judgments are displaced away from an extreme standard just encountered (the assimilation–contrast work of Sherif, Taub & Hovland, in the adaptation-level lineage [8]), and the effect reaches social judgment — attractiveness ratings of ordinary people drop after exposure to idealized media images [8]. The sequential exploit is *show the bad one first*: real-estate "setup" properties — overpriced, run-down listings toured before the house they mean to sell — and the suit-before-the-sweater sales order, so the second price lands soft [8]. This is the perceptual engine inside [[door-in-the-face]] — that file owns the request-sequence tactic; the mechanism here is why the second ask *looks* small.
- **Distinction bias** — comparing options side by side magnifies differences you will never feel once you live with one alone: in joint evaluation people predict large satisfaction gaps between nearby options, then experience almost none in separate (single-option) consumption [9] — one strong demonstration sitting on the well-replicated joint/separate-evaluation preference-reversal literature [9]. The exploit is the *spec-sheet showroom*: side-by-side screens, megapixels, trim tables — engineered joint evaluation selling a premium on distinctions that won't survive the ride home, because ownership is lived in separate mode.
- **Focusing effect (focusing illusion)** — whatever attention rests on gets over-weighted in judgments of the whole. Midwesterners and Californians report equal life satisfaction, yet both predict Californians are happier the moment climate is made salient [10]; income gaps likewise loom larger in prediction than in experienced well-being [10]. Kahneman's maxim: "Nothing in life is as important as you think it is when you are thinking about it" [10]. Wilson & Gilbert's **focalism** is the affective-forecasting twin — imagining an event vividly while forgetting everything else that will fill those days [10]. The exploit is *single-attribute selling*: the pitch that parks your attention on one feature — the view, the kitchen, the badge, "imagine how happy you'll be" — and lets attention do the inflating.
- **Weber–Fechner law** — perceived magnitude grows roughly logarithmically with actual magnitude (Fechner 1860; Stevens' power law is the modern refinement — the log form is an approximation, but diminishing relative sensitivity is the durable core) [11], so the same absolute difference shrinks as the baseline grows. $5 off a $15 item feels substantial; $5 off a $1,500 item feels like nothing — the jacket–calculator problem: majorities will drive across town to save $5 on a $15 calculator but not on a $125 one [1][11]. The exploit is timing: add-ons and fees introduced at the big-ticket moment, when your log-scaled perception rounds them to zero — and savings stated in whichever unit (percentage or absolute) *looks* bigger [11].

## Evidence base

- Tversky & Kahneman (1974), anchoring-and-adjustment; (1981), the framing of decisions [1]; Furnham & Boo (2011): robustness review [2].
- Galinsky & Mussweiler (2001): first-offer anchoring — *and* its documented counter: the anchor's advantage disappears when the recipient focuses on the opponent's reservation price or their own target [3].
- Huber, Payne & Puto (1982): the attraction/decoy effect; Frederick, Lee & Baskin (2014): its limits with real goods (with the original authors' rebuttal in the same issue — graded real-but-fragile) [4].
- Pricing field evidence: $9 endings raised demand in catalog experiments, strongest for new items where price knowledge is weakest [5]; partitioned prices lower recalled totals and raise demand [6].
- Regulation knows the lever: FTC Guides Against Deceptive Pricing (16 CFR 233) treat fictitious "was" prices as deceptive — and fake reference prices have driven major litigation (*Hinojos v. Kohl's*, 9th Cir. 2013) [7]; the FTC's junk-fee rule (16 CFR 464, effective May 12, 2025) now mandates up-front total prices for live-event tickets and short-term lodging [7].
- Sherif, Taub & Hovland (1958): judgments displaced away from extreme anchor stimuli — the contrast half of assimilation–contrast [8]; Kenrick & Gutierres (1980): the media-image contrast effect on attractiveness judgments [8].
- Hsee & Zhang (2004): distinction bias in joint vs separate evaluation [9], grounded in the joint/separate preference-reversal review (Hsee et al., 1999) and bounded by general evaluability theory (2010) [9].
- Schkade & Kahneman (1998) and Kahneman et al. (2006, *Science*): the focusing illusion for climate and income [10]; Wilson et al. (2000): focalism in affective forecasting [10] — magnitude contested by Levine et al. (2012), defended in Wilson & Gilbert's (2013) reply [10].
- Fechner (1860) / Stevens (1957): psychophysical magnitude scaling and its power-law refinement [11]; Dehaene (2003): the logarithmic mental number line [11]; Thaler (1980) and the jacket–calculator problem [1]: relative-vs-absolute savings.

## How it's weaponized

The recurring exploit: **a reference point chosen for what it does to you, not for what's true.**

- **Fictitious reference prices** — "was $499, now $199" where $499 was never charged: the anchor is simply invented [7] ([[sales]], [[advertising-marketing]]; outlet "compare at" tags; inflated MSRP games).
- **Aggressive first anchors** — the opening number in negotiations, salary discussions, and settlements, set high/low precisely because final agreements gravitate toward it [3] ([[bad-faith-negotiation]]; within disclosed haggling norms it's *hard bargaining* — the lever is identical, the concealment differs).
- **Decoy menus** — the overpriced middle option existing solely to make the target option look reasonable [4]; subscription-tier design; popcorn sizes ([[thats-not-all-decoys]]).
- **Drip and partitioned pricing** — the advertised number anchoring the decision before mandatory fees surface at checkout [6][7] ([[dark-patterns-obstruction]] adjacent; now illegal in covered US sectors [7]).
- **Frame selection in pitches** — "saves 200 lives" vs "400 still die"; "90% fat free" vs "10% fat"; insurance sold in loss frames, investments in gain frames [1] ([[loss-aversion-endowment]] supplies the asymmetry frames exploit; [[propaganda-devices]]: card stacking is frame control at scale).
- **Charm pricing and unit games** — $9.99, "per day" reframings ("just $3/day" anchoring away from $1,095/year), shrinkflation holding the price anchor while the quantity moves [5].
- **Setup sequencing** — the deliberately bad option shown first: run-down overpriced listings before the target house, the premium suit before the sweater, the outrageous ask before the real one [8] ([[door-in-the-face]] is this lever built into a request sequence).
- **Spec-sheet showdowns** — comparison selling engineered for joint evaluation: trim ladders, megapixel tables, tier grids magnifying differences you'll never feel in single-option life [9].
- **Spotlight pitches** — "imagine how happy you'll be": holding one attribute in attention until it swallows the whole judgment, while everything that actually fills a life goes unweighted [10].
- **Big-ticket add-on stacking** — $400 rustproofing in the shadow of a $30,000 car, warranties and fees timed for the moment log-scaled perception rounds them down [11]; drip-priced fees hiding in a large anchor's shadow [6]; discounts stated in percentages on small items and absolutes on large ones — whichever reads bigger [11].
- **Conversational anchoring in conflict** — opening accusations setting the negotiation range for apologies; "I could have done much worse" framing mistreatment against an invented baseline ([[darvo]] adjacency; [[history-rewriting]] re-anchors the past itself).

## Ethical use vs exploitation

All communication frames; all negotiation anchors — choosing emphasis is unavoidable and often honest (a true "was" price is information). The line ([[manipulation-vs-influence]]): exploitation **fabricates the reference point** (fake prices, invented baselines), **hides the total** until anchored commitment exists (drip pricing), or **constructs comparison sets whose only function is distortion** (pure decoys) — fabrication and concealment, not the mere presence of a frame.

## Recognition

- **You're evaluating against a number they supplied** — the sticker, the "compare at", their opening offer — rather than against independent information you gathered.
- **The deal only looks good relative to its own packaging**: remove their reference point and the absolute numbers stop impressing.
- **Totals that grow as you commit** — fees surfacing after the anchor has done its work [6][7].
- A third option nobody would choose, sitting there making the expensive one look wise [4].
- **You only saw the good option after worse ones** — the viewing order was chosen for you; you never evaluated it cold [8].
- **You're buying differences off a comparison table** — ask which of them you'd notice in a month of ownership, alone, with the table gone [9].
- **The pitch keeps steering attention back to one feature**, and every happiness claim hangs on it [10].
- **Response to total-price requests** — asking for the all-in number up front met with evasion, re-anchoring, or irritation: the response-to-resistance tell ([[manipulation-vs-influence]]).
- **Felt-sense indicators:** the pull of "but it was $499!" about a thing you'd never have paid $199 for cold; doing arithmetic in their units ("only $3/day"); feeling you've "won" a negotiation that ended exactly where their first number pointed; an add-on that would outrage you as a stand-alone purchase feeling "small" next to the big number [11].

## Resistance

1. **Bring your own anchor** ([[verification-rituals]]): independent price/comp research *before* exposure to theirs — market rate, comparable sales, salary data. The documented negotiation counter: focus on your target and their alternatives, which erases the first-offer advantage [3].
2. **Re-derive the absolute**: ignore the "was", the tier names, the per-day units — compute total cost and value from zero ("would I pay $199 for this seen cold?").
3. **Strip the comparison set** [4]: evaluate the option you want against your *need*, not against its menu-mates; ask which options exist only to flatter others.
4. **Demand the total up front** — "all-in price, in writing, before anything else" [7]; refusal is the disclosure ([[detection-heuristics]]).
5. **Reframe deliberately, both ways**: restate the gain frame as its loss frame and vice versa ("90% fat-free = 10% fat") — preference that flips with the restatement was frame, not judgment [1].
6. **Anchor-check your conflicts**: when an opening accusation or baseline feels off, name your own baseline explicitly before negotiating within theirs.
7. **Evaluate in the mode you'll live in** [9][10]: you will own one option, alone — cover the comparison column and judge the candidate by itself; run the month-later test ("which of these differences will I still feel?"); and de-focus deliberately by listing what else fills an ordinary week before believing any single-feature happiness claim.
8. **Re-price every add-on cold** [11]: state add-ons, fees, and discounts in absolute dollars as stand-alone purchases — "would I drive across town to save this $5? Then it's the same $5 here"; a saving worth the trip at $15 is worth exactly as much at $1,500.

## Caveats

- **Framing and anchoring are universal cognition**, not malice — everyone who quotes a price or opens a negotiation anchors; teachers and doctors frame honestly to communicate (*healthy persuasion*). The diagnostics are fabrication, concealment, and pure-distortion sets ([[epistemic-guardrails]]).
- **True reference prices are information** — a genuine markdown is real news; the law's line (fictitious vs actual former prices [7]) is the right one.
- **Decoy effects are real but fragile** [4] — don't over-attribute menu design to dark psychology; much of it is ordinary merchandising.
- **Contrast is how perception works** — a seller showing the good house after a mediocre one may just have that inventory; the tell is an *engineered* sequence and foil options nobody was meant to choose [8].
- **Distinction bias is one strong paper on a robust base** [9] — the joint/separate reversal is well-replicated, but the specific "you won't feel it after purchase" hedonic claim has thinner direct replication (hence supported). And joint evaluation is often *good for you*: when differences are real and consequential — interest rates, safety ratings — comparing hard is the defense, not the trap [9].
- **The focusing illusion's size is debated across three published rounds, not two** [10] — Levine et al. (2012) argue procedural artifacts inflate forecasting biases; Wilson & Gilbert (2013) reply that the impact bias survives; Levine et al. (2013) rejoin, claiming to rule out focalism specifically. The exchange is unresolved, and [[time-distortions]] carries the affective-forecasting side of it in full. The core — attended attributes get over-weighted — is safe; precise magnitudes are not.
- **Weber–Fechner is established psychophysics; its pricing application is a mechanism extrapolation** [11] — the relative-savings effect is directly demonstrated [1], but "log perception explains price framing" overstates: money judgments also run through mental accounting and money illusion ([[probability-blindspots]] owns those).
- **First offers are legitimate strategy** within recognized norms (*hard bargaining*); the manipulation grade attaches to fabricated constraints and concealed totals, not to opening strong.
- **Boundary with [[loss-aversion-endowment]]:** that file owns the *asymmetry* (losses loom larger); this one owns *reference-point placement* — frames work largely by deciding what counts as a loss. ([[scarcity-urgency]]'s availability-constraint boundary is declared in that file, against loss-aversion.)
