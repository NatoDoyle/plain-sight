# Memory Retrieval Quirks

> Nineteen codex entries about how memory hands things back. One family — order — is genuinely worked, for votes and money. Two describe what repeated selective questioning does to a witness. One tilts what a target retrieves about their own history with nobody doing anything to them. Three are learning techniques the codex miscategorized as biases. Three are this file's best false-positive guards. The rest is laboratory furniture that nobody has ever exploited and nobody could. This is the file where the honest answer is mostly "nothing pulls this lever, and here is why the entry is in the index anyway."

## The lever

This file is the codex-honesty test case, and it should announce that in its first paragraph. Every other mechanism file in this KB exists because tactics pull on it. This one exists because [[bias-codex-index]] enumerates the whole Cognitive Bias Codex (189 listed slots / 188 unique names) and promises each a home — and when the honest verdict on a member is "no manipulation angle exists," the file says so rather than inventing one. A defense KB that manufactures threats is failing in the same direction as one that misses them ([[epistemic-guardrails]]).

The honest tally of the nineteen rows that land here, stated up front: **one** member family has a substantial applied-exploitation literature (order effects). **Two** describe a real, documented hazard in how questions are asked (retrieval-induced forgetting and part-list cueing). **One** shapes what a target retrieves about their own history without anyone doing anything (mood-congruent recall). **Three** are among the best-evidenced *beneficial* findings in cognitive psychology and belong in the defense stack. **Three** are the single most useful false-positive guards this KB has. The remaining members are lab phenomena, and two of the most famous are in worse shape than their citation counts suggest.

The unifying substrate is **cue-dependent forgetting**: Tulving and Pearlstone's distinction between what is *available* in memory and what is *accessible* right now — items unrecalled in free recall come back readily when a category cue is supplied, so most forgetting is retrieval failure, not trace loss; the encoding-specificity principle adds that a cue works to the extent its features were encoded alongside the target [10]. (Relevance: not-a-lever; but it is the theoretical spine of every counter in this file.) Everything below is a way that spine bends.

**Order.** The **serial position effect**: in free recall of a list, the probability of recall is a U-shaped function of input position — a **primacy effect** at the start and a **recency effect** at the end, with the middle lost. Murdock established the curve across list lengths and presentation rates; Glanzer and Cunitz produced the double dissociation that made it famous — a ~30-second filled delay abolishes recency while leaving primacy intact, and slowing presentation boosts primacy while leaving recency alone [1]. The curve is not in doubt (evidence: established). The two-store story built on it is only one explanation among several: recency reappears in delayed recall under continuous-distractor conditions, and single-store temporal-distinctiveness accounts reproduce the same curve without a short-term buffer [1]. The **serial recall effect** is the codex listing the same phenomenon twice — it is the position curve measured under order-constrained rather than free recall (steeper primacy, weaker recency, transposition errors to adjacent positions), not an independent finding. Naming that redundancy is exactly the kind of honesty this file exists to model. (Relevance: lever — the one in the cluster.)

**Selective retrieval.** **Memory inhibition**, better known as **retrieval-induced forgetting** (RIF): practising retrieval of *some* items from a studied category makes the *unpractised* items of that same category harder to recall later than baseline items from unpractised categories [7]. Remembering causes forgetting. The meta-analysis across 512 samples puts the effect at g = 0.35 (95% CI 0.32–0.38), robust to publication-bias correction [7]. Its sibling is the **part-list cueing effect**: being handed a subset of the studied items as "hints" at test makes you recall *fewer* of the rest than getting no hints at all — Slamecka's 1968 result, still called "a persisting enigma" sixteen years later and meta-analytically confirmed in 2023 as a robust, medium-sized impairment across 109 samples (N = 5,605) [9]. Both matter here for one reason: they are mechanisms by which **someone else's control of the cues shapes what you can produce**. (Relevance: lever, narrowly — see How it's weaponized.)

**Context and mood.** The **context effect** — recall is better when the retrieval environment matches the encoding environment — is the entry whose flagship demonstration collapsed; the story is told in Evidence base. What survives is small, recall-specific, and easily overwhelmed [11]. **Mood-congruent memory bias** is the tilt of recall toward material matching your current mood [12]. The clinically important version is not "sad people remember sad things" but the loss of the healthy positivity bias: non-depressed people preferentially recall positive material, depressed groups preferentially recall negative material, and self-relevance is a repeatedly-reported moderator — the deficit for positive information shows up most when the material is *about you* [12]. In adolescents the robustly-measured effect is not valence-congruent item recall at all but **overgeneral autobiographical memory** (g = 0.86 across 36 studies, N = 4,293), with most valence contrasts null [12]. (Relevance: amplifier, target-side. It is not something anyone does to you; it is a reason your own retrospective inventory is not a neutral instrument.)

**The learning tools the codex miscategorized.** The **spacing effect** (distributed practice beats massed practice; 839 assessments across 317 experiments, with the optimal gap widening as the required retention interval lengthens) [13] and the **testing effect** (retrieval practice beats restudy for long-term retention, with initial *recall* tests producing larger benefits than recognition tests) [14] are among the largest, cleanest findings in the whole cluster — and they are not biases. Their presence under "we edit and reinforce some memories after the fact" is a category error in the codex, and the correct KB response is to use them: they are the scheduling engine for [[inoculation-prebunking]]. The **levels of processing effect** — semantic encoding produces better retention than structural or phonemic encoding — is a third beneficial regularity with a famous problem in its theory (Evidence base). (Relevance: not-a-lever; defensive tools.)

**The false-positive shelf.** **Absent-mindedness** is Schacter's second sin of memory: a breakdown at the attention/encoding interface — keys, appointments, whether you locked the door — as distinct from losing something properly stored [17]. The **tip of the tongue phenomenon** is retrieval failure with preserved partial access: you can report the first letter, the syllable count, the word's shape, while the word itself stays out of reach. It is near-universal, happens about once a week, increases with age, is elicited especially by proper names, and resolves during the experience about half the time [18]. The **next-in-line effect** is a recall deficit for whatever was said immediately before your own turn to speak in a round-robin — plausible on ordinary divided-attention grounds, but a two-paper literature with almost no citation traffic and no modern replication located [18]. These three are the most *defensively* useful members of the cluster precisely because they argue against over-detection: they are the base-rate explanations for the memory gaps that [[gaslighting]] claims to explain. (Relevance: observer-side.)

**Lab furniture.** The **suffix effect**: appending one redundant spoken item ("go") that participants are told to ignore selectively destroys the recency end of an auditorily-presented list [19]. The **modality effect**: heard lists show larger and longer-lasting recency than read lists [19]. The **list-length effect**: recognition of a studied item is worse when it came from a longer list — an existence dispute that ran for over two decades (Evidence base) [19]. Each is real in the lab. None has any documented use by anyone against anyone, and there is no plausible pathway by which one could. Say it plainly; the codex lists them because it lists everything in the memory literature, not because they are levers. (Relevance: not-a-lever.)

**The contested one.** The **Google effect** — the claim that expecting information to remain available online reduces what you encode of it — is the entry where the popular version, the debunk, and the truth are three different things. Full story in Evidence base. (Relevance: not-a-lever, at any evidential grade it ends up with.)

## Evidence base

The file grade reflects the core (serial position, cue-dependence, RIF, part-list cueing, spacing, testing, tip-of-the-tongue — all established). Members carry their own grades. Honestly sorted:

- **Survived cleanly:** the serial-position curve [1]; encoding specificity and availability-vs-accessibility, now with engram-level support in mice [10]; RIF (k = 512, g = 0.35, unchanged when restricted to genuine retrieval practice) [7]; part-list cueing (109 samples, insensitive to most moderators — the signature of a robust rather than fragile effect) [9]; spacing [13]; testing [14]; tip-of-the-tongue [18].
- **The most consequential applied finding, and it is not what the folk version says.** Selective questioning reshapes recall *without any misinformation*. Shaw, Bjork and Handal's conclusion, verbatim: "Repeated interrogation of a witness can modify the witness's memory — enhancing the recall of certain details while inducing the forgetting of other details — even when no misinformation is contained or implied in the questioning" [8]. Socially-shared RIF then took it out of the lab: a speaker's selective retelling induces forgetting of unmentioned-but-related material in the *listener*, demonstrated for 9/11 memories, for the emotional autobiographical memories of intimate couples, in free-flowing conversation, and merely from overhearing — regardless of emotional valence or whose memory it was [8].
- **Reversed.** The **context effect**'s flagship is gone. Godden and Baddeley's 1975 diver study is one of the most reproduced figures in introductory psychology; Murre's 2021 direct replication (16 divers, same four-condition design) found no same-context advantage, and his own 2022 addendum — correcting his analysis — *reinforced* the failure [11]. Godden and Baddeley themselves had already found "no trace of context dependency" for recognition in 1980 [11]. The broader phenomenon does survive meta-analysis as "reliable," but small and fragile: it shrinks or vanishes with non-contextual cues at learning (overshadowing) or at test (outshining) — which in ordinary life is nearly always [11]. Grade the environmental context effect `supported`, and never reproduce the diver study as a settled dramatic result.
- **Reversed, and worse than its citation count.** The **Google effect** (evidence: contested). Sparrow, Liu and Wegner's 2011 *Science* paper made two separable claims: a *priming* claim (hard trivia questions automatically activate internet concepts — the "Google Stroop effect") and an *offloading* claim (expected future access lowers recall of content and raises recall of location). The priming claim was included in the Social Sciences Replication Project, where 13 of 21 studies replicated and replication effect sizes averaged ~50% of originals — and it was among the failures [20]. The original author objected on design grounds; Hesselmann then re-ran it with a design closer to the original and reported "no conclusive evidence" [20]. That is a two-strikes record on a redesign the original author asked for. The offloading half is alive but conditional — it replicates when participants have prior experience that saved information really does stay accessible, and offloading can also *help* (saving one list improves memory for the next) [20]. A 2024 meta-analysis of 35 studies reports support, but it is a correlational media-effects synthesis in a public-health venue, not a replication of the experimental effects, and it does not rehabilitate the priming result [20]. Two further honesty notes: "digital amnesia" is not a scientific construct — it is reportedly a term coined by a security vendor in 2015 for an unreviewed marketing survey [20]; and what *does* replicate is a confidence finding, not a memory one — across five experiments, people who studied by searching online learned less while being as confident or more confident that they had mastered the material [20], which routes to [[memory-self-editing]] — that file owns the misattributed-prior-knowledge row (mistaking what you just looked up for what you already knew) and carries the internet-confidence evidence, while [[self-evaluation-illusions]] owns only the generic overconfidence row — and not to this file.
- **Mechanism disputes that leave the phenomenon intact.** RIF's *existence* is not in question; its *explanation* is (inhibition vs. interference vs. context change). This correction matters because the opposite claim circulates: Jonker, Seli and MacLeod (2013) is a rival theoretical account in *Psychological Review*, **not a failed replication of RIF** — and it is Jonker et al.'s own supporting finding (that restudy plus context change also produces forgetting) that failed to replicate, in three independent reports [7]. Writing "RIF has notable replication failures" would be a factual error. Similarly, the suffix effect's original precategorical-acoustic-store account was undercut by suffix effects with mouthed and visuospatial suffixes — the phenomenon survived, the echoic-store explanation did not [19]; and the two-store reading of the serial-position curve is one account among several [1].
- **An existence dispute, narrower than it sounds, and recently reported resolved.** The **list-length effect** dispute was always about *recognition* memory only — in free recall, longer lists straightforwardly yield lower per-item recall and nobody contests it. Dennis and Humphreys's context-noise model predicts no recognition list-length effect; Kinnell and Dennis concluded there is none once attention confounds are controlled; item-noise theorists disputed the partitioning of interference [19]. A 2025 systematic reexamination co-authored by Dennis himself reports evidence for the effect after all, best described by a square-root function of list length and small relative to other interference sources [19]. Two sweeps of this literature disagreed about whether the dispute is closed; the file reports both and grades it `contested`. It also has zero exploitation angle, which is the point of including it.
- **Established phenomenon, unfalsifiable framework.** **Levels of processing**: Craik and Lockhart's "depth" is operationally defined by the retention it predicts, so "deeper processing produces better memory" risks being true by definition — the objection Baddeley made in print and that Craik and Lockhart themselves answered by defending the framework on heuristic rather than predictive grounds [16]. The empirical correction is stronger: with a rhyme-based test, shallow phonemic encoding *beats* semantic encoding. Memory is an interaction between encoding operations and the demands of the test, not a main effect of depth [16].
- **Weak.** The **next-in-line effect**: original paper plus one follow-up, negligible citation traffic, no modern replication found by two independent sweeps. Grade it under-replicated and lean on the well-established divided-attention-at-encoding literature instead, which is where its plausibility actually comes from [17][18].
- **Supported, not established.** Mood-*congruent* recall is meta-analytically supported but heavily moderated, with documented mood-*incongruent* findings (mood repair) and no clean effect-size estimate in healthy participants; a seven-experiment study found mood-congruent *beliefs* about memory without mood-congruent metacognitive judgments [12]. Keep it strictly separate from mood-*dependent* (state-dependent) retrieval, which is a different and much weaker claim — Bower published his own failure to replicate it, an independent failure appeared the same year, and Eich's programme recovered it only under restrictive conditions [12].
- **Not a bias at all.** Spacing, testing and levels of processing are learning techniques. Even the testing literature has a live methodological correction — a 2026 meta-analysis finds 66% of published "testing effects" confound direct and forward testing effects, with mixed studies producing *larger* effects, concluding the literature "is not a coherent body" [14]. And testing has a documented dark side that matters more to this KB than the benefit: retrieval-enhanced suggestibility, where repeated initial recounting *increases* susceptibility to misinformation introduced afterwards [8].

## How it's weaponized

Start with the negative, by name, because it is most of the answer: **the suffix effect, the modality effect, the list-length effect, the next-in-line effect, tip-of-the-tongue, levels of processing, cue-dependent forgetting, the context effect and the Google effect have no documented use by manipulators, and no plausible pathway to one.** Nobody is going to work you by appending a redundant syllable to a list, changing the room, or making you study a longer one. Two specific inferences are tempting and both are wrong: scammers prefer phone calls for pressure, isolation and the absence of a written record — not because of auditory recency; and abusers control the record by deleting messages, taking the phone and insisting agreements stay verbal — a real and serious tactic that owes nothing to the Google effect and everything to [[documentation-practices]] being the target's actual defense.

What is left is real, and it concentrates in one place: **whoever controls the order, and whoever controls the questions.**

- **Sequence control in a pitch.** Whoever sets the order decides which claims get primacy weight and which get recency weight — and the direction is not fixed. Order effects flip with the timing of the messages relative to the decision (Miller and Campbell's classic result), and with elaboration: high personal relevance and high elaboration produce primacy, low elaboration produces recency [3]. Hogarth and Einhorn's belief-adjustment model gives the general rule — recency dominates short, simple, step-by-step-evaluated sequences; primacy dominates long, complex, end-of-sequence-evaluated ones [3]. The operational consequence for a target is that a party controlling both the sequence *and* your pace can pick the regime. This is heavily entangled with [[framing-anchoring]] (whose anchor lands first) and [[manufactured-urgency]] (which forces the recency regime by removing deliberation) — those files own the moves; this one supplies the retrieval reason they work. Seen structurally in [[timeshare-playbook]] and [[four-square-payment-packing]], where the order of disclosure is the product.
- **Position in a list you didn't order.** Menu items placed at the beginning or end of their category were up to twice as popular as the same items in the middle — in the lab and in a real restaurant [4]. The dark-pattern descendant is list and default ordering in checkout and comparison flows ([[dark-patterns-obstruction]]).
- **Ballot order.** The best-identified causal evidence in the cluster, and the folk version ("being listed first wins elections") is wrong. California has randomized ballot order by alphabet lottery since 1975; across 1978–2002, first position significantly moved only minor-party candidates in general elections, with **no detectable effect on major-party candidates**; in primaries first position benefited everyone (one to three percentage points for major-party candidates, with minor-party candidates sometimes doubling their share); the largest effects were in nonpartisan races [2]. Texas's within-county randomization yields a much larger headline — going from last to first worth nearly ten percentage points in down-ballot and judicial races — with the boundary condition stated in the same sentence: "except for a few high-profile, high-information races" [2]. New Hampshire data run the other way on one moderator, finding primacy in 86% of 84 tests including major-party general-election candidates in 2016 [2]. The KB should not pick a winner: **first position is worth on the order of one to three points, largest in primaries, nonpartisan and low-information contests; whether it moves high-salience partisan races is actively disputed.** The moderator study is the one that generalizes: name-order effects are strongest when voters have less information, feel more ambivalent, have more limited cognitive skills, and devote less effort [2] — the same profile every other mechanism in this KB exploits. The effect extends to Danish PR ballots and to corporate proxy elections for independent directors, strengthening with ballot complexity and shareholder inattention [2].
- **Sequential evaluation of people** — auditions, interviews, pitch days, panel reviews. Later performers score systematically higher in Eurovision and figure-skating judging, and order of appearance predicts final ranking in a major music competition where order is randomly assigned [5]. **The honesty note is load-bearing:** the author of the skating and Eurovision work explicitly rejects a memory explanation — the effect holds under step-by-step scoring with no memory load, so it is standard-shifting and anchoring, not serial position in recall. A 2026 study of a piano competition attributes its later-position premium to delayed use of the top of the scale [5]. And order bias is not universal: a study of elite gymnastics found none [5]. Whoever schedules the sequence still shapes the outcome; the cause is usually not memory.
- **Order of evidence in testimony.** Evidence arranged in story order — chronological and causally coherent — is substantially more persuasive than the same evidence in witness order [6]. This is a narrative-coherence effect, not a primacy/recency effect, and it is arguably more exploitation-relevant than serial position itself.
- **Lineup position** is a fourth case where "position matters" is true and "memory serial position" is false: sequential lineups show a criterion shift — witnesses apply a strict standard early and a looser one at later positions — while simultaneous lineups show no position effect [6]; position moves guessing, not detection. The methodological war over sequential-vs-simultaneous superiority is live (ratio-based diagnosticity measures are confounded with response bias; ROC analysis is the contested corrective), so the file asserts neither [6]. What the field *has* converged on is the correct counter-model for this entire file: blind administration, a pre-lineup free-narrative interview, immediate confidence statements, video-recording the whole procedure, and no repeated identification procedures with the same witness [6].
- **Selective questioning — the "no intent required" hazard.** An investigator, HR officer, lawyer, parent or partner who repeatedly walks someone through *part* of an event measurably degrades that person's later access to the related parts not walked through [8]. Part-list cueing compounds it: being handed someone else's partial account before you give your own suppresses recall of the rest [9]. And repeated recounting increases susceptibility to misinformation introduced afterwards [8]. **Repeated questioning by an interested party is not neutral** — it selectively strengthens some details, weakens others, and softens the ground for later suggestion, with no lies told at any point. **Both brakes belong in the same breath as that sentence.** Magnitude: RIF is a modest effect on word lists over minutes to hours. It tilts retrievability; it does not erase memories, and it is not evidence for repression. Direction: this is an argument for one-shot, free-narrative-first interviewing (Wells et al. procedures) [6], not a reason to discount the account of someone who has been interviewed repeatedly.
- **Selective retelling as history control.** Socially-shared RIF is the cognitive engine underneath [[history-rewriting]]: a partner who repeatedly narrates a fixed curated subset of the shared past — his rescues, your failures, never the surrounding events — can degrade *your* access to the unrehearsed neighbours, and merely overhearing was enough in the experiments [8]. This is a mechanism-level substrate for [[gaslighting]] that requires the manipulator to assert nothing false. The tactic files own the moves and declare the `exploits` edges; this file supplies the reason. **Two mandatory brakes.** First, magnitude: RIF is a modest effect on word lists over minutes to hours. It tilts retrievability; it does not erase memories, and it is not evidence for repression. Nobody should ever be told "your abuser made you forget." Second, sign: selective retrieval by a speaker sometimes *improves* the listener's recall, when access to the encoding context is impaired [8]. Overclaiming here would be precisely the failure this file exists to demonstrate against.
- **Overload at the moment of disclosure.** Divided attention at encoding has large detrimental effects; divided attention at retrieval has much smaller ones [17]. What you fail to attend to when something is said is not weakly stored — it is largely not stored at all, and no later effort recovers it. That asymmetry is why disclosures delivered during flooding, exhaustion, time pressure or a deliberately confusing multi-topic barrage are so effective, and why "I told you about that" is so hard to dispute. The mechanism is not "exploiting absent-mindedness"; it is degrading encoding conditions and then weaponizing the resulting gaps. [[cognitive-overload-confusion]] and [[emotional-flooding]] own that move.
- **Mood-congruent recall is not a tactic** and no file should imply anyone deploys it. It matters because it changes what a target retrieves about their own history in both directions: in a depressive phase, self-relevant negative episodes come back more easily than exculpating ones, so "name three times you were unreasonable" is a much easier question to answer than its converse — a retrieval asymmetry, not a fact about who was unreasonable; and during a reconciliation or [[hoovering]] episode, elevated mood makes the good history retrievable and the bad history oddly remote, which is one contributor to the [[abuse-cycle]]'s repeat. The retrospective-drift side of this — fading affect, peak–end, rosy retrospection — belongs to [[memory-self-editing]], not here.

## Ethical use vs exploitation

Order has to be *something*. Every agenda, syllabus, menu, ballot and closing argument imposes a sequence, and doing so is not manipulation ([[manipulation-vs-influence]]). Likewise, every conversation is selective — nobody recounts a shared past exhaustively, and selective retelling is what talking *is*. The lines that matter:

- **Simultaneous versus sequential presentation.** Honest presentation of options lets you see them at once, in writing, in an order you can change. Exploitation withholds the full set and reveals it in a sequence the other party chose, especially when a simultaneous comparison would be trivially easy to provide.
- **Order plus pace.** Setting a sequence is normal; setting a sequence *and* removing the deliberation time that would neutralize it is the move. A disclosure ordered for clarity survives your sleeping on it; one engineered for recency does not.
- **Free narrative before questions.** A helper, investigator or partner acting in good faith asks for your account first and their questions second, and does not repeat the same narrow subset of questions for months. The forensic standard — blind administration, recording, one-shot procedures, contemporaneous confidence statements [6] — generalizes directly to HR investigations, family mediations and "let's go over it again" conversations.
- **Directionality over time.** All retelling is selective; what distinguishes manipulation is that the selection is *systematically one-directional, resistant to correction, and paired with pressure to accept it as the record*.
- **The disclosure test.** An honest party survives "can I see all the options on one page?", "can I answer tomorrow?", "let me write down my own version first," and "why is it always these same three stories?"

## Recognition

**Be honest about the ceiling here: most of this cluster produces no recognizable experience at all.** There is no felt sense of the suffix effect, the modality effect, the list-length effect, or the Google effect, and a Recognition section pretending otherwise would be manufacturing threat. Two situations do produce recognizable patterns.

**Order effects — situational tells (not feelings):**

- You are one of several being evaluated in sequence and someone else chose the sequence; you are in the middle.
- The options were revealed one at a time when they could trivially have been shown side by side; the order is fixed and not yours to change.
- "Let me lay out the background first" — before you are allowed to ask anything.
- The pitch ends on its strongest claim exactly as you are asked to decide; or an anchor is planted first and then buried in detail.
- A long account is narrated to you in a curated order, and your judgment is requested at the end rather than at each step.
- Decisions demanded immediately after the pitch, with sleeping on it discouraged.
- **Felt-sense:** "the last thing they said is what I keep replaying"; "I can remember the opening and the closing of that conversation and nothing in between."

**Repeated selective questioning and retelling:**

- "Let's go over it again" — repeatedly, always about the same narrow subset of details, always by the same interested party.
- "Remember when you…" as a fixed conversational ritual with the same three examples and never any others.
- Being asked closed, topic-specific questions before you have ever given your own uninterrupted account.
- Your shared history has narrowed to a repertoire of anecdotes told by one person.
- **Felt-sense:** "his account has become my account — I can't reconstruct my own version anymore"; "details I know I once knew feel unreachable, specifically around the things we talk about constantly."

**Phrases heard from the other side that turn ordinary retrieval failure into evidence against you** (this is the seam where the cluster actually matters):

- "If it was that important you'd remember it."
- "I told you that. You were right there."
- "You can't even keep the timeline straight."
- "You can't give me one single example."

None of those inferences is sound, and the last one is aimed straight at overgeneral autobiographical recall, which is a correlate of depressed mood rather than of dishonesty [12].

## Resistance

1. **Force simultaneous comparison** ([[verification-rituals]]): all options on one page, in writing, in an order you set or randomize. This is the single highest-value counter to the whole order family, because it dissolves the sequence rather than fighting it.
2. **Impose a delay, then re-read in reverse.** A filled delay is what abolishes recency in the lab [1]; sleeping on an end-of-sequence pitch is the same intervention. Reading the list bottom-up, and writing out the middle items before deciding, attacks the curve directly.
3. **Score step-by-step, not at the end.** When evaluating candidates or options in sequence, score each one against a fixed written rubric immediately after seeing it, rather than comparing at the end against whoever came last [3][5].
4. **Free narrative first, questions second — and write it down.** Give your own uninterrupted account before anyone supplies theirs, and record it contemporaneously ([[documentation-practices]]). A written record is not subject to retrieval-induced forgetting, part-list cueing, mood-congruent sampling, or anyone's questioning strategy. This file's real defensive payload is the sheer number of independent ways retrieval degrades — all of which the contemporaneous record routes around.
5. **Deliberately rehearse the unrehearsed.** Socially-shared RIF operates on what goes *unmentioned*, so writing down the parts of the history the other person never brings up is a mechanism-matched counter. (Reasonable inference from the RIF literature, not a tested intervention — labelled as such.)
6. **Use mental context reinstatement, with its price disclosed.** Reconstructing where you were, what the room looked like, the time of day, what you were feeling is a validated retrieval aid and the basis of the Cognitive Interview — and imagined reinstatement works nearly as well as physically returning, so a target never has to re-enter an unsafe place to do it [10][11]. The honest caveat: cognitive-interview meta-analyses find gains in correct details accompanied by some increase in errors, so pair it with contemporaneous notes rather than substituting it for them [10]. It does not recover repressed memories, and this file does not stand adjacent to that literature ([[memory-fallibility]] carries the false-memory evidence).
7. **Refuse consequential exchanges while overloaded.** The counter to divided attention at encoding is not "pay better attention" — the effect is large and largely involuntary. It is structural: nothing consequential agreed while flooded, exhausted or rushed; get it in writing; ask again later in calm conditions [17].
8. **Space and self-test the defensive knowledge itself.** This is where the two "beneficial biases" earn their place. Inoculation against misinformation stayed stable for at least three months when participants were tested at regular intervals — and decayed to non-significance over two months when they were not [15]. A large preregistered programme (N = 11,759) confirms that memory-targeted booster interventions extend the protection [15]. Reading a tactic file once is a decayed intervention within two months; spaced re-exposure plus free-recall self-quizzing ("what are the tells, without looking?") is the difference [13][14][15]. Note the honest tension: retrieval practice is the same operation that produces RIF, so practising some of what you know can cost access to the neighbouring things you didn't practise. The net is strongly positive, but a file that presented testing as pure upside next to a RIF section would be inconsistent.

## Caveats

- **Forgetting is not evidence of manipulation.** Every effect in this file occurs in ordinary, unmanipulated people every day. Memory drift, order effects, mood-tinted recall and forgetting-from-selective-remembering are the baseline operating characteristics of human memory. A file listing nineteen ways retrieval degrades, read as nineteen red flags, would be a net harm.
- **And forgetting is not evidence of lying.** This is the caveat the KB most needs from this file. Inconsistent recall, gaps, an unproduceable name or date, a shifting timeline, and an inability to give "one specific example" are normal — tip-of-the-tongue states are near-universal and weekly, retrieval is cue-dependent so the right cue may simply be absent, and overgeneral autobiographical recall tracks depressed mood rather than deception [10][12][18]. Inconsistent recall is routinely weaponized against victims and witnesses in exactly the way [[epistemic-guardrails]] warns about: a target whose account has gaps or shifting detail is showing normal memory, not deception. Hesitation and retrieval failure are also poor deception cues generally ([[lying-typologies]]).
- **Absent-mindedness has an enormous innocent base rate.** Not remembering a conversation overwhelmingly more often means you were tired, distracted, or the conversation was unremarkable — [[gaslighting]]'s trademark gaps have this as their leading innocent explanation. Age, sleep debt, stress, medication, ADHD, depression, perimenopause, concussion and post-viral illness all degrade encoding and retrieval. Memory that has worsened during a difficult period is a reason to see a doctor and start writing things down; it is not, by itself, evidence about anyone else's behaviour.
- **Selective retelling is not a tell.** All conversation is selective, and socially-shared RIF was demonstrated on ordinary people telling each other about 9/11 and about their own lives [8]. What distinguishes manipulation is one-directionality over time, resistance to correction, and pressure to adopt the version — not selectivity itself. And the effect has a documented sign flip: under some conditions a speaker's selective retrieval *improves* the listener's recall [8].
- **RIF does not delete memories and is not repression.** It shifts accessibility at the margin, measured on word lists, over short intervals. Keep it strictly separate from the think/no-think suppression paradigm, where reliable overall effect-size estimates are unavailable, and never let this file be used to relitigate repressed memory.
- **Mood-congruence cuts both ways, and one half is a gaslighting script.** "You're only remembering the bad times" is a standard minimization move; mood-congruent *sampling* does not make the recalled events false, and the correct response is to widen the sample, not discount it. The other half is equally true: a purely recall-based inventory of a relationship taken during a low period is not a neutral instrument, and positive recall during a good phase is a retrieval tilt rather than proof the good phase is fake. People do change and relationships do improve; over-reading this section pessimistically is as much an error as over-reading it optimistically.
- **Most real-world order effects are not memory effects.** Ballot order is driven by low information, menu position by visual scanning, sequential judging by scale calibration, jury order by narrative coherence, and lineup position by response criterion. Order effects also have prominent nulls, and their direction is not predictable a priori — "go last so they remember you" is folk advice, not a finding [3][4][5][6].
- **Do not launder lab effects into defensive advice they don't support.** Two named traps: the modality effect does not justify "scammers prefer phone calls because of auditory recency," and the levels of processing effect does not make "getting you to elaborate on their frame" a new mechanism — that is [[commitment-consistency]] and [[dissonance-exploitation]], which the KB covers with far better evidence. Routing the reader to the better-evidenced file is the correct move.
- **Seams.** [[memory-fallibility]] owns externally-induced distortion — suggestion, misinformation, implanted and false memories, and confident confabulation; this file owns what retrieval hands back and in what order. [[memory-self-editing]] owns self-serving retrospective drift — hindsight bias, rosy retrospection, fading affect, peak–end, telescoping — which is where the "it wasn't that bad" phenomenology lives and where the abuse cycle's episode-level re-rating is best explained; it is this file's declared `distinguished-from` for exactly that reason. [[pattern-illusions]]' recency *illusion* (a linguistics observation about noticing) is not this file's recency *effect*. Two sincere people remembering the same event differently is *memory-divergence*, not evidence of anyone's bad faith. Depressive recall inside abusive relationships is handled with safety guidance in [[coercive-control]] and [[memory-fallibility]], not here.
- **The codex is a name list, not a threat list.** Several members of this cluster are in the index only because [[bias-codex-index]] promises every entry a home. Naming specific members as non-threats — the suffix effect, the modality effect, the list-length effect, the next-in-line effect — is more credible than a general disclaimer, and it is the whole point of the file.
