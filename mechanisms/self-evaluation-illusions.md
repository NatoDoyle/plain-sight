# Self-Evaluation Illusions

> The instrument measuring your competence is the competence being measured, and it grades on a curve you drew. Whoever ratifies the self-image you already hold — too sharp to be conned, in control of this, disciplined enough to handle it, virtuous enough to have earned it — gets you to skip the check that would have caught them. The flattery isn't the payload; the skipped check is.

## The lever

Five things you assess badly about yourself: where you rank, how calibrated you are, how much of the work was yours, how much of the outcome you control, and how you will behave next.

**Ranking yourself.** The **better-than-average effect** (BTAE) — also **illusory superiority** (evidence: established) — is "the tendency for people to perceive their abilities, attributes, and personality traits as superior compared with their average peer" [3]. It is the best-evidenced claim in this file: 124 articles, 291 independent samples, more than 950,000 participants, dz = 0.78, 95% CI [0.71, 0.84], with little evidence of publication bias [3]. Svenson's drivers are the folk icon — 93% of a US sample and 69% of a Swedish one placed themselves in the top half for driving skill [3]. The verified moderators matter defensively: the effect is larger for personality traits than for measurable abilities, larger on positive dimensions, larger when you rate yourself against "the average person" on one scale, and larger in European American than East Asian samples, especially on individualistic traits [3][6]. The **Lake Wobegone effect** (spelled *Lake Wobegon* in the literature and in Kruger's title) is a synonym row — Keillor's town where all the children are above average, borrowed into the literature by Kruger's title [5]. Its one distinct use is institutional rather than psychological: Cannell's late-1980s reports found essentially every US state claiming above-national-average scores on nationally normed school tests, an artifact of stale norms and teaching to the test [3] (gray literature; institutional gaming, not a bias in anyone's head).

**The forgotten half.** BTAE is not blanket self-flattery, and the file that says so is Kruger's own: the **below-average effect** (evidence: supported). Because "people focus egocentrically on their own skills and insufficiently take into account the skills of the comparison group," the same egocentrism "engenders the oft-documented above-average effect in domains in which absolute skills tend to be high but produces a reliable below-average effect in domains in which absolute skills tend to be low" [5]. Juggling, programming, chess: people place themselves *below* median regardless of actual standing, and cognitive load worsens both directions [5]. Moore & Healy's taxonomy is the umbrella that keeps this coherent — **overestimation** (of your absolute score), **overplacement** (of your rank), and **overprecision** (excessive certainty in your own judgments) are three different effects that dissociate, and people overplace on easy tasks while underplacing on hard ones [5]. Anyone who tells you "people always overrate themselves" is telling you half the finding.

**Calibrating yourself.** The **Dunning-Kruger effect** is this file's honesty exhibit, and the first job is stating what the 1999 paper actually found: bottom-quartile performers on humor, grammar, and logic tests scored around the 12th percentile and estimated themselves around the 62nd — above average, but still *below* what top performers claimed for themselves; top performers slightly *under*estimated their rank; and training that improved skill also improved self-assessment [1]. The authors' explanation was a dual burden: "their incompetence robs them of the metacognitive ability to realize it" [1]. Three things the popular version gets wrong. The pop gloss ("stupid people think they're geniuses") is not in the data [1][2]. The **"Mount Stupid" curve** — a confidence spike at zero experience, a valley of despair, a slope of enlightenment — appears nowhere in Kruger & Dunning; their figures are quartile plots of perceived versus actual percentile, both rising monotonically. That chart is internet folklore, drawn later, and it is the version most people arguing about "Dunning-Kruger" have in mind. And the mechanism (evidence: contested) is genuinely unresolved: Krueger & Mueller argued the pattern is regression to the mean plus a general better-than-average heuristic [2]; Nuhfer's random-number simulations reproduce the classic crossing-lines figure from pure noise, and with paired-measures methods most people at all skill levels self-assess reasonably and only a small minority are badly off [2]; Gignac & Zajenkowski tested self-assessed against measured intelligence in 929 people using the statistically appropriate tools (Glejser heteroscedasticity test, quadratic regression instead of quartile plots) and "failed to identify statistically significant heteroscedasticity, contrary to the Dunning-Kruger hypothesis," concluding the effect "may be much smaller than reported previously" [2]; Burson, Larrick & Klayman found that on genuinely hard tasks the pattern flips and the *best* performers are the most miscalibrated [2]; Jansen, Rafferty & Griffiths reproduced it from rational Bayesian updating on poor evidence [2]. Dunning's team defended the metacognitive account with incentive and field studies [1], the dispute ran on into *Intelligence* comment-and-reply in 2023 [2], and it is live in 2026 — Nuhfer & Fleisher's "Unskilled but Aware of It" continues the paired-measures line in *Numeracy* this year [2]. What survives every account, and is all an exploiter needs: **novices cannot audit their own competence, and their felt certainty carries almost no information.**

**The calibration curve.** The **hard-easy effect** (evidence: contested) — overconfidence on hard question sets, shrinking or reversing to underconfidence on easy ones [7] (note this runs opposite to the overplacement pattern above — that is Moore & Healy's point: overestimation and overplacement dissociate, moving in opposite directions with difficulty [5]) — is this file's clearest artifact casualty. Erev, Wallsten & Budescu showed random error alone produces overconfidence when you condition on confidence and underconfidence when you condition on accuracy, from the same data [7]; Gigerenzer, Hoffrage & Kleinbölting showed overconfidence largely disappears when items are sampled representatively instead of selected for trickiness [7]; and Juslin, Winman & Olsson found "very little support for a cognitive-processing bias in these data" and "near elimination of the hard-easy effect when there is control for scale-end effects and linear dependency" [7]. Keep the practical residue, drop the mechanism: your confidence scale does not stretch when the domain gets harder, so difficulty — not felt certainty — predicts where you are miscalibrated.

**Crediting yourself.** **Egocentric bias** (evidence: established) is the sturdiest workhorse here. Ross & Sicoly asked married couples, basketball players, and collaborators to apportion responsibility for joint outcomes; the summed self-claims reliably exceed 100% [8]. The decisive detail is that overclaiming extends to *negative* contributions — spouses also overclaim responsibility for starting arguments — which points at availability rather than ego: you were present for all of your own contributions and none of the invisible ones [8]. Overclaiming grows with group size, and "unpacking" (explicitly listing what each other person did) reduces it [8]. Two honest colleagues can both sincerely believe they carried the project. Note the boundary: egocentric bias is a memory/retrieval distortion; *self-serving attribution* (my successes are me, my failures are circumstances) is a motivated inference and lives in [[attribution-errors]]. Don't merge them.

**Controlling outcomes.** The **illusion of control** (evidence: supported, with moderators) is Langer's finding that chance situations carrying "skill cues" — choice, familiarity, active involvement, competition — inflate perceived success probability above what the odds warrant; people who chose their own lottery ticket demanded several times more money to sell it back than people handed one [9]. The meta-analytic picture is real but heterogeneous: for the 1996 Presson & Benassi review ("Illusion of control: A meta-analytic review") the title, authors, and year are confirmed, but volume/pages and full text are unverifiable (journal defunct, absent from Crossref/PubMed), so anchor on Stefan & David's 2013 meta — weighted mean effect D = .62, 95% CI [.49, .75], with the authors' own warning that "the large heterogeneity of the studies in terms of dependent measures suggests that the underlying phenomena can be of a different nature" [9]. The companion claim that depressed people see their lack of control accurately (depressive realism) has itself shrunk to a small, method-dependent effect and should not be leaned on [9].

**Forecasting your own restraint.** The **restraint bias** (evidence: supported — thin) is "a tendency for people to overestimate their capacity for impulse control," and the consequence chain is the point: "Inflated impulse-control beliefs led people to overexpose themselves to temptation, thereby promoting impulsive behavior," with recovering smokers holding more inflated beliefs exposing themselves to more temptation and relapsing more four months later [11]. Say the weaknesses plainly: four studies, one lab, one 2009 paper, no known large direct replication. And say what this is *not*: the KB does not endorse willpower-as-fuel — the ego-depletion model failed a 23-lab Registered Replication Report — and restraint bias is logically separable from it, being a claim about *miscalibrated belief*, not a depletable tank [11]. The sturdier neighbouring mechanism is Loewenstein's hot-cold empathy gap: a calm person cannot simulate the force of a future craving, so the confidence is generated by exactly the state that will be absent when it matters [11].

**Banking your own virtue.** The **moral credential effect** / **moral licensing** (evidence: contested) is Monin & Miller's finding that "people are more willing to express attitudes that could be viewed as prejudiced when their past behavior has established their credentials as nonprejudiced persons" [12]. Then the replication record: Blanken, van de Ven & Zeelenberg's meta of 91 studies and 7,397 participants estimates d = 0.31 and reports, from the meta's own authors, that "published studies tend to have larger moral licensing effects than unpublished studies" and that studies "require many more participants to draw solid conclusions" [13]; three high-powered direct replications of the flagship "sinning saints" paradigm found nothing [13]; the famous green-consumption-licenses-cheating result failed too [13]. The most informative recent work moderates rather than rescues it: across 115 experiments (N = 21,770), licensing was substantial when participants were observed (g = 0.65) and near zero when unobserved (g = 0.13), and after correcting for publication bias there was "moderate evidence for licensing when participants were observed (g = 0.51) but moderate evidence against licensing when unobserved (g = −0.01)" [13]. The honest reading: what survives is *reputational* — people cash moral credit in front of an audience. Which is precisely the manipulation-relevant half.

**Reporting yourself.** **Social desirability bias** (evidence: established as a measurement phenomenon) is the systematic prettying-up of self-report: over-reported voting and church attendance, under-reported drug use and stigmatized behavior, validated against records; reliable mode effects (people answer more honestly to a screen than to an interviewer); and the bogus-pipeline meta-analysis, where believing a lie detector works shifts reported attitudes toward less flattering answers — direct evidence of baseline misreporting [14]. Crowne & Marlowe's 33-item scale gave it a measure [14]; Paulhus's two-component model gave it the distinction this file cares about — **impression management** (deliberate, audience-tailored) versus **self-deceptive enhancement** (sincerely believed and still inflated) [14]. The second is the bridge to everything above: people's self-descriptions in conflicts are systematically rosy without anyone consciously lying. The contested part is the scales, not the phenomenon: "correcting" other measures for social-desirability scores does not improve validity, because high scorers are partly just genuinely conscientious and agreeable — the "red herring" finding [15]. A high lie-scale score is not proof anyone lied.

**Protecting your standing.** **Social comparison bias** (evidence: supported — thin) is Garcia, Song & Tesser's "tainted recommendations": evaluators recommend against candidates who would outshine them *on the evaluator's own signature dimension*, while happily championing strength on other dimensions [16]. It rests essentially on one five-page paper with no meta-analysis and no direct replication identified. The parent theory is much stronger — Tesser's self-evaluation maintenance model, where a close other's success in a self-relevant domain threatens self-evaluation, and Festinger's social comparison theory beneath it [16]. Honest structure: well-evidenced motive, thinly-evidenced gatekeeping effect.

## Evidence base

The file grade reflects the family core (better-than-average, egocentric overclaiming, socially desirable responding, illusion of control); members carry their own grades above. Sorted honestly:

- **Survived cleanly:** BTAE (modern meta, N > 950,000, explicit publication-bias analyses finding little bias) [3]; egocentric responsibility overclaiming (the sum-exceeds-100% method is arithmetic, replicated across couples, teams, and multi-author collaborations; no failed-replication literature located) [8]; socially desirable misreporting (six decades of survey methodology, record-validated, plus bogus-pipeline evidence) [14]; illusion of control across paradigms, with heterogeneity [9].
- **The flagship dispute:** Dunning-Kruger. The *descriptive pattern* — low scorers overplace themselves most on quartile plots — replicates broadly. Whether it reflects a distinct metacognitive deficit [1], regression plus better-than-average [2], task difficulty [2], or rational updating under noisy evidence [2] is unresolved as of 2026 [2]. Graded here as **phenomenon supported / mechanism contested**, with the pop version and the Mount Stupid curve explicitly disowned.
- **Largely artifact:** the hard-easy effect — scale-end effects, regression, and non-representative item selection account for most of it [7]. What survives is that miscalibration varies with difficulty, which Moore & Healy's framework recovers as a signature of inference under imperfect self-knowledge rather than a cognitive defect [5].
- **Publication-bias-flagged and partly failed:** moral licensing — small pooled d, pub-bias signal inside its own meta, failed high-powered direct replications of two flagship paradigms, and a moderator analysis suggesting the intrapsychic version is near zero [13].
- **Thin, one lab or one paper:** restraint bias [11] and social comparison bias [16]. Both are carried at low weight; both have sturdier parent literatures doing the real work (hot-cold empathy gap; self-evaluation maintenance).
- **Two artifact families recur here** and are worth naming once rather than per-entry: (a) regression, measurement noise, and item selection (Dunning-Kruger quartile plots, hard-easy, the Nuhfer simulations, Erev et al.) [2][7]; (b) small-N publication bias (moral licensing, restraint bias, social comparison bias) [11][13][16]. When a "well-known bias" in this family shrinks, one of those two is usually why.
- **Cultural boundary:** self-enhancement is weaker and sometimes absent in East Asian samples, a moderator confirmed in the BTAE meta and the subject of a long-running dispute over whether self-enhancement is universal on culturally valued traits or culturally bounded [3][6]. These are WEIRD-heavy magnitudes; do not export them as human constants.

## How it's weaponized

The recurring exploit: **the operator ratifies the self-image you already hold, and collects on the verification you then skip.**

- **"You're too smart to be scammed"** — the move this file sees most often, because it converts your competence into the reason not to check ([[flattery-ego]] supplies the mechanics of the compliment; [[why-smart-people-fall]] owns the profile). The record: overconfidence in one's own financial knowledge is associated with fraud victimization, and it pairs badly with declining cognition, where confidence outlives competence [17]. The sharp nuance that keeps this honest — *objective* financial literacy did **not** significantly predict victimization in a national longitudinal sample [17]. The exploitable variable is the **confidence–knowledge gap**, not ignorance. Which is why the savvy get worked: they are cheaper marks, because they skip steps ([[optimism-overconfidence]] carries the target-side risk data).
- **Flattery-then-request sequencing** — con scripts open by ratifying an above-average self-view before any ask: "most people wouldn't get this, but you would"; "you're not like the others"; "you're a great judge of character." Romance fraud runs a documented idealization phase before the money [17], and the modern framing of scams as psychological operations targeting the lure mechanisms rather than the facts is the right one [17] ([[love-bombing]], [[romance-scam-arc]], [[charm-offensive]]). The tell is sequencing: the praise concerns your *judgment*, and it arrives immediately before you are asked to exercise it in their favor.
- **Skill-framing chance products** — the illusion of control is the most cleanly documented exploitation in this cluster because the exploitation is a *design specification*. Near-misses are engineered: they were "experienced as less pleasant, but increased desire to play," specifically when the player had personal control over the gamble, recruited win-related striatal and insula circuitry, and are interpreted by the gambler "as evidence that he has acquired skill at the game" [10]. Pick your own numbers, stop the reels, choose your table: skill cues injected into pure chance. The UK Gambling Commission's 2021 online-slots package banned features that speed play or manufacture a feeling of agency, including turbo/quick-spin and reel-stopping (UK jurisdiction) [10]. Same shelf, less regulated: gamified trading apps, crypto dashboards dense with levers, and pig-butchering platforms that manufacture early "wins" so the mark experiences a developing skill before the harvest ([[hope-greed]], [[ponzi-psychology]]).
- **Competence flattery as a sales channel** — "be your own boss," "you're exactly the kind of person who succeeds at this," "I only reach out to people with your drive": the [[mlm-recruitment-scripts]] opening is BTAE ratification attached to an inventory invoice, and the downstream attribution ("anyone can do this, so failure is you") is set up in the same breath ([[mlm-lifecycle]]). Timeshare and high-pressure floors run the same ratification with a shorter clock ([[timeshare-playbook]]).
- **Engineered difficulty** — when a decision is moved into unfamiliar complex territory (exotic structures, crypto mechanics, contract language), the gap between how confident you feel and how right you are widens while your felt certainty does not move. The tell: complexity arrives and a fast decision is still expected. *Flagged as inference:* the bias is documented, the deliberate use of it is the KB's synthesis, not a documented playbook [7].
- **Credit fog** — because everyone honestly overclaims, deliberate credit-taking is nearly unadjudicable from memory, and the bias itself becomes the shield: "we all think we did more than we did — you're doing it too" ([[credit-theft-visibility]]). The same machinery has a darker use: egocentric encoding means a victim can genuinely retrieve their own contributions to any conflict, which is raw material for reversal — exposure to [[darvo]] during a confrontation is associated with increased self-blame in the person confronting, and self-blame is how silence is enforced [18]. The counter is unusually clean: people educated about the DARVO pattern rate the perpetrator as less believable [18].
- **Moral-credential hooks** — "you've done your research, so you're covered"; the donation-then-ask sequence; the loyalty-then-favor sequence; and the résumé-in-place-of-an-answer ("look at everything I've done for this family / this church / you") when a specific allegation lands ([[guilt-tripping]], [[non-apology-patterns]], [[spiritual-abuse]]). Keep the two claims separate, because the evidence differs: the *intrapsychic* licensing effect is contested and may be near zero unobserved [13], while the *observer-side* move — banking advertised good deeds so that later accusations get discounted, in organizations as ethics-washing and diversity theater — is ordinary reputation management and doesn't depend on the lab effect at all. That the surviving licensing effect is the *observed* one is exactly what you'd expect of a move aimed at an audience [13].
- **Virtue extraction** — social desirability is a compliance lever whenever the ask is staged for an audience: solicited on the doorstep with a neighbour watching, on camera, in the meeting, in front of the team, where refusing means publicly confessing to being cheap or disloyal. Push-polls and loaded survey items do it in writing — only one answer sounds like a decent person, and the "agreement" is later cited as endorsement ([[dark-patterns-social-urgency]], [[fake-reviews-astroturfing]]). The same machinery protects perpetrators after the fact: fraud victims stay silent rather than admit they were fooled, which enables re-targeting [17] ([[elder-targeting]], [[shame-leverage]]).
- **"You can handle it" exposure engineering** — anyone whose interests are served by your relapse or overcommitment can work the restraint bias: "one won't hurt you, you're disciplined"; reactivation vouchers mailed to lapsed gamblers; "prove you're over it." The mechanism is verified from the victim side (inflated belief → voluntary exposure → relapse) [11]; the deliberate use is inference and is written as recognition, not as a documented perpetrator playbook.
- **Gatekeeping you can't see** — an evaluator strong on your dimension may quietly filter you out to protect their standing; watch for someone describing you to an evaluator as a threat to that evaluator's standing. Evidence-thin [16], so it belongs in your hypothesis list, never in your conclusion — the more probable readings are ordinary complementary hiring and ordinary rejection.
- **The label as a weapon** — "that's just Dunning-Kruger" is deployed as a status move to dismiss an outsider without engaging the argument: your standing to speak is attacked, your claim never is, and the accuser silently exempts themselves from an effect that, where real, is universal. It is a double manipulation — an ad hominem *and* a misstatement of the science [2] ([[bad-faith-argumentation]], [[epistemic-guardrails]]).

## Ethical use vs exploitation

Building someone's confidence is a legitimate and often generous act: coaches, teachers, therapists, and good managers work on self-efficacy for a living, honest praise is one of the better things people do for each other, and an accurate "you're good at this" is information ([[flattery-ego]] carries the *earned-praise* contrast). The line ([[manipulation-vs-influence]]): exploitation **ratifies a self-image in order to suppress a check** (praise timed to a decision, "someone like you doesn't need to read that"), **manufactures skill cues in outcomes you cannot influence** (choice, stop buttons, near-misses in a chance game), **sells competence back to you** (flattery attached to an invoice, an inventory order, or a wire), **stages the ask for an audience** so refusing costs you face, or **turns your own honest memory and virtue into arguments against you** ("everyone thinks they did more"; "after everything I've done"). The disclosure test: an honest counterparty survives "I'm going to verify this with someone who has nothing to gain," "show me the track record, not the reasoning," and "can I decide this tomorrow, in private?" A flatterer who becomes irritated at your basic questions has told you what the flattery was for.

## Recognition

- **The compliment that arrives just before the decision** — "you clearly know more than most people about this"; "you're not like the others"; "I can tell you do your own thinking." Praise about your *judgment*, delivered in the last minute before you exercise it in their favor, is a working part, not a nicety.
- **"I'd never fall for that"** — the belief itself is the exposure. So is its cousin, "I'm a good judge of character," which is a comparative claim you almost certainly hold without ever having sampled how well others do. If you can't name the evidence behind "better than most," it isn't an observation.
- **"I've done my research"** — said as a completed credential rather than a description of a method. Ask which specific claim you checked, against which independent source, and what result would have made you walk away. Self-directed "research" that only confirms is covered by [[confirmation-bias]]; the piece this file owns is the felt *sufficiency* of it.
- **"I can quit anytime" / "I can have just one" / "keeping it in the house is fine — I have willpower"** — confidence about future restraint, generated while calm, about a state you cannot currently simulate. Notice who benefits from you testing it, and whether someone is praising your self-control while arranging your proximity to the thing.
- **"I have a system" / "I can feel when it's about to hit" / "let me pick the numbers"** — control talk about outcomes you don't control, plus an interface that keeps handing you meaningless choices. Also: winning streaks in chance games experienced as a skill developing.
- **"I do everything around here" / "that was mostly my work"** — your own contributions are vivid and enumerable; theirs are a vague summary. The asymmetry in retrieval vividness *is* the bias operating, in you and in them simultaneously. Two honest people, one impossible argument.
- **"After everything I've done for you" / "I'm one of the good guys"** — a moral résumé recited in answer to a specific allegation, usually with someone watching who can be expected to credit it.
- **The reputation trap** — you're asked in front of others, on camera, with a name attached, and the honest answer would make you look cheap, disloyal, unspiritual, or unfun. Notice that the audience was arranged.
- **Felt-sense indicators:** the absence of doubt where you have no track record (incompetence in a domain doesn't feel like anything — it feels like competence); irritation at your own basic questions, or at someone else's; a warm click when someone describes you as you'd like to be described, followed by a reluctance to check them; certainty that stayed exactly as strong when the subject matter got much harder; relief when a strong rival "isn't a good fit."

## Resistance

1. **Substitute a process for the self-image** ([[verification-rituals]]): the belief "I'm too sharp to be fooled" cannot be improved, only replaced — with an independent second opinion, a callback on a number you sourced yourself, a waiting period, pre-committed before any charged pitch. Treat "someone like you doesn't need to check" as a red flag *in itself*, regardless of how competent you actually are.
2. **Score yourself instead of feeling yourself** ([[detection-heuristics]]): felt confidence carries almost no information in domains where you have no scored track record. Write predictions down with dates and check them later; prefer people who can show outcomes over people who project conviction. This is also how you tell *justified-confidence* from its imitation — in yourself and in the person in front of you.
3. **Run the plain-language restatement test**: if you cannot restate the deal, the diagnosis, or the mechanism in one sentence a friend would understand, your confidence is about the situation being *familiar-feeling*, not about it being understood. Answers that respond to your questions with more complexity rather than less are answering a different question.
4. **Ask the robot question** for anything chance-based: would the outcome distribution change if a machine played instead of you? If not, every choice, stop button, ritual, and "practice mode" is a cost dressed as agency — and its presence is a disclosure about the product [10].
5. **Choose structure over willpower**: distance beats restraint. Pre-commitment, stimulus control, and not testing yourself are what the relapse-prevention literature converged on independently, and the bias evidence points the same way [11]. Anyone urging exposure *as a compliment* to your self-control is not acting in your interest.
6. **Unpack the other side before you allocate credit** [8]: list what each other person actually did before deciding what share was yours — the one debiasing move with direct evidence behind it. Then stop litigating memory: contemporaneous dated artifacts settle what two honest recollections cannot ([[documentation-practices]]).
7. **Judge acts, not accounts**: a long good record is real evidence about a person's character and no evidence at all about a specific allegation. "Look at everything I've done" is a change of subject; the question was what happened on Tuesday.
8. **Move the decision out of the room** ([[boundary-scripts]]): "I don't decide anything on the spot" and "I'll email you tomorrow" remove the audience that social desirability needs. Anything that has to be decided in front of witnesses is being decided by the witnesses.
9. **Aggregate, then re-decide from zero** ([[manipulation-audit]]): individually, each flattering moment is pleasant and each skipped check is reasonable. Written down in one place — every compliment that preceded an ask, every verification you waived, every rule you relaxed — they read as a pattern, and the question becomes "knowing all of this, would I start this today?"

## Caveats

- **Confidence is frequently justified** (*justified-confidence*). Calibrated experts exist; weather forecasters and bridge players are textbook cases; in feedback-rich home domains people are often well calibrated [7]. A confident person is not a red flag. The discriminators are a scored track record, willingness to name what would change their mind, and hedging where the domain demands it — not tone, not humility, not hesitation.
- **About half of people genuinely are above the median.** BTAE is visible in aggregates, not in any individual's self-report; any single "I'm better than most at this" has roughly coin-flip base-rate odds of being true, and someone accurately reporting a real skill gap ("I'm the stronger programmer of us two") may simply be right [3]. Never use this file to dismiss a specific person's self-assessment — including your own.
- **"Dunning-Kruger" as an internet insult is concept creep, and it is almost always mis-stated.** The pop version is not in the paper, the Mount Stupid curve is not in the paper, and the effect — where it is real — applies to everyone, including whoever is invoking it. Dunning's own corrective is "the effect is about us, not them" [2]. Naming a bias at a person is a status move, not an argument: the reply is "which part is wrong?" ([[epistemic-guardrails]] governs; this is the same failure mode as "you're just being paranoid").
- **Positive illusions are a live debate, not a settled harm.** Taylor & Brown argued that mildly inflated self-views, optimism, and exaggerated control beliefs are characteristic of *good* mental health; Colvin and Block replied that the evidence is weak and that self-enhancers get rated worse by observers over time [4]. The moderate reading is what the data support: BTAE correlates with self-esteem (r = .34) and life satisfaction (r = .33) [3], moderate control beliefs travel with persistence and wellbeing, and depressive realism is a small method-dependent effect that does not establish that sadder is wiser [9]. This file is not "think less of yourself."
- **Divergence is the base case, and it does not license splitting the difference.** Egocentric bias predicts both parties overclaim *somewhat* [8] — so a 60/40 dispute between honest people is normal and is not evidence of theft or [[gaslighting]]. But contributions in real households and real teams are often genuinely unequal, and the aggrieved party is frequently just correct. The bias explains why memory can't settle it; a time log can.
- **This file is quotable as a put-down, and that misuse is itself a move.** "Everyone thinks they do more — you're biased"; "that's just your illusory superiority"; "you always overestimate your contribution." Aimed at someone with a documented pattern, that is reality-denial wearing a citation. The severity rules in [[epistemic-guardrails]] apply, and the counter is the same either way: records, not a memory contest.
- **Self-esteem is not the disease and humility-performance is not the cure.** Performed self-deprecation is an impression-management behavior in its own right [14], self-deprecating people are not thereby better calibrated (in hard domains everyone underrates themselves [5]), and shrinking your self-estimate does nothing to close the confidence–knowledge gap that fraud actually exploits [17]. Calibration is a measurement practice, not a personality.
- **The thin entries are thin.** Restraint bias is one lab [11]; social comparison bias is one short paper [16]; moral licensing may be near zero without an audience [13]. Use them as vocabulary and hypotheses, not as findings — and note that "my boss blocked me because I outshine them" is itself a self-flattering attribution that this very file predicts. Complementary hiring is usually just correct team design; the claim needs a cross-decision pattern, and the KB's own concepts cut both ways.
- **Good deeds do not generally cause bad ones.** Moral consistency effects — past moral behavior predicting *more* moral behavior — are at least as well documented as licensing, and the field cannot reliably predict which occurs: Mullen & Monin's review organizes the moderators into five themes and still concludes that evidence for consistency and licensing *in the same study* was "nearly nonexistent," with many designs lacking the baseline condition needed to tell whether licensing happened at all [19]. Citing a track record is ordinary reputation reasoning; the flag is offering it *instead of* engaging the specific claim.
- **A high "virtue" self-report proves nothing in either direction.** Politeness, privacy, and tact produce the same shading as impression management, declining to disclose to a stranger is healthy, and social-desirability scale scores partly reflect people who genuinely behave well — which is why "correcting" for them fails [15]. Never treat a lie-scale score, or a too-clean self-description, as proof of deception.
- **Magnitudes are WEIRD-heavy.** Self-enhancement is weaker or absent in East Asian samples and the universality question is actively disputed [3][6]; exporting these effect sizes across cultures is unwarranted, and reading a culturally normal self-presentation as either arrogance or false modesty is a predictable error.
- **Seams.** [[optimism-overconfidence]] owns the target-side risk profile — "it won't happen to me," the invulnerability illusion, the bias blind spot and third-person effect, and the fraud-outcome data; this file owns the self-evaluation machinery those beliefs run on. [[attribution-errors]] owns self-serving attribution and cause-assignment (distinct from egocentric availability). [[mind-reading-illusions]] owns your beliefs about *other* people's minds and what they can see of yours. [[confirmation-bias]] owns favoring belief-consistent evidence; [[pattern-illusions]] owns structure seen in randomness (the gambler's-fallacy half of the casino floor lives there, this file has the skill-cue half); [[probability-blindspots]] owns the arithmetic. [[bias-codex-index]] maps the rest of the codex onto their homes.
