## Sources cited in tactics/reactive-abuse-provocation.md

1. Reactive-abuse pattern and the contested term: DV-practitioner and clinical commentary (e.g., GoodRx, Charlie Health, Thriveworks, DomesticShelters' "Ask Amanda") — consistently describe the provoke→react→weaponize dynamic, tie it to DARVO, and note "reactive abuse" is *not* a clinical/legal term, with many sources arguing the victim's reaction is self-defense and the term itself can be victim-blaming. Folk/clinical (practitioner consensus on the pattern; the label is contested). DARVO: Freyd, J. J. (coinage); Harsey, S. J., & Freyd, J. J. (2020), "Deny, Attack, and Reverse Victim and Offender (DARVO): What Is the Influence on Perceived Perpetrator and Victim Credibility?" *Journal of Aggression, Maltreatment & Trauma* — experimental evidence that DARVO shifts perceived credibility (the peer-reviewed anchor for the reversal).
2. Johnson, M. P. (2008). *A Typology of Domestic Violence* — situational couple violence (conflict without a control campaign) vs. intimate terrorism (coercive-controlling violence); the basis for distinguishing a provoked victim's reaction from genuinely mutual situational violence. Supported. See *situational couple violence*.
3. Predominant aggressor / dual arrest (US, jurisdiction-labeled): predominant-aggressor statutes (~majority of jurisdictions) direct police to identify the person posing the ongoing threat rather than arresting whoever reacted or both parties; studies (e.g., 2016/2017) find primary-aggressor laws reduce wrongful dual arrests (BWJP and peer-reviewed analyses). Supported; US-specific.
4. Counters: gray-rock / disengagement (Cleveland Clinic; Medical News Today; with the documented caveat that it can escalate danger and isn't a long-term solution). Practitioner.
