# Bad-Faith Argumentation

> Arguing to win, exhaust, or derail — not to reach the truth. The form of debate is preserved (questions, demands for evidence, logical-sounding moves) while its cooperative purpose is abandoned. The tell is never a single message; it's the pattern: positions that shift when pressed, questions already answered, an appetite for your effort that's never reciprocated.

## Definition

Bad-faith argumentation is **a family of rhetorical tactics that simulate honest reasoning while covertly abandoning its goal — to derail, exhaust, dominate, or score rather than to understand or resolve**. The grounding contrast is Grice's **Cooperative Principle**: ordinary conversation assumes participants are cooperating toward mutual understanding, observing maxims of quality (be truthful), quantity, relevance, and manner [1]. Bad-faith argumentation *systematically and covertly flouts* these while pretending to honor them; in pragma-dialectical terms, fallacies are violations of the rules for critical discussion [1]. The family includes several documented moves: the **motte-and-bailey** (Shackel's "Motte and Bailey *Doctrine*" — advancing a desirable-but-indefensible claim, the "bailey," then retreating to a modest-defensible one, the "motte," when challenged, and re-occupying the bailey once the attacker withdraws) [2]; the **Gish gallop** (burying an opponent in more claims than can possibly be rebutted in the time available — [[cognitive-overload-confusion]]) [3]; **sealioning** (persistent, ostensibly-polite demands for evidence and debate that function as harassment — coined in a 2014 webcomic, likened to a denial-of-service attack on a person) [4]; the **strawman family** (straw: distorting the opponent's actual argument; **weak man**: attacking the weakest real version as if representative; **hollow man**: attacking a fabricated opponent nobody holds) [5]; and **whataboutism / tu quoque** (deflecting a criticism by pointing at the critic's alleged hypocrisy instead of answering it) [6]. **Critically — and this is the file's core caveat — the *accusation* of bad faith is itself weaponizable**: "you're just sealioning" / "you're arguing in bad faith" is a standard way to dismiss legitimate questions and honest disagreement, and the line cannot be read from any single message — only from the pattern [4][7].

## Variants & aliases

- **Motte-and-bailey** — defend the controversial claim by swapping in a trivially-true one when challenged, then quietly switch back [2] (e.g., asserting a strong, contestable thesis, and when pressed retreating to "I'm just saying words have meanings").
- **Gish gallop** — a flood of weak claims faster than anyone can answer; the *unrebutted* points are then claimed as wins [3] (debate and online register; [[cognitive-overload-confusion]], [[disinformation-playbooks]]'s firehose).
- **Sealioning** — relentless, faux-civil "just asking you to back that up" demands that consume the target's time and energy indefinitely; civility as cover for harassment [4]. Overlaps **"JAQing off"** (framing assertions/accusations as "just asking questions") and concern trolling.
- **Strawman / weak man / hollow man** — misrepresenting, cherry-picking the weakest, or fabricating the opponent's position to knock it down [5].
- **Whataboutism / tu quoque** — "what about *your* side's failings?" deflecting the substance by attacking the questioner; the Cold-War-era variant of an ancient fallacy [6].
- **Goalpost-shifting in argument** — conceding nothing because the standard of proof rises every time it's met (the argumentative face of [[moving-goalposts]]).

## How it works

Two levers. **Cognitive-overload-confusion** ([[cognitive-overload-confusion]]): several of these tactics win by *exhaustion and volume* rather than merit — the Gish gallop and sealioning impose an asymmetric workload (it takes far longer to refute a claim than to assert one, the "bullshit asymmetry"), so the target loses by attrition, runs out of time, or gives up, and silence is then framed as defeat. **Framing-anchoring** ([[framing-anchoring]]): motte-and-bailey, strawman, and whataboutism win by *controlling what's being argued about* — substituting a different (easier or more damaging) proposition for the real one, so the opponent is forced to fight on rigged ground. Both depend on a third, enabling factor: the target's **good-faith presumption** — we're socially primed to assume our interlocutor is cooperating (Grice [1]), so we keep answering the questions, steelmanning the position, and extending charity long after the other side has stopped reciprocating. The tactics exploit the gap between the *appearance* of reasoning and its *abandoned purpose*.

## Recognition

### Behavioral markers

- **Position shifts under pressure, then reverts** [2]: the claim becomes modest and defensible exactly when challenged, and ambitious again once you move on — you can never pin it down.
- **The workload is all yours**: endless demands for evidence, sources, and re-explanation, with nothing offered back and no evidence that would ever change their mind (the sealioning signature) [4].
- **Volume over substance** [3]: a rapid pile of assertions such that answering one means conceding ten; the *unanswered* ones get claimed as victories.
- **Your argument keeps getting replaced**: you're suddenly defending a position you didn't take (strawman) or the conversation lurches to the other side's grievances (whataboutism) [5][6].
- **No update, ever**: answered questions return unchanged; refuted points reappear; the standard of proof rises each time you meet it. The *pattern*, not any one move, is the tell.

### Typical phrases

"I'm just asking questions." · "Can you cite a source for that? … and that? … and that?" (endless) · "So what you're *really* saying is [distortion]." · "But what about [unrelated thing your side did]?" · "I never said *that* — I only said [retreat to the motte]." · "You can't even answer one simple question." (after a flood of them) · "If you can't explain it to me right now, you must not have a point."

### Felt-sense indicators

- Exhaustion and a sense of being *trapped in* a conversation that's going nowhere by design ([[circular-conversation]] feel).
- The frustration of "that's not what I said" — repeatedly having to reclaim your own position.
- Sensing the other person isn't *listening to update* but *waiting to deploy* the next move.
- The creeping realization that no possible answer would satisfy them — the goalposts move every time.

### Escalation signs

The "debate" expanding to consume unbounded time and energy (sealioning as harassment, sometimes coordinated by multiple people — [[proxy-recruitment]]); bad-faith argument fused with [[gaslighting]] ("I never said that," when they did) or [[circular-conversation]] (the loop that never resolves); in public/political registers, scaling into [[disinformation-playbooks]] (firehose of falsehood — volume and contradiction as strategy). When the goal is visibly to wear you down rather than resolve anything, disengagement — not better arguments — is the move.

## Where it appears

Online and social-media discourse (the native habitat of sealioning, gish gallop, and JAQ — [[digital-platforms]]); politics and propaganda (whataboutism, motte-and-bailey, the firehose — [[propaganda-politics]], [[disinformation-playbooks]]); business and competitive contexts (bad-faith negotiation, FUD, strawmanning rivals — [[competitors-business]]); and intimate relationships and families, where "winning" the argument displaces resolving the issue ([[circular-conversation]], [[moving-goalposts]]). It thrives wherever the *appearance* of reasoned debate confers legitimacy and the audience can't easily audit good faith.

## Typical sequences & co-occurrence

A toolkit that overlaps the other "you-can-never-resolve-it" tactics: it pairs with [[circular-conversation]] (the unresolvable loop) and [[moving-goalposts]] (the rising bar) — both declared here — and supplies the argumentative engine behind [[thought-terminating-cliches]] (the slogan that ends thought) and propaganda's [[disinformation-playbooks]] (firehose/flooding). It exploits [[cognitive-overload-confusion]] (volume, exhaustion) and [[framing-anchoring]] (substituting the proposition), and frequently rides alongside [[gaslighting]] and [[darvo]] when the bad-faith arguing is also rewriting what was said.

## Counter-strategies

### In the moment

- **Refuse the asymmetric workload** ([[jade-avoidance]]): you are not obligated to Justify, Argue, Defend, or Explain on demand. "I've answered that; I'm not going to keep re-litigating it" is a complete response to sealioning — the tactic *needs* your continued engagement, so withdrawing it is the counter.
- **Pin the position** ([[detection-heuristics]]): against motte-and-bailey, make them commit — "Which claim are you defending: the strong one or the modest one? Because they're different." Naming the swap disarms it [2].
- **Don't chase the gallop or the deflection**: against a flood, pick the single most load-bearing claim and refuse to move on ("we're not proceeding until we settle this one"); against whataboutism, "that may be worth discussing separately, but it doesn't answer the question" [3][6]. Reclaim your actual argument when it's strawmanned: "that's not what I said — here's what I said."

### Structural / long-term

- **Distinguish the forum from the relationship**: in public/online settings, the audience — not the bad-faith arguer — is who matters; a brief correction for onlookers, then disengagement, beats an endless one-on-one. In close relationships, name the *pattern* ("we're not actually trying to solve this, we're trying to win") rather than playing another round.
- **Set time and scope limits in advance**: decide what you'll engage with and for how long, so the attrition strategy can't run indefinitely ([[circular-conversation]]'s exit discipline).
- **Hold to good-faith standards yourself**: the durable defense is modeling and expecting the cooperative norms [1] — willingness to update, steelmanning, conceding good points — and noticing, over repeated interactions, who reciprocates and who only ever takes.

## Caveats & false positives

### This section is core — the accusation is as weaponizable as the tactic

- **"You're sealioning / arguing in bad faith" is itself a silencing move** [4][7]: the single biggest false-positive risk. Labeling someone a bad-faith arguer is a standard way to **dismiss legitimate questions, honest disagreement, and inconvenient evidence** without engaging them — to exit a debate you're losing while claiming the high ground. The accusation carries the *same* burden the tactic does, and (as one critic put it) sometimes the persistent questioner is simply *right*. Be at least as suspicious of the accusation as of the behavior.
- **Genuine inquiry** ([[genuine-inquiry]]): sincere questions — even persistent, even naive, even inconvenient ones — aimed at understanding are *good*, and responsive to answers. Persistence alone is not sealioning; the difference is whether the questioner *updates* when answered. Don't pathologize curiosity or skepticism.
- **Honest disagreement** ([[honest-disagreement]]): people can sincerely hold a different view, argue hard for it, and still be in good faith. Strength of conviction, repetition, or refusing to concede a point you find obvious is not bad faith — it may just be disagreement.
- **Non-fallacious cousins** [5]: pointing out a real inconsistency in someone's position (not pure tu quoque deflection), refining a claim when legitimately challenged (not a motte-and-bailey bait-and-switch), or asking for a source on an extraordinary assertion (not sealioning) are all *legitimate*. The strawman/weak-man/hollow-man distinctions exist precisely because some criticisms of weak arguments are fair.
- **The line is the pattern, not the message** [7]: no single question, request for evidence, or hard-fought point can be diagnosed as bad faith. Only the pattern — positions that shift and revert, questions answered yet re-asked, a standard of proof that rises whenever it's met, effort demanded but never reciprocated — distinguishes bad faith from vigorous good-faith argument.

### Base rates & severity calibration

Most argument is good-faith-but-heated, not strategically bad-faith; people get repetitive, defensive, and over-attached to positions without any intent to derail. The tactic-grade signature: **the cooperative goal is abandoned (winning/exhausting/derailing, not resolving) + the move recurs as a pattern (shift-and-revert, unanswerable volume, never updating) + the workload is asymmetric and unreciprocated** ([[epistemic-guardrails]]; one persistent questioner is rung-1 evidence of a persistent person, not a sea lion). Severity is `medium`: interpersonally it's exhausting and corrosive to resolution; at scale (political/online disinformation) it degrades public discourse — but the over-detection cost is high, because branding good-faith dissent "bad faith" is itself a potent way to shut down legitimate challenge.

## Evidence & debates

Graded `supported`: the family rests on solid argumentation theory and pragmatics — Grice's Cooperative Principle and pragma-dialectics' rules for critical discussion [1] (established), the strawman/weak-man/hollow-man taxonomy [5] (peer-reviewed), and tu quoque as a standard fallacy [6] (established logic). The newer coinages are well-documented but informal: motte-and-bailey is a philosopher's term [2] (Shackel's original is "Doctrine"; the common "motte-and-bailey *fallacy*" is a later popularization, largely via Scott Alexander/LessWrong — worth flagging so it isn't misattributed); "Gish gallop" [3] and "sealioning" [4] are apt folk coinages (a creationism-debate term and a 2014 webcomic, respectively) rather than formal literature; "JAQing off" is folk. The genuine and important debate is the reversal [7]: because good faith is an *internal state* not directly observable from any single utterance, the accusation of bad faith is unusually easy to weaponize against honest interlocutors — which is why this file's diagnostic is strictly pattern-based and its caveat is treated as core, not appended.
