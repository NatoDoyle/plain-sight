# Bad-Faith Negotiation Gambits

> A cluster of **deceptive deal-table tactics** designed to extract concessions by manipulation rather than reach a fair agreement: *good cop/bad cop*, the fake *higher authority*, the *nibble*, *exploding deadlines*, *lowball/highball* anchoring, the *bogey*, the *snow job*, and manufactured *deadlock*. The line that matters: **hard bargaining is legitimate; bad faith is not.** Anchoring high, conceding slowly, and bluffing about your *reservation price* are normal and ethical. The crossing point is narrow and specific: **lying about material facts, fabricating deadlines or authority, and tactics aimed at exploiting rather than settling** [1][3].

## What it is

The manipulative subset of negotiation tactics — distinct from tough-but-honest bargaining. The research anchor is **Lewicki's SINS scale** (*Self-reported Inappropriate Negotiation Strategies*; Robinson, Lewicki & Donahue, 2000), which empirically separates five factors of bargaining conduct — and rates ordinary **"traditional competitive bargaining" (hiding your bottom line, inflated openings) as the *most acceptable***, while **misrepresentation, false promises, attacking the opponent's network, and inappropriate information-gathering** are the genuinely unethical clusters [2]. This file catalogs the named gambits so a target can recognize them — not run them.

## The named gambits

Well-documented in the negotiation literature (Lewicki, Saunders & Barry; Harvard PON; Dawson; Cohen) [1][2]:

- **Good cop / bad cop** — one party is harsh/unreasonable, the other "reasonable," so you gratefully concede to the good cop (a [[reciprocity]] / contrast play).
- **Higher (limited) authority** — "I'd love to, but my manager/committee/finance won't approve" — a fictional or strategically-absent decision-maker used to stonewall and reset your expectations. Signature of car/retail closes; overlaps [[four-square-payment-packing]].
- **The nibble** — a small extra concession requested *after* you've mentally committed and the deal feels done ("just throw in the floor mats / one more revision"), when refusing feels petty against the near-complete agreement.
- **Lowball / highball** — an extreme opening anchor meant to drag the whole range your way (see [[lowballing]]).
- **The bogey** — they treat a trivial issue as critical, then "reluctantly" surrender it for a real concession from you (a manufactured trade).
- **The snow job** — burying you in detail, jargon, or documents so the real terms get lost ([[cognitive-overload-confusion]]).
- **Exploding / false deadlines & take-it-or-leave-it** — fabricated time pressure ("this price is only good today"; [[manufactured-urgency]]) and brinkmanship.
- **Deliberate deadlock / walkout** — staged impasse or storming-out to panic you into moving.

## How it works

- **[[framing-anchoring]]** — lowball/highball; the opening number drags your sense of "fair" (the strongest, best-evidenced mapping).
- **[[scarcity-urgency]]** — fabricated/exploding deadlines and take-it-or-leave-it pressure.
- **[[commitment-consistency]]** — the nibble: once you've committed, a small add-on feels trivial against the nearly-closed deal.
- **[[reciprocity]]** — concession games and good-cop/bad-cop: a "concession" obligates a return concession.
- **[[cognitive-overload-confusion]]** — the snow job buries the real terms.
- **[[authority]]** — the higher-authority gambit (note: this is absent-authority used *as leverage/constraint*, not Cialdini-style deference — a looser fit, included for recognition).

## Recognition

**Behavioral markers**
- A "decision-maker" who is never in the room, and whose limits only ever move *against* you.
- New asks appearing **after** you thought you had a deal (the nibble).
- Sudden, unverifiable deadlines and "today only" pricing.
- Wild opening numbers far outside any defensible range.
- One hostile party + one friendly party tag-teaming you.
- Avalanche of paperwork/jargon right before signing.

**Typical phrasing**
- "Personally I'd say yes, but my manager will never go for that."
- "We've got a deal — oh, just one small thing before we sign…"
- "This price is only good if you commit right now."
- "That's my final offer, take it or leave it."
- "Let me see what I can do for you" (then a staged disappearance to "the manager").

**Felt-sense indicators**
- A rush, a fog, or a feeling of being **worn down** rather than persuaded.
- Relief-driven agreement to the "reasonable" party just to escape the harsh one.
- Reluctance to "make a fuss" over a late add-on, even though it irritates you.
- The sense the goalposts keep moving and you can never quite close.

**Escalation signs**
- Bluffing about price/intent (legitimate) hardening into **lies about material facts** — fake competing offers, invented specs, fabricated approvals — which crosses into fraud (see Caveats).

## Counter-strategies

- **Know your [[boundary-scripts|BATNA]]** — your best alternative to a negotiated agreement. If you know your walk-away, no anchor or deadline can define your options for you (Fisher & Ury) [4].
- **Name the tactic, neutrally.** "Is that a real deadline, or a negotiating position?" "Let's both bring our decision-makers" calls the higher-authority bluff. Naming a gambit usually defuses it ([[detection-heuristics]]).
- **Refuse to renegotiate after agreement.** The nibble dies if "we had a deal" is a firm, calm boundary ([[boundary-scripts]]).
- **Slow down.** Decline to be rushed; "I don't decide under deadline pressure — I'll need until X" ([[manufactured-urgency]] counters).
- **Verify material claims in writing** ([[verification-rituals]]) — competing bids, specs, "approvals." Bluffing on price is fair; a *factual* claim should survive being put in the contract.
- **Separate the people from the problem; insist on objective criteria** (Fisher & Ury) — anchor on market data, not on their number [4].

## Caveats & false positives

**Hard bargaining is legitimate — do not pathologize a tough counterpart.**

- **[[hard-bargaining]] is the innocent twin.** Anchoring high, conceding slowly, a credible walkaway, silence, and **bluffing about your reservation price / settlement intentions** are normal, ethical, and (per the SINS scale) the *most accepted* tactics [2]. Toughness is not manipulation.
- **[[puffery]] and price-bluffing are legally tolerated.** Under **ABA Model Rule 4.1 cmt. [2]**, estimates of price/value and a party's intentions as to acceptable settlement are *not* treated as statements of material fact — so bluffing them is permitted; **material misrepresentations of fact are prohibited and actionable as fraud** [3]. (Rule 4.1 governs lawyers; common-law fraud — material misrepresentation + reliance + damages — applies to everyone. Note: cmt. [2] is itself **criticized** by ethicists as too permissive — present it as the codified majority view, not uncontested.)
- **The crossing point into bad faith is narrow:** (a) deception about **material facts**, (b) **fabricated** deadlines or authority, (c) tactics aimed at **exploiting rather than reaching** agreement. Below that line, it's just negotiation.
- **Distinct from [[bad-faith-argumentation]]** — that's deceptive *debate/discourse* (sealioning, gish gallop, motte-and-bailey); this is deceptive *deal-making*. Different arena, don't conflate.
- **Efficacy is mostly asserted, not proven.** The gambits are well *named* in practitioner books (Dawson, Cohen, Voss); their *reliability* is largely textbook lore, not RCT-validated — recognize them, don't assume they always work. (Anchoring and the lowball, by contrast, have solid experimental support.)
