# Conditional Love

> Making love, warmth, or approval contingent on compliance — affection flows when you meet the conditions and is withdrawn when you don't — so the relationship itself becomes the lever. **The critical line:** disciplining behavior while loving the person is healthy; withdrawing *love* as leverage is not. And counter-intuitively, the "nice" version (extra warmth as a reward) is harmful too.

## Definition

Conditional love (conditional regard) is **the rationing of affection, warmth, approval, or the relationship itself based on whether the target meets the conditions the other person sets** — so love is experienced as earned and revocable rather than secure. The foundations are Rogerian: Carl Rogers contrasted **unconditional positive regard** with **"conditions of worth,"** under which a person comes to feel worthy only when meeting others' conditions, distorting the self [1]. The key empirical work (Assor, Roth & Deci, 2004; Roth et al., 2009) distinguishes **conditional *positive* regard** (more affection than usual when you comply) from **conditional *negative* regard** (withdrawing affection when you don't) — and finds, importantly, that **both are harmful**: even the seemingly benign "reward good behavior with extra warmth" approach produces internal pressure, fragile **contingent self-worth**, resentment, and poorer wellbeing [2][3]. The diagnostic line — and this file's center — is **love/regard used as leverage**, which is categorically different from disciplining behavior while keeping regard for the person constant ([[loving-discipline]]).

## Variants & aliases

- **Love withdrawal / conditional negative regard** — affection, warmth, or contact withdrawn when the target fails to comply ("I can't even look at you right now"); the cold shoulder as punishment (overlaps [[silent-treatment]]).
- **Conditional positive regard** — extra warmth/approval *as a reward* for compliance ("I'm so proud of you" only when you perform); subtler, often well-meant, and still harmful [2][3].
- **Approval rationing** — affection metered to shape behavior, so the target performs for love.
- **In romantic relationships** — affection doled out for compliance; closely linked to [[intermittent-reinforcement]] and the [[love-bombing]] → devaluation cycle, where warmth becomes a reward the target chases.

## How it works

Conditional love exploits the most basic lever there is: **the need for love and belonging** ([[attachment-needs]]). Because secure attachment is a fundamental need, making it contingent means the target will reshape their behavior — and eventually their *self* — to keep the bond. In Self-Determination Theory terms, conditional regard thwarts autonomy: the person complies through internal *pressure* (introjected regulation — "I have to, or I'll lose their love") rather than genuine choice, which is why it produces compliance *plus* resentment and fragile self-esteem [3]. The damage runs deep because it attaches worth itself to performance: the target internalizes **contingent self-worth**, feeling lovable only when meeting conditions [4]. And it is insidious precisely because the "positive" version looks like good parenting or a loving reward — yet still teaches that love must be earned.

## Recognition

### Behavioral markers

- Warmth, affection, or approval visibly track compliance — present when you please them, withdrawn when you don't.
- Disapproval of *behavior* is expressed as withdrawal of *love/the relationship* ("you're not my child when you act like this," icy distance).
- Praise and affection are contingent on performance or meeting expectations (the "positive" form).
- You feel you must earn love by being good/useful/successful, never that it's secure.

### Typical phrases

"I don't love you when you're like this." · "After everything I've done, *this* is how you repay me?" · "I'm only proud of you when you…" · (warm and effusive on compliance; cold and withdrawn on defiance) · "You know I love you — so you'll do this for me."

### Felt-sense indicators

- Love feels earned and revocable, never safe; a background fear of losing it.
- Performing or appeasing to restore warmth after withdrawal.
- Self-worth that rises and falls with others' approval ([[certainty-needs]]/contingent self-esteem).
- Walking on eggshells around the conditions; relief when warmth returns (and dread of losing it again).

### Escalation signs

The conditions tightening or multiplying ([[moving-goalposts]]); withdrawal extending into prolonged [[silent-treatment]] or [[isolation-tactics]]; in relationships, conditional warmth alternating with devaluation in an [[intermittent-reinforcement]] cycle that builds [[trauma-bonding]]; the target organizing their whole identity around the conditions.

## Where it appears

Families (parent–child — the core developmental arena; [[family-parents]]) and intimate relationships ([[intimate-relationships]]). It also appears in any close bond where one party can dispense or withhold approval (some mentor, coach, or high-control-group dynamics).

## Typical sequences & co-occurrence

The contingent-affection engine beneath several tactics: [[love-bombing]] precedes it (declared there: idealization that later becomes conditional), and it co-occurs with [[moving-goalposts]], [[guilt-tripping]], and [[silent-treatment]] (all declared in those files: the shifting conditions, the guilt, the withdrawal). It pairs with [[golden-child-scapegoat]] and [[parentification]] (declared there: the golden child's and the caretaker-child's love is conditional), and in relationships fuels [[trauma-bonding]] via [[intermittent-reinforcement]].

## Counter-strategies

- **Recognize that secure love isn't earned** ([[detection-heuristics]]): naming "their warmth is contingent on my compliance" separates *being loved* from *performing for love* — and undermines the contingent self-worth the pattern installs. Healthy regard is stable; it survives your disagreement and your "no."
- **Decouple your worth from their approval** ([[boundary-scripts]]): you can decline to perform for affection — "I'm not going to earn back warmth by giving in" — and tolerate the withdrawal rather than appease it. The withdrawal is the lever; not chasing it disarms it.
- **For parents (self-check)**: the healthy alternative is *autonomy-supportive* parenting — keep love and regard constant, set limits and consequences on behavior, and give reasons ([[loving-discipline]]). "I love you, *and* there's a consequence for this" beats "I'm only proud of you when…".
- **In relationships, watch the cycle**: conditional warmth alternating with coldness is the [[intermittent-reinforcement]] pattern that builds attachment to mistreatment — name it and weight consistency over the highs.

## Caveats & false positives

### Not conditional love when…

- **It's discipline, limits, or disapproval of behavior** ([[loving-discipline]]): setting expectations, enforcing consequences, and being disappointed in what someone *did* are normal and healthy — *as long as love and regard for the person stay constant*. "I love you, and you're still grounded" is not conditional love; "I don't love you when you do that" is. The line is whether **love itself** is the lever, not whether behavior has consequences. Rogers's unconditional positive regard never meant "no rules."
- **Normal warmth in response to good things**: feeling closer, prouder, or more affectionate when someone is kind or succeeds is human — the harm is *systematically rationing* love to control, and (per the research) even using warmth as a deliberate reward-tool, not ordinary responsive affection.
- **Don't over-attribute intent** ([[epistemic-guardrails]]): conditional regard is extremely common and usually *unconscious and well-intentioned* — many parents "reward good behavior" with extra warmth believing it's good parenting, and pass the pattern down generations. The harm occurs *regardless of intent*; the same surface behavior spans benign-but-harmful to calculated coercion, and intent is rarely knowable from outside. Recognize the pattern without assuming villainy.
- **Boundaries are not conditional love**: a person who says "I'll step back if you keep treating me this way" is setting a boundary about *behavior/safety*, not rationing love to control — distinct from withdrawing affection to enforce compliance.

### Base rates & severity calibration

Responsive affection is normal; *systematically rationing love to control* is the tactic, and even the "positive" reward-version carries costs. The tactic-grade signature: **love/warmth/approval (or the relationship itself) made contingent on compliance, as a pattern that teaches worth must be earned** — distinguished from disciplining behavior while regard stays constant. Severity is `medium`: formative and corrosive to self-worth over time (contingent self-esteem, anxiety, appeasement patterns), though it rarely presents as acute danger on its own.

## Evidence & debates

Graded `supported`: a clean theory-to-evidence chain. Rogers's unconditional positive regard and "conditions of worth" are foundational humanistic *theory* [1]; Assor, Roth & Deci (2004) and especially Roth et al. (2009) provide the empirical core — comparing conditional positive regard, conditional negative regard, and autonomy support, and finding **both forms of conditional regard harmful** while autonomy support is beneficial — a counterintuitive, load-bearing result [2][3]; a 2023 meta-analysis (Haines & Schutte) corroborates the harms across studies [3]; and Crocker's contingent-self-worth work supplies the downstream mechanism [4]. The honest caveats: the discipline-vs-conditional-love line is drawn by the research itself (autonomy support is fully compatible with limits), and conditional regard is usually unconscious cultural practice rather than deliberate manipulation — so the file foregrounds the "don't-over-attribute-intent" and "discipline-is-fine" caveats while still naming the harm.
