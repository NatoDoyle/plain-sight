# Credit Theft & Visibility Games

> Taking the credit for your work — or quietly erasing your visibility — so your contributions advance someone else's career. The idea ignored when you say it and praised when a higher-status person repeats it; the project you built, presented as theirs; the report whose author disappears. **Read the caveats: collaboration and a manager representing the team are normal; the tactic is systematic appropriation plus erasure.**

## Definition

Credit theft and visibility games are **the appropriation of others' work, ideas, or contributions — and the suppression of their visibility — to claim advancement that belongs to someone else**. It ranges from a manager claiming a report's work as their own (which measurably harms employees' voice and performance via perceived unfairness and anger [1]) to the meeting dynamic where an idea is ignored until a higher-status person restates it and is credited (in one controlled study, only ~30% of observers correctly identified who said an idea *first* [2]). It has a well-documented **bias dimension**: women's and underrepresented groups' contributions are systematically under-credited — co-authored work is credited less to women, especially when they collaborate with men [3] (the upstream evaluation bias is captured by the "Heidi/Howard" finding that identical work is rated differently by the author's gender [4]). The diagnostic line is **systematic appropriation of specific contributions + erasure of visibility for one's own advancement** — distinguished from collaboration and legitimate team representation ([[collaborative-credit]]).

## Variants & aliases

- **Idea appropriation in meetings** ("hepeating" / idea-rebranding) — your idea is ignored, then restated by a higher-status colleague and praised as theirs [2].
- **Manager claims the report's work** — a supervisor presenting a subordinate's project, analysis, or idea to leadership as their own [1].
- **Visibility starvation** — being kept off high-profile projects, excluded from the meetings/threads where credit is assigned, or loaded with non-promotable "office housework" while others get the visible wins.
- **Erasure / contribution-burying** — your name dropped from the deck, the email, the byline; "we" becomes "I" when leadership is watching.
- **Gendered/racial under-crediting** — contributions attributed to a higher-status (often male, often white) colleague; the racial analog of "hepeating" is sometimes called "rewhiting" (folk terms) [3][4].

## How it works

Credit games exploit a **status/authority bias in attribution** ([[authority]]): observers and leadership disproportionately register and credit whoever is *higher-status or more visible*, so when a senior person restates an idea or presents a team's work, the credit attaches to them almost automatically — often without anyone (including the appropriator) consciously deciding to steal [2]. The thief leverages this by being the one in the room with leadership, controlling the narrative of "who did what," and ensuring the actual contributor is absent from the visibility. The harm compounds: research finds credit-claiming by leaders breeds anger and unfairness that suppress the victim's future contributions and performance, and "knowledge-theft" victims withdraw, hide work, and disengage — effects that persist even after they leave [1].

## Recognition

(A *pattern*, not a one-off mis-attribution — see Caveats.)

### Behavioral markers

- Your ideas/work routinely resurface as someone else's, with your role minimized or erased.
- An idea you raised is ignored until a higher-status person repeats it and is credited.
- "We" language with you in private becomes "I" in front of leadership.
- You're systematically kept off visible projects or out of credit-assigning forums, while doing the unglamorous work.
- A manager presents your specific deliverables upward without naming you.

### Typical phrases

(Appropriator, to leadership) "So what *I* did was…" (describing your work) · "Great point" — said only when the senior person echoes your earlier, ignored idea · "Let me present that to the execs" (then your name vanishes) · "We're a team — let's not worry about who did what" (deployed selectively to blur your specific credit).

### Felt-sense indicators

- The sinking recognition that the thing you built is being presented as theirs.
- Being talked over, then hearing your idea praised in someone else's mouth.
- Working hard but staying invisible while others get the recognition and promotions.

### Escalation signs

The pattern compounding into stalled advancement while the appropriator rises; credit theft combined with active reputation suppression ([[smear-campaigns]]) or being managed out ([[pip-weaponization]]); visibility-starvation as part of a broader push to sideline or expel ([[mobbing]]).

## Where it appears

Workplaces and organizations (the core arena — [[workplace-bosses]]): especially in hierarchical, low-transparency environments, in meetings, and around promotions/performance reviews where credit translates to advancement. It hits women and underrepresented groups disproportionately [3][4].

## Typical sequences & co-occurrence

Often part of a broader pattern of being sidelined: it accompanies [[smear-campaigns]] (undermining your reputation while taking your work), [[mobbing]] (visibility-starvation as exclusion), and [[pip-weaponization]] (erasing your contributions builds the "underperformer" narrative). It exploits the same status bias that powers [[authority]].

## Counter-strategies

- **Create a paper trail** ([[documentation-practices]]): put ideas in writing (email, shared docs, "summarizing my proposal from the meeting…"), timestamped and visible, so authorship is on record before it can be reassigned. Cc the right people; make your contributions legible.
- **Claim credit in the moment, graciously**: "Building on the idea I raised earlier…" / "Happy to walk leadership through the analysis I built." Reasserting authorship without rancor is legitimate, not arrogant.
- **Use amplification** ([[detection-heuristics]] applied collectively): a documented counter is allies repeating each other's points *and naming the originator* ("As Maya said…"), which forces recognition and denies the re-credit — the strategy Obama-era women staffers used to combat exactly this.
- **Build visibility deliberately**: seek the high-profile assignments and the credit-assigning forums; don't let "office housework" crowd out promotable, visible work.
- **Escalate the pattern, with evidence**: if systematic, raise it (with your documentation) to a manager or HR — understanding HR's limits ([[workplace-bosses]]).

## Caveats & false positives

### Not credit theft when…

- **It's collaboration or legitimate representation** ([[collaborative-credit]]): building on others' ideas, shared credit, and a manager *legitimately* synthesizing and presenting the team's work to leadership are normal and good — that's how organizations function. A leader representing a team isn't stealing from it. The tactic is *systematic appropriation of specific contributions + erasure*, not shared or representative credit.
- **Independent and simultaneous ideas are real**: the same idea genuinely occurs to multiple people (the sociology of science calls these "multiples"); overlap is not proof someone stole from you.
- **Much misattribution is unconscious bias, not deliberate theft** ([[epistemic-guardrails]]): people genuinely fail to register who spoke first [2], and gendered/structural under-crediting is often non-deliberate [3][4]. The harm is real either way, but recognize the *pattern* before assuming calculated theft — and don't read one forgotten attribution as a campaign.
- **Wanting recognition is legitimate**: caring about credit isn't pettiness; advancement depends on it. But not every unshared spotlight is theft — sometimes others genuinely did more.

### Base rates & severity calibration

Workplaces run on collaboration, and credit is usually shared or assigned roughly fairly. The tactic-grade signature: **a pattern of your specific contributions being appropriated and your visibility suppressed for another's advancement** — repeated, not a single mis-credit. Severity is `medium`: it stalls careers and corrodes morale, and (per the research) suppresses the victim's future contributions, but it isn't a safety harm.

## Evidence & debates

Graded `supported`: the core mechanisms rest on peer-reviewed work — leader credit-claiming harming employee outcomes (Chen et al., 2022) [1], inattentional blindness to who-said-it-first in meetings (2024) [2], gendered credit allocation in co-authored work (Sarsons, 2017) [3], and the upstream gender-evaluation bias (Flynn & Anderson, 2003) [4]. Two honesty notes: Flynn & Anderson is about *likeability/evaluation bias*, the upstream mechanism, not literal credit-stealing; and Sarsons' effect is discipline-specific (economics' alphabetical authorship — sociology shows no such gap), so don't overgeneralize. The recognition vocabulary ("hepeating," "bropriating," "rewhiting") and the "amplification" counter are well-reported folk/journalistic, not study findings. The honest synthesis: a real, partly bias-driven phenomenon — much of it non-deliberate — best countered by documentation and visibility, not by assuming malice.
