# Door-in-the-Face

> Ask for something outrageous, get refused, then "retreat" to the request you wanted all along — which now feels like a reasonable concession you should reciprocate. The big ask was never the point; it exists to make the real ask look small and to put you under an obligation to meet the asker halfway.

## Definition

Door-in-the-face (DITF) is **a sequential-request technique in which an inflated first request is made and expected to be refused, so that a smaller second request — the actual target — gains compliance** (Cialdini et al., 1975) [1]. Two mechanisms drive it: **reciprocal concessions** (the asker's visible "retreat" from the big request to the small one triggers the reciprocity norm — they gave ground, so you should too — [[reciprocity]]) and **perceptual contrast** (the second ask looks modest against the first). Like its mirror-image sibling [[foot-in-the-door]], it sits on the **persuasion–manipulation boundary**: charities and honest negotiators use it openly. The diagnostic is whether the first ask is a **sincere opening you'd actually accept** (legitimate negotiation — *hard bargaining*) or one **inflated solely to manufacture a reciprocity obligation** for the real target.

## Variants & aliases

- **The charity retreat** — "Would you mentor a kid weekly for two years?" → (no) → "Would you chaperone one zoo trip?" (the original 1975 study) [1].
- **The negotiation anchor-and-concede** — an extreme opening demand, then a "generous" concession to the figure you wanted (legitimate when the opener is sincere; DITF when it's theater).
- **The sales down-sell** — pitch the premium package, then "settle" for the mid-tier you were always selling.

## How it works

The inflated request is a setup. When you refuse it and the asker visibly scales back, two things push you toward yes: the **reciprocity norm** makes their concession feel like a gift you're obliged to return with a concession of your own (agreeing), and **contrast** makes the smaller ask look reasonable next to the rejected one [1]. The technique depends on the concession appearing genuine and coming from the **same requester** with **little delay** — and, notably, the *size* of the concession doesn't matter, only that a retreat is performed [1]. Its power is in the felt obligation to "meet them halfway," even when the halfway point was the destination all along.

## Recognition

### Behavioral markers

- An opening request so large it's clearly meant to be refused, immediately followed by the "real" (smaller) ask.
- The second request framed as a concession or compromise ("okay, how about just…").
- A felt pull to reciprocate their "giving ground" by agreeing.

### Typical phrases

"I know this is a lot, but…" (huge ask) → "Okay, I understand — could you at least…" (the target) · "I'll come down to X, just for you." · "Since I'm being flexible, surely you can…"

### Felt-sense indicators

- Relief that the second ask is "only" that — then agreeing to something you'd otherwise have declined.
- A sense of owing them for "compromising."

### Escalation signs

The technique stacked with [[manufactured-urgency]] ("this concession is only good now") or used to close a larger [[con-anatomy]]/sales sequence; the "concession" itself still being more than you wanted.

## Where it appears

Sales and negotiation (the core arenas — [[sales]], [[negotiation]]) and fundraising; everyday requests, often benignly.

## Typical sequences & co-occurrence

The large-ask-first mirror of [[foot-in-the-door]] (small-ask-first) — the two classic contrasting sequential-request techniques. It exploits [[reciprocity]] and overlaps the anchoring of [[framing-anchoring]]; in sales it pairs with [[manufactured-urgency]] and the down-sell.

## Counter-strategies

- **Evaluate the second ask on its own terms** ([[detection-heuristics]]): "Setting aside their first request, do I actually want to agree to *this*?" The contrast effect dies once you stop comparing.
- **Don't reciprocate theater** ([[verification-rituals]] / slow down): a concession from an opener that was never sincere isn't a real gift, and you owe nothing for it. "I appreciate the flexibility, but the answer's still no."
- **Name the structure**: recognizing "extreme ask → retreat → real ask" defuses the reciprocity pull.

## Caveats & false positives

- **Legitimate negotiation uses anchoring and concession** (*hard bargaining*): a sincere high opening you'd genuinely accept, followed by real give-and-take, is honest bargaining — not DITF. The line is whether the first ask was *meant to be granted* or inflated purely to manufacture the obligation.
- **Genuine compromise is normal and good**: people really do scale back requests in good faith; reciprocating a sincere concession is healthy cooperation, not manipulation.
- **It's mild** ([[epistemic-guardrails]]): a small, well-replicated but low-magnitude effect; severity is `low` (the harm is one over-large compliance). Don't read every negotiation that opens high as a manipulation.

## Evidence & debates

Graded **`established`**: Cialdini et al. (1975) demonstrated it [1]; it has meta-analytic support (O'Keefe & Hale, 1998, mean r ≈ .10) and a well-powered **2020 direct replication** (Genschow et al.) reproduced the original almost exactly [1]. The effect size is small in absolute terms (typical for social-influence effects) and depends on same-requester / no-delay / prosocial-cause moderators, but the phenomenon is robust and the reciprocal-concessions mechanism is well-supported. Not contested — just modest in magnitude.
