# Elder Targeting

> Exploiting an older person's dependency, isolation, loneliness, or cognitive decline to extract money, property, or control — most often by a trusted family member, caregiver, or new "friend." It works through **undue influence**: isolating the elder, becoming indispensable, then steering money, wills, and decisions toward the influencer. **The honest caveat cuts both ways: most caregiving and inheritance is benign, *and* "they're being scammed" can be weaponized to strip a competent elder's autonomy.**

## Definition

Elder targeting is **the exploitation of an older adult's vulnerability — dependency, isolation, loneliness, or cognitive impairment — to obtain money, property, or control**, typically via **undue influence**: excessive persuasion that overcomes the person's free will and produces an inequitable result. The legal anchor is well-developed: California's statutory test weighs four factors — the **victim's vulnerability**, the **influencer's apparent authority**, the **tactics used**, and the **inequity of the result** — though these are *factors, not elements*, and an inequitable result *alone* is not enough to prove undue influence [1]. Practitioner models map the mechanics: Bennett Blum's **IDEAL** (Isolation, Dependency, Emotional manipulation/Exploitation, Acquiescence, Loss) and Margaret Singer's six-condition undue-influence model [2]. Perpetrators are most often **family, caregivers, or trusted others** [3]. The diagnostic line — and the file's two-sided caveat — is **exploitation of dependency/vulnerability for the influencer's gain** (isolation + undue influence + inequitable result), distinguished from ordinary caregiving and inheritance (*legitimate caregiving*).

## Variants & aliases

- **Family financial exploitation** — an adult child, relative, or caregiver draining accounts, "borrowing" that's never repaid, or controlling an elder's finances for their own benefit ([[financial-abuse]] of an elder).
- **Undue influence over wills/estates** — isolating the elder and steering changes to wills, beneficiaries, deeds, or power of attorney toward the influencer.
- **The predatory caregiver / new "friend" or partner** — someone who inserts themselves, becomes indispensable, isolates the elder from family, and redirects assets ("sweetheart" exploitation of a lonely elder).
- **Scam targeting of elders** — strangers exploiting cognitive vulnerability/loneliness (romance, grandparent, tech-support, investment scams — [[scams-fraud]], [[romance-scam-arc]]).
- **Abuse of a protective mechanism** — power of attorney, guardianship, or conservatorship itself misused to control and exploit (the protective tool becomes the vector).

## How it works

Elder targeting exploits **trust and attachment** ([[trust-mechanics]], [[attachment-needs]]): the influencer manufactures or leverages a trusted, indispensable role — caregiver, devoted relative, attentive new friend — so the elder relies on and confides in them, and loneliness makes the attention precious. From there the undue-influence engine runs: **isolate** the elder from family and advisors (so the influencer becomes the sole source of information and care — [[isolation-tactics]]), **deepen dependency**, then steer money and decisions, often with affection withdrawn when the elder resists. Cognitive decline compounds it: diminished financial capacity is one of the *earliest* functional changes in MCI/early dementia (sometimes preceding diagnosis by years), and insight erodes even as confidence remains — so the elder may not recognize the exploitation and may defend the exploiter.

## Recognition

(By *pattern* — isolation + undue influence + inequity — not a single financial decision; see Caveats.)

### Behavioral markers

- A caregiver/relative/new friend who **isolates** the elder — screening calls, limiting visits, "they're tired," moving them away from family.
- Sudden changes to wills, beneficiaries, deeds, or power of attorney favoring the influencer; new "loans," gifts, or account access.
- One person becomes the sole, indispensable intermediary for the elder's finances and care.
- Unexplained financial changes: missing money, new debts, unpaid bills despite resources, altered spending.
- The elder seems fearful of, or deferential to, the influencer; affection from the influencer appears contingent on compliance.

### Typical phrases

(From the influencer) "Your family doesn't really care — I'm the only one here for you." · "You can trust me with all this — don't bother the others." · "After everything I do for you, surely you'd…" · (to family) "They don't want to see you" / "I'm just protecting them."

### Felt-sense indicators (elder or concerned family)

- (Family) Being shut out, unable to reach or see the elder; a new person controlling access.
- (Elder) Reliance on one person for everything; reluctance to act without their approval; a sense you'd be abandoned if you resisted.
- Decisions that don't match the elder's long-standing wishes or values.

### Escalation signs

Isolation tightening as family raises concerns; rapid estate/POA changes; the influencer resisting any independent professional involvement (lawyer, doctor, financial adviser) or second opinion; cognitive decline accelerating alongside escalating asset transfers. Resistance to *any* verification or outside check is itself a flag ([[verification-rituals]]).

## Where it appears

Families (the most common — adult children, relatives, in-home caregivers; [[family-parents]]) and the broader fraud landscape (predatory strangers and scams targeting elders — [[scams-fraud]], [[romance-scam-arc]]). It concentrates where there is **isolation + cognitive vulnerability + dependency**.

## Typical sequences & co-occurrence

Built on [[isolation-tactics]] and [[financial-abuse]] (declared here: severing outside contact, then exploiting finances), it follows the undue-influence arc and overlaps the fraud dynamics ([[con-anatomy]], [[romance-scam-arc]]) when the perpetrator is a stranger. The trust-building-then-extraction shape mirrors the [[grooming-sequence]] (adult-grooming register).

## Counter-strategies

- **Keep the elder connected and checked** ([[verification-rituals]]): the single best protection is breaking isolation — regular independent contact from multiple family/friends, and **independent professional advice** (a lawyer, a fee-only fiduciary, a doctor) for major financial/estate decisions, separate from the suspected influencer.
- **Verify big decisions independently**: get major changes (wills, POA, large transfers) reviewed by a professional the influencer didn't choose; resistance to that review is informative.
- **Use the protective infrastructure**: banks can flag/refuse suspect transactions (Uniform Power of Attorney Act); estate documents can build in checks; trusted-contact designations on accounts help.
- **Report and escalate** (Safety notes): suspected exploitation goes to **Adult Protective Services**; this is a situation to escalate to professionals/authorities, not adjudicate alone.

## Caveats & false positives

### The honest version cuts both ways

- **Most caregiving, inheritance, and financial help is benign** (*legitimate caregiving*): caring for an aging parent, being a beneficiary, holding power of attorney, helping manage finances, or receiving a gift are *normal and overwhelmingly good*. None of these is exploitation. The tactic is the *convergence* — **isolation + undue influence + an inequitable result** — not the existence of a caregiver or an inheritance ([[epistemic-guardrails]]).
- **An inequitable result alone is not proof** [1]: that one person inherits more, or a devoted caregiver is remembered in a will, is not by itself undue influence — the law explicitly requires more (vulnerability + apparent authority + tactics + inequity, weighed together). Family inheritance disputes routinely *weaponize* "undue influence" against a sibling who simply did the caregiving.
- **Over-detection harms the elder too**: "they're being scammed / they can't decide" is also used to **strip a competent older person's autonomy** — overriding their valid choices, or abusing guardianship/conservatorship to control them (a documented failure mode; cf. high-profile conservatorship abuse). A competent adult has the right to make choices others dislike, including generous or unwise ones. Default to **supported decision-making and the least-restrictive intervention**, not reflexive control.
- **Capacity is the hinge, and it's a professional judgment**: genuine cognitive impairment changes the picture, but capacity is assessed by professionals, is decision-specific, and is not established by "I disagree with their choice."

### Base rates & severity calibration

Most relationships around an elder are caring; exploitation is the marked exception (WHO estimates ~1 in 6 community adults 60+ experience some abuse in a year, with *psychological* abuse more prevalent than financial; perpetrator and under-reporting splits come from advocacy/agency sources — treat as indicative) [3]. The tactic-grade signature: **isolation from family/advisors + an indispensable influencer + steering of money/estate/decisions + cognitive or emotional vulnerability + an inequitable result**, as a pattern. Severity is `high`: it can strip an older person's life savings, security, and autonomy, often irreversibly.

## Safety notes

`safety: elder-abuse-escalation`. Suspected elder abuse or financial exploitation is a matter to **escalate to professionals/authorities, not handle alone**. **US:** report to **Adult Protective Services** (find your local agency via the **Eldercare Locator, 1-800-677-1116**); for elder fraud/scams, the **DOJ National Elder Fraud Hotline, 1-833-372-8311**; if an elder is in immediate danger, 911. Banks and the **Uniform Power of Attorney Act** allow institutions to flag/refuse suspect transactions. The **Elder Justice Act (2010)** funds APS and protections. **UK:** Hourglass (elder-abuse helpline) 0808 808 8141; elsewhere, contact local adult-safeguarding services or police. Balance escalation with the autonomy caveat above: protect against exploitation *and* against wrongly overriding a competent elder's choices.

## Evidence & debates

Graded `supported`: undue influence is a well-developed legal and practitioner construct (California's statutory four-factor test; Blum's IDEAL; Singer's six-condition model) [1][2], and elder-abuse prevalence has meta-analytic backing (WHO/Yon et al.: ~1 in 6 of 60+ in community settings) [3]. The honest caveats: several widely-cited figures (perpetrator splits, the 1-in-24 / 1-in-44 under-reporting ratios) come from **advocacy/agency or single-study sources** and should be cited as indicative, not epidemiological consensus; "financial abuse is the most common" is imprecise (per WHO sub-types, *psychological* abuse is more prevalent — financial is the most economically consequential); and the construct is genuinely **double-edged** — under-detection of real exploitation and over-reach against competent elders' autonomy are *both* real harms, which is why the file foregrounds supported-decision-making and the "inequity alone isn't proof" rule. (Note: do not attribute a "SCAM model" to Singer — that acronym is unverified; use Singer's six-condition model and Blum's IDEAL.)
