# Fake Reviews & Astroturfing

> Manufacturing the appearance of an independent crowd — fake five-star reviews, paid testimonials, sockpuppets, AI-generated praise, bot followers, and "grassroots" support that's actually orchestrated — so you trust a product, company, or cause because "everyone" seems to. As of October 2024 in the US, most of it is squarely illegal.

## Definition

Fake reviews and astroturfing are **the fabrication of independent-looking social proof — reviews, testimonials, ratings, followers, or "grassroots" support — to manufacture trust the product/company/cause hasn't earned**. *Astroturfing* (coined by Senator Lloyd Bentsen, 1985 — fake "grass roots") covers orchestrated campaigns disguised as spontaneous public support; fake reviews are its retail-commerce form. The US now regulates this directly: the **FTC Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials (16 CFR Part 465, effective Oct 21, 2024)** bans fake/AI-generated reviews, buying positive *or* negative reviews, undisclosed insider reviews, company-controlled "independent" review sites, review suppression, and fake social-media indicators (bought followers/views), with civil penalties up to ~$51,744 per violation (inflation-adjusted) [1]. The diagnostic — the file's center — is **fakery, undisclosed material connection, or sentiment-conditioned payment**, not enthusiasm or honest opinion (*genuine reviews*).

## Variants & aliases

- **Fake / AI-generated reviews** — invented five-star (or competitor-trashing one-star) reviews by people with no real experience, increasingly mass-produced by AI [1].
- **Sockpuppets / review farms** — networks of fake accounts or paid reviewers posting coordinated praise; bought followers, likes, and views (fake social-media indicators).
- **Undisclosed insider reviews** — employees, owners, or paid parties posting as ordinary customers without disclosing the connection [1].
- **Review suppression** — burying or removing genuine negative reviews, or threatening reviewers (gag clauses are themselves restricted by the Consumer Review Fairness Act) to skew the average.
- **Astroturfing / front groups** — orchestrated "grassroots" campaigns, sockpuppet activism, or industry-funded "citizen" groups that hide their sponsor ([[competitors-business]], [[propaganda-politics]]).

## How it works

Fake social proof hijacks the **social-proof** heuristic ([[social-proof]]): under uncertainty, we treat others' choices and opinions as evidence ("4.8 stars from 5,000 reviews — it must be good"), a normally-sound shortcut. Astroturfing manufactures that evidence — fabricating the crowd so its apparent consensus does the persuading. It works because the signal is *cheap to fake and hard to verify*: you can't easily tell a real reviewer from a paid or AI one, and a manufactured "everyone's switching to X" or "thousands love this" exploits exactly the cue (apparent independent consensus) that we're wired to trust.

## Recognition

- A flood of similar, generic, or oddly-timed five-star reviews (bursts), or templated/AI-flavored phrasing.
- Reviews light on specifics, heavy on superlatives; a bimodal "J-curve" (lots of 5s and 1s, little middle).
- Few or no *verified-purchase* reviews; reviewer accounts with no history or implausible review volume.
- "Grassroots" support or a "citizen group" whose funding/sponsor is hidden.
- Suspiciously high follower/engagement counts that don't match real interaction.

### Felt-sense indicators

- Trusting something mainly because "look how many people love it" — without any independent check.
- A vague sense the reviews are too uniform, too glowing, or too sudden.

### Escalation signs

Fake reviews feeding [[dark-patterns-social-urgency]] (fabricated "bestseller"/"trending" social proof); astroturfed campaigns scaling into [[disinformation-playbooks]] / manufactured consensus in the political arena ([[propaganda-politics]]); fake negative reviews deployed against a competitor ([[competitors-business]], [[smear-campaigns]]).

## Where it appears

E-commerce and app stores, advertising/marketing, influencer ecosystems ([[advertising-marketing]], [[digital-platforms]]); competitor attacks ([[competitors-business]]); and politics/advocacy as orchestrated "grassroots" ([[propaganda-politics]]).

## Counter-strategies

- **Verify before you trust the crowd** ([[verification-rituals]]): weight *verified-purchase* reviews; read the critical (3-star) reviews for specifics; cross-check on independent sites; reverse-search suspicious testimonial photos; discount follower/engagement counts.
- **Read the pattern, not the average** ([[detection-heuristics]]): bursts of generic praise, templated language, no review history, and a J-curve distribution are tells of manufactured proof.
- **Treat "everyone loves it" as a claim to check, not evidence**: manufactured consensus is exactly the cue this tactic fakes — independent verification is the counter.
- **Report**: fake reviews and undisclosed paid endorsements can be reported to the FTC (reportfraud.ftc.gov) and the platform.

## Legal protections & reporting

*US, jurisdiction-labeled; not legal advice.* The **FTC Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials (16 CFR Part 465, eff. Oct 21, 2024)** makes most of this illegal — fake/AI reviews, bought reviews, undisclosed insider reviews, fake "independent" review sites, review suppression, and fake social-media indicators — with civil penalties up to ~$51,744 per violation (2024; adjusts annually) [1]. The **Consumer Review Fairness Act (2016)** voids contract clauses that gag honest negative reviews. Undisclosed paid endorsements also violate the **FTC Endorsement Guides** ([[parasocial-influencer-tactics]]). Report to the FTC and the hosting platform; for competitor false reviews, see the Lanham Act route in [[competitors-business]].

## Caveats & false positives

- **Genuine reviews and real grassroots opinion are normal and valuable** (*genuine reviews*): authentic enthusiasm, organic word-of-mouth, real customer reviews (even glowing ones), and genuine coordinated advocacy (a real community rallying) are legitimate. The violation is **fakery, undisclosed material connection, or payment conditioned on a particular sentiment** — not popularity or enthusiasm.
- **Disclosed incentivized reviews can be lawful**: "I got this free to review" with honest opinion is permitted; the problem is *undisclosed* connections or *sentiment-conditioned* incentives.
- **Don't cry "astroturf" at real support** ([[epistemic-guardrails]]): dismissing a genuine grassroots backlash or authentic positive reviews as "fake" is itself a bad-faith move (and the mirror error). Reserve the label for evidence of fabrication/coordination.
- **It's medium-severity**: the harm is misled purchases/decisions and distorted markets — real, but a single bad buy, not abuse.

## Evidence & debates

Graded `supported` — and the most **regulatorily grounded** of the dark-patterns set: 16 CFR Part 465 is binding US law as of 2024 [1], astroturfing is a long-documented practice (Bentsen, 1985), and the underlying mechanism ([[social-proof]]) is established psychology. Detection heuristics (review bursts, J-curves, verified-purchase rates, AI-phrasing tells) are practitioner signals, not perfect diagnostics — fake-review detection is an arms race, especially with AI-generated text. The honest line, foregrounded in the file: the offense is fabrication/non-disclosure, so the boundary from *genuine reviews* turns on authenticity and disclosure, not on how positive the reviews are.
