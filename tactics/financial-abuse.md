# Financial Abuse

> Controlling a partner's access to money so they can't build the independence it takes to leave. It's one of the quietest forms of abuse and one of the most effective traps — financial dependence is repeatedly cited as a leading reason victims stay or return. The bruises are invisible: a controlled allowance, a sabotaged job, a credit score wrecked by debt taken in your name.

## Definition

Financial (or economic) abuse is **the use of money and economic resources to control, exploit, and entrap a partner or family member** — a recognized form of [[coercive-control]] of which it is a near-constant component. The validated measure (Adams et al.'s Scale of Economic Abuse, 2008) originally identified two dimensions — **economic control** and **economic exploitation** — with **employment sabotage** added as a third in later revisions (Postmus et al., 2016) [1]:
- **Economic control** — restricting access to and knowledge of money: allowances, monitoring/policing spending, withholding necessities, denying information about finances.
- **Economic exploitation** — destroying the target's resources: stealing, running up debt, and **coerced debt** (debt incurred by fraud or coercion in the victim's name — Littwin, 2012) that wrecks credit and forecloses escape [2].
- **Employment sabotage** — preventing or interfering with work or school: harassment at the job, hiding car keys, inflicting visible injuries before interviews, sabotaging childcare.

The defining line is **coercive restriction of economic freedom that manufactures dependence** — not the mere fact that one partner manages the money ([[shared-financial-management]]).

## Variants & aliases

- **The allowance / financial leash** — the target must account for every dollar, receives a controlled allowance, or has no access to joint funds.
- **Coerced debt** [2] — credit cards or loans opened in the victim's name (by pressure, deception, or forgery); the damaged credit then blocks housing, employment, and independence.
- **Employment sabotage** — the partner who makes you quit, gets you fired, or makes holding a job impossible, so you have no independent income.
- **Economic exploitation / theft** — taking the target's wages, benefits, or savings; refusing to contribute while consuming the target's resources.
- **Elder financial abuse** — an adult child or caregiver controlling/draining an older relative's money, pension, or estate ([[elder-targeting]], [[family-parents]]).

## How it works

Financial abuse runs on **engineered dependence and the fear it creates** ([[fear-exploitation]]): if you control the money, you control the exits. By restricting income, access, and credit, the abuser ensures the target *cannot afford* to leave — no deposit, no savings, ruined credit, no job history — so the threat of destitution does the controlling without a single overt demand. It typically interlocks with [[isolation-tactics]] (cutting off the support network that might lend help) to close off every independent resource at once. Because money management is a normal, often-shared part of partnership, the control can be reframed as practicality ("I'm just better with money"), which is what lets it hide.

## Recognition

### Behavioral markers

- You have no access to, or knowledge of, the household finances; money is "their" domain and questions are unwelcome.
- You're given an allowance and must justify every expenditure; spending is monitored and policed.
- Debt, accounts, or contracts exist in your name that you didn't agree to (coerced debt).
- Your working is blocked, sabotaged, or punished; your earnings are taken or controlled.
- You can't access necessities (food, medicine, transport) without permission.

### Typical phrases

"You're hopeless with money — I'll handle it." · "Why do you need your own account? What are you hiding?" · "You don't *earn* it, so you don't decide how it's spent." · "I'll give you what you need — just ask." · "If you leave, you'll have nothing."

### Felt-sense indicators

- Having to ask permission, or account for receipts, like a child rather than a partner.
- A specific dread about money that you can't separate from the relationship.
- The sense that you couldn't survive financially if you left — by design.
- Shame about debt or accounts you didn't knowingly create.

### Escalation signs

Control tightening as you gain (or seek) any independence; debt or financial sabotage escalating when you mention leaving or get a job; finances weaponized alongside [[isolation-tactics]] and surveillance ([[stalkerware-monitoring]]) into full [[coercive-control]]. Because economic entrapment is a barrier to exit, the danger often peaks exactly when the target tries to build the independence to leave.

## Where it appears

Intimate relationships (the core arena — a near-universal component of coercive control; [[intimate-relationships]]) and families (controlling parents over adult children; adult children or caregivers over elders — [[elder-targeting]]). It crosses every income level — controlling a millionaire's spouse and trapping a low-income partner use the same playbook.

## Typical sequences & co-occurrence

A structural pillar of [[coercive-control]] (declared there as a constituent) that interlocks with [[isolation-tactics]] (declared there: severing the people who might offer financial help). It frequently accompanies [[gaslighting]] ("you agreed to this," "you spend recklessly") and the FOG of [[emotional-blackmail]], and the engineered dependence is a core mechanism of [[escalation-entrapment]].

## Counter-strategies

### In the moment / building independence

- **Establish independent access** ([[dv-safety-planning]]): your own bank account (at a different bank), redirected direct deposit, changed PINs/passwords, and a small accessible cash reserve — done carefully, since discovery can escalate danger (see Safety notes).
- **Protect your credit**: check your credit reports for accounts you didn't open; in the US a **credit freeze is free** (FCRA) and blocks new coerced debt; document any coerced debt for later dispute.
- **Document the financial picture** ([[documentation-practices]]): copies of account statements, tax returns, IDs, and a record of controlled/sabotaged finances — kept somewhere the abuser can't access.

### Structural / when to exit

- **Get specialist help**: economic-abuse advocates can map a financial-safety plan, coerced-debt remedies, and benefits. **US:** National Domestic Violence Hotline 1-800-799-7233 / thehotline.org; NNEDV's economic-justice resources. **UK:** Surviving Economic Abuse (survivingeconomicabuse.org); Refuge 0808 2000 247.
- **Coerced-debt remedies are emerging**: some jurisdictions now let survivors void debt incurred by coercion (e.g., California's SB 975, effective 2023) — worth checking current local law.
- **Sequence safety before independence moves**: building financial autonomy is exactly what a controlling partner is watching for, so plan the timing with an advocate (Safety notes).

## Caveats & false positives

### Not financial abuse when…

- **One partner manages the money by agreement** ([[shared-financial-management]]): plenty of healthy couples have one person handle the budget, pool finances, or support a stay-at-home partner — *by mutual, transparent agreement, with both retaining knowledge, access, and autonomy*. That is not abuse; the abuse is *coercive restriction that manufactures dependence*.
- **Ordinary financial disagreements and constraints**: arguing about spending, being broke, disagreeing on a budget, or one partner being genuinely better with money are normal ([[epistemic-guardrails]]). Poverty is not abuse; conflict about money is not control.
- **Cultural and religious financial arrangements**: single-earner models, pooled family finances, and culturally normative divisions of financial labor are not abuse by Western individualist default — the test is consent, transparency, and retained autonomy, not the arrangement's shape.
- **The diagnostic is the *substantial adverse effect on economic freedom*** (the UK statute's threshold): a *pattern* of control/exploitation/sabotage that restricts the target's ability to acquire, use, or keep money — not a single financial decision or rough patch.

### Base rates & severity calibration

Economic abuse co-occurs in **the large majority** of domestic-violence cases — but note this figure (often cited as ~94–99%) comes from clinical/advocacy samples of help-seeking survivors, not the general population, so it describes "within abusive relationships," not "of all relationships" [1]. Most couples share or divide finances without any abuse. The tactic-grade signature: **coercive control of economic life — restricted access, monitored/allowanced spending, coerced debt, or sabotaged employment — that manufactures dependence and forecloses exit**, as a pattern over time. Severity is `high`: it rarely leaves marks but is one of the most effective traps keeping victims in danger.

## Safety notes

`safety: dv-escalation`. Financial abuse is a core component of [[coercive-control]], and the moves that build financial independence (opening an account, getting a job, checking credit) are exactly what a controlling partner monitors for — discovery can escalate danger, and **separation is the peak-danger window**. Plan financial independence and exit *with a domestic-violence advocate*, ideally from a device the abuser can't monitor ([[stalkerware-monitoring]]), and don't announce the plan. **US:** National Domestic Violence Hotline **1-800-799-7233** / thehotline.org; **UK:** Surviving Economic Abuse + Refuge **0808 2000 247**. Protocol: [[dv-safety-planning]].

## Evidence & debates

Graded `supported`: economic abuse is a measured construct with validated instruments — Adams et al.'s Scale of Economic Abuse (2008, two factors: control + exploitation), revised by Postmus et al. (2016, adding employment sabotage as a third; the SEA2 later reverted toward two) — so the exact factor count is debated, but the phenomenon is well-established [1]. Coerced debt is a documented legal-empirical construct (Littwin, 2012) [2]. It is now recognized in law (UK Domestic Abuse Act 2021's statutory "economic abuse"). The honest caveats: the headline prevalence figures come from non-representative help-seeking samples (state "the large majority," not a population rate), and the line between abuse and a freely-agreed division of financial labor must be drawn at coercion and lost autonomy, not at the existence of one money-manager.
