# Foot-in-the-Door

> Get a small "yes" first, then ask for the big one. Agreeing to a minor request makes you more likely to agree to a larger one — because the first yes quietly revises your self-image into "someone who goes along with this." It's a mild, boundary-line influence technique used as much by charities as by con artists; the manipulation is in *engineering* the ladder, not in any single ask.

## Definition

Foot-in-the-door (FITD) is **a sequential-request technique in which a small initial request is used to increase compliance with a larger later one** (Freedman & Fraser, 1966) [1]. The mechanism is **commitment-consistency / self-perception**: saying yes to the small ask shifts your self-concept ("I'm the kind of person who helps / who's involved with this"), and the larger request then rides on the drive to stay consistent ([[commitment-consistency]]). It sits squarely on the **persuasion–manipulation boundary** — charities, health campaigns, and honest salespeople use it openly and legitimately — so the diagnostic isn't the structure but whether the small yes was a *genuine* ask or **bait engineered to manufacture consistency pressure** for a costly target (*healthy persuasion*). It's also a *modest* effect (see Evidence): a lever, not a mind-control switch.

## Variants & aliases

- **The charity ladder** — "Would you sign this petition?" → "Would you donate?" → "Would you set up a monthly gift?" (often legitimate, sometimes engineered).
- **The sales ladder** — a free trial / tiny commitment → an upsell; "just give me five minutes" → the full pitch.
- **The scammer's small ask** — a tiny initial payment, favor, or disclosure that opens the door to escalating requests ([[con-anatomy]], [[romance-scam-arc]]'s first small ask).
- **Incremental boundary erosion** — each small concession becomes the baseline for the next ([[boundary-testing]]); the engine of [[escalation-entrapment]] (which is *built from* this — declared there).

## How it works

The first compliance is the hinge: a small, low-cost yes that you'd never refuse subtly recategorizes you to yourself as a person who agrees, helps, or is "in." When the larger request arrives, refusing it would feel inconsistent with that fresh self-image, so consistency pressure does the persuading ([[commitment-consistency]]). It works best precisely where it looks most innocent — when there's **no external incentive** (so you attribute the first yes to your own values, not a reward), when the cause is **prosocial**, when you actually *performed* the small act, and when the two asks are **similar** [1]. That's also why it's modest and easily defeated: make the small yes trivial, or notice the ladder, and the effect evaporates.

## Recognition

### Behavioral markers

- A trivial, hard-to-refuse first ask, followed (sometimes later) by a much larger one in the same domain.
- Each agreement quietly becoming the new baseline for a bigger request.
- A sense that you "already started, so…" pulling you toward the next step.

### Typical phrases

"Could you just sign this?" (then: "…and donate?") · "Just try the free version." · "Can you do me one tiny favor?" · "You already helped with X — how about Y?"

### Felt-sense indicators

- The pull of "I already said yes to the small thing, it'd be inconsistent to stop now."
- Realizing you've climbed several rungs you never consciously agreed to.

### Escalation signs

The asks growing faster or larger; the small-yes pattern used by a con or scam to walk you toward real money or commitment ([[con-anatomy]], [[romance-scam-arc]]); incremental compliance hardening into [[escalation-entrapment]] where backing out feels costlier each step.

## Where it appears

Sales and fundraising (the classic arenas — [[sales]]), and scams/fraud as the opening small ask ([[scams-fraud]], [[con-anatomy]]). Also everyday social and workplace influence, mostly benignly.

## Typical sequences & co-occurrence

The opening move of laddered compliance: it's the engine [[escalation-entrapment]] is *composed of* (declared there) and pairs with [[boundary-testing]] (each small violation resets the baseline). It exploits [[commitment-consistency]]; its mirror-image sibling is [[door-in-the-face]] (large-ask-first), the two are the classic contrasting sequential-request techniques.

## Counter-strategies

- **Judge each ask on its own merits** ([[detection-heuristics]]): "Would I agree to *this* request if it were the first thing asked?" Decoupling the current ask from the prior small yes neutralizes the consistency pressure.
- **A prior yes doesn't obligate the next** ([[boundary-scripts]]): "I was happy to do the small thing; this is a bigger ask and the answer is no." Consistency with your *values* matters; consistency with a manufactured ladder does not.
- **Watch the ladder, not the rung**: notice when small agreements are becoming a staircase, especially around money, commitment, or boundaries.

## Caveats & false positives

- **It's a legitimate, ubiquitous influence structure** (*healthy persuasion*): charities, public-health campaigns, and honest sales use small-then-large asks openly, and *genuine* engagement often does grow from a small first step. That's normal persuasion, not manipulation. The line is **covert engineering of the ladder to extract a costly target**, not the existence of a small first ask.
- **A small request leading to a bigger one is often innocent**: people who help once are genuinely more likely to help again — sometimes that's just developing involvement, not a trap.
- **It's mild and modest** ([[epistemic-guardrails]]): FITD is a small, moderator-dependent effect (about half the original studies found none — see Evidence), so don't over-read every two-step ask as a manipulation campaign. Severity is `low`: the harm is usually a single over-large compliance, not abuse.

## Evidence & debates

Graded **`supported`** — and the honest headline is its *modesty*. Freedman & Fraser (1966) established the effect [1]; meta-analysis (Beaman et al., 1983) put it at roughly r ≈ .15, with nearly half of studies showing no effect or a reversal, and Burger's (1999) review reframed it as a *multiple-process* effect heavily dependent on moderators (no incentive, prosocial framing, performed and similar requests) [1]. So it's a real but small lever, not a robust one — which is exactly why it's `low` severity and easily countered by judging each ask on its merits. The construct itself is not contested; its *reliability and size* are the caveat.
