# Four-Square & Payment Packing

> The car-dealership numbers game: a worksheet split into four boxes (trade-in, price, down payment, monthly payment) shuffled until you lose track of the total, so you negotiate the *monthly payment* while the dealer quietly recovers profit elsewhere — then the finance office "fits" add-ons into a payment you've already accepted so they feel free. The move is the **obfuscation of total cost**, not the selling of a car.

## Definition

Four-square and payment packing are **auto-sales techniques that move your attention off the one number that matters — the total, out-the-door price — and onto the monthly payment, so cost can be hidden in the loan term and in padded add-ons**. The **four-square** is a worksheet divided into four boxes — **trade-in allowance, purchase price, down payment (cash down), and monthly payment** — negotiated simultaneously and rewritten so many times that the buyer can't track the whole; the salesperson concedes on one box (a generous trade-in figure, a lower payment) while recovering it in another, most often by **stretching the loan term** (e.g., 36 → 72 months), which lowers the payment while *raising* total interest and price [1]. **Payment packing** (a.k.a. "payment stuffing") is the finance-office sequel: quote an inflated monthly payment, get agreement, then slot in F&I products — extended warranty/service contract, GAP insurance, credit life/disability insurance, paint/fabric protection, theft etching — so they appear to cost nothing because the payment "doesn't change" [2]. The diagnostic — the file's center — is **deception and undisclosed/non-consensual charges**, not negotiation or dealer profit, both of which are legal and normal (*hard bargaining*).

## Variants & aliases

- **The four-square worksheet** — the four-box grid; you're asked to initial it early to signal commitment, then the numbers churn [1].
- **Payment packing / stuffing** — padding the agreed monthly payment with optional F&I add-ons presented as free or near-free; sometimes only signature lines are shown, not the itemization [2].
- **Loan-term stretching** — lowering the payment by lengthening the loan, hiding a higher total cost (and often leaving the buyer underwater).
- **The "T.O." (turn-over)** — handing a hesitant buyer to a closer, sales manager, then the F&I "box," each a fresh, more skilled negotiator [1].
- **Spot delivery / "yo-yo" financing** — letting you drive the car home before financing is final, then calling you back days later claiming the loan "fell through," demanding worse terms (higher APR, more down) when you're already attached and the old car may be gone [3].

## How it works

The engine is **cognitive overload and confusion** ([[cognitive-overload-confusion]]): four interdependent numbers, crossed out and rewritten, exceed what most people can track in real time, so you anchor on the single figure you *can* follow — the monthly payment — and lose the total. **Information asymmetry** ([[information-asymmetry]]) compounds it: the dealer knows the true cost, the holdback, and the term math; you usually don't, so a "great payment" can conceal a worse price. **Commitment and consistency** ([[commitment-consistency]]) supplies the foothold — initialing the box, agreeing to a payment, and (in spot delivery) physically taking the car all create prior commitments the closer and finance manager leverage. Secondary levers ride along: **scarcity/urgency** ([[scarcity-urgency]], the wear-down and "today only"), **reciprocity** ([[reciprocity]], the coffee and favors), and, in yo-yo financing, the **sunk cost** of having already driven off ([[manufactured-urgency]] register).

## Recognition

### Behavioral markers

- A handwritten **four-box worksheet** appears; numbers get crossed out and rewritten while the conversation centers on "what payment works for you."
- You're steered to a **monthly-payment** discussion and away from the **out-the-door / total price**.
- The loan **term lengthens** to hit a payment ("we can get you there at 72 months").
- In the finance office, **add-ons appear** (warranty, GAP, protection packages) framed as fitting your payment, often with only signature lines shown.
- You're encouraged to **take the car home before financing is signed off**, then contacted later to "redo the paperwork."

### Typical phrases

"What monthly payment are you comfortable with?" · "Let's see what we can do to get you there." · "Just initial here so I can take this to my manager." · "For only a few dollars more a month, you also get…" · "Congratulations, you're approved — drive it home today!" (then, days later) "The bank didn't go for it; we need a bit more down." · "This price is only good today."

### Felt-sense indicators

- Confusion about **what the car actually costs in total** — you know the payment but not the price.
- A sense of being **worn down**, rushed, and passed between people.
- Discovering **products on the contract you don't remember agreeing to**, or that "free" things weren't free.
- After driving off: a stomach-drop call that the deal has to change.

### Escalation signs

The "T.O." chain escalating to ever-more-aggressive closers; refusal to give a written out-the-door price; pressure to sign without reading; spot delivery followed by a demand for worse terms (yo-yo) — a point where you can lawfully unwind and walk.

## Where it appears

Car dealerships and other high-pressure, finance-heavy sales floors ([[sales]]) — new and used auto, RV/powersports, and any setting with a separate "F&I" (Finance & Insurance) office where financing and add-on products are sold after the price is "agreed."

## Typical sequences & co-occurrence

It co-occurs with [[lowballing]] (a too-good number on price or trade-in that quietly worsens) and with [[manufactured-urgency]] ("today only"), and is the analog-paperwork cousin of [[dark-patterns-obstruction]]'s sneak-into-basket (charges you didn't knowingly accept). It exploits [[cognitive-overload-confusion]], [[information-asymmetry]], and [[commitment-consistency]].

## Counter-strategies

### In the moment

- **Negotiate the out-the-door (total) price, not the monthly payment.** Say it plainly: "I'll only discuss total price; we can talk financing after." Refuse to fill in the payment box ([[detection-heuristics]]).
- **Negotiate each element separately** — price, then trade-in, then financing, then any add-ons — never as one bundle where concessions hide.
- **Decline add-ons in the F&I office** unless you've independently decided you want them: "No products today; just the financing." They are optional and usually cancelable.
- **Verify the math** on your own phone (price + rate + term → total and payment) before agreeing.

### Structural / long-term

- **Arrange financing independently first** — a bank or credit-union **pre-approval** removes the dealer's single biggest lever ([[verification-rituals]]).
- **Get the final terms in writing and read them before taking delivery.** Do not drive off until financing is *fully* approved and signed — this defeats yo-yo/spot delivery.
- **Research add-ons (GAP, service contracts) separately**; decide before you walk in, not under pressure.

### When to exit or escalate

If they won't give a written out-the-door price, keep changing agreed numbers, or pull a post-delivery "the loan fell through" — **walk**. The right to leave is your strongest leverage. For yo-yo/packing harm, see the legal routes below.

## Legal protections & reporting

*US, jurisdiction-labeled; not legal advice — and this area is in flux.* The FTC's **CARS Rule (Combating Auto Retail Scams, 16 CFR Part 463)**, finalized Dec 2023, was **vacated by the Fifth Circuit on Jan 27, 2025** (procedural — the FTC skipped a required advance notice of proposed rulemaking) and **formally withdrawn from the CFR effective Feb 12, 2026** — so there is currently **no dedicated federal auto-sales rule**; the FTC would have to restart rulemaking [4]. What still applies: the **Truth in Lending Act / Regulation Z (12 CFR Part 1026)** requires clear, conspicuous disclosure of the **APR, finance charge, amount financed, payment schedule, and total of payments** before you sign; and **FTC Act §5 plus state UDAP laws** reach deceptive packing and yo-yo practices — the FTC has enforced, e.g., **Napleton Automotive ($10M, 2022)** and **Passport Automotive ($3.38M, 2022)** for sneaking in unwanted add-ons [5]. "Yo-yo"/spot-delivery abuses are documented by the **National Consumer Law Center** (*Automobile Fraud*) and the Center for Responsible Lending [3]. Report problems to the **FTC (reportfraud.ftc.gov)**, the **CFPB**, and your **state attorney general**; consult a consumer attorney for yo-yo or packing.

## Caveats & false positives

### Not this tactic when…

- **Financing a car and dealer profit are normal and legal.** A dealer making money on price, financing, and optional products is not fraud (*hard bargaining*).
- **A four-square is not, by itself, deception** — it's a negotiation worksheet; hard, even aggressive, bargaining over disclosed numbers is lawful. The violation is **obscuring the total cost** or **packing products without disclosure or consent**, not the grid.
- **Some add-ons genuinely help some buyers.** Per the **CFPB**, GAP insurance can be worth it for a buyer who'll be *underwater* (little down, long term, fast-depreciating car) — it's optional and cancelable, valuable for some and pointless for others. Offering optional products *with itemized prices and informed consent* is legitimate.

### Base rates & severity calibration

A confusing or high-pressure negotiation is **not automatically fraud**, and buyer's remorse is not packing ([[epistemic-guardrails]]). The line is **disclosure + informed consent + accurate representation** — the exact standard the FTC's enforcement orders imposed. Severity is **medium**: the harm is financial (overpaying, an underwater loan, unwanted products) — real and sometimes large, but generally recoverable and not a safety matter. Treat "four-square," "payment packing," and "yo-yo" as descriptive consumer-advocacy terms for patterns reachable under §5/UDAP, **not** as named statutory crimes.

## Evidence & debates

Graded `supported`: the techniques are richly documented in consumer journalism and consumer-law sources — the canonical insider account is Chandler Phillips's undercover **"Confessions of a Car Salesman"** (Edmunds, 2001), which calls the four-square "the salesman's number-one tool" [1]; payment packing is documented by consumer-law writers [2]; yo-yo/spot delivery is authoritatively covered by the NCLC and CRL [3]; and the FTC has brought enforcement actions [5]. There is **no peer-reviewed academic literature** naming "four-square," and no reliable figure for "average amount packed per deal" — treat any such dollar/percentage stat as unsourced. The honest line, central here: the boundary from legitimate selling is *deception and undisclosed charges*, not the existence of negotiation or profit.
