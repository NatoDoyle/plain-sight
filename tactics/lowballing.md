# Lowballing

> Get the commitment first on attractive terms, then raise the cost — and the commitment holds, even though you'd never have agreed to the worse deal upfront. The classic car-lot version: you agree on a price, then "the manager won't approve it" and fees appear. The deal you said yes to isn't the deal you get.

## Definition

Lowballing (the low-ball technique) is **securing a commitment to an attractive offer, then removing the favorable terms or adding cost — after which the commitment tends to hold despite the worsened deal** (Cialdini, Cacioppo, Bassett & Miller, 1978) [1]. The mechanism is **commitment-consistency**: once you've decided and feel committed (especially publicly), you stay the course even when the original reason for the decision is removed ([[commitment-consistency]]). It must be distinguished from **bait-and-switch** (a different, more overtly deceptive move — swapping the *product*, not just the terms — and often illegal): lowballing keeps the *same* item and worsens the *terms* after you've committed. The diagnostic line is **securing commitment first, then revealing a cost that was foreseeable and deliberately withheld** — versus a good-faith price change honestly disclosed ([[hard-bargaining]]).

## Variants & aliases

- **The car-lot bump** — agree on a price, then "my manager won't approve that," or fees/add-ons appear in the finance office ([[four-square-payment-packing]]).
- **The commitment-then-cost** — sign up / say yes to an attractive deal, then learn of the setup fee, the shipping, the exclusion, the catch.
- **The "small" added cost** — once committed, a modest extra is tacked on that you'd have refused as part of the original ask.
- **The deferred bump** — the worse terms revealed after a delay, once your commitment has solidified.

## How it works

The trick is sequencing: get the decision *first*, on terms good enough to commit to, then change them. By the time the cost rises, you've already mentally bought in — pictured yourself in the car, told people, filled out the form — and backing out would mean undoing a decision you've committed to, which consistency pressure resists ([[commitment-consistency]]). It works best when the **commitment is public**, the **added cost is small** relative to the original, and the bump comes **after a slight delay** that lets the commitment set [1]. The original favorable offer was, in effect, a foot in your psychology — its job was to get the yes, not to be the final deal.

## Recognition

### Behavioral markers

- An attractive offer secures your agreement, then the terms worsen ("actually, that doesn't include…", "the price went up", "there's a fee").
- The worsening comes *after* you've committed, signed, or emotionally bought in — rarely before.
- The same product/service, now at a worse price or with added cost (distinguishing it from a product swap).

### Typical phrases

"Great, let's write it up — oh, the manager needs to add…" · "That price was before the [fee/tax/add-on]." · "I can do that price, but not with [the thing you wanted]." · "You're already approved — there's just one more cost."

### Felt-sense indicators

- Having mentally committed (you can already picture owning it), so the added cost feels like a small hurdle rather than a dealbreaker.
- Reluctance to "start over" or look indecisive after agreeing.

### Escalation signs

Multiple bumps stacking after commitment (the finance-office add-on parade — [[four-square-payment-packing]]); the bump combined with [[manufactured-urgency]] ("we need to close today"); pressure not to reconsider the now-worse deal.

## Where it appears

Sales (the core arena — [[sales]]), especially big-ticket purchases with a commitment step then a separate cost/paperwork step (cars, home improvement, gym/club memberships, services with setup fees).

## Typical sequences & co-occurrence

The commitment-cost cousin of [[foot-in-the-door]] (both run on [[commitment-consistency]]); in car sales it's part of [[four-square-payment-packing]], and it pairs with [[manufactured-urgency]] to discourage reconsideration. Distinct from bait-and-switch (product swap; see Caveats).

## Counter-strategies

- **Treat any post-commitment cost change as a fresh decision** ([[detection-heuristics]]): "The deal I agreed to was X; this is a different, worse deal, so I'm re-deciding from scratch — and I can walk." The commitment was to the *original* terms, not the new ones.
- **Get the full terms in writing before committing** ([[verification-rituals]]): pin the total price (all fees, taxes, add-ons) before you say yes, so there's nothing left to "bump."
- **Be willing to walk after the bump**: the technique relies on your reluctance to undo a decision; demonstrating you'll leave over the change defeats it.

## Caveats & false positives

- **Good-faith price changes happen** ([[hard-bargaining]]): genuinely new or unforeseeable circumstances, or an honest "I quoted wrong," disclosed openly and giving you a real chance to walk, are not lowballing. The line is *securing commitment first* and revealing a *foreseeable, withheld* cost to exploit consistency.
- **Not bait-and-switch**: bait-and-switch swaps the product (advertised item "unavailable," steered to a pricier one) and is often illegal; lowballing keeps the same item with worse terms. Don't conflate them.
- **Negotiation involves moving numbers**: in an openly adversarial negotiation, prices move and that's normal. Lowballing is specifically the *commit-then-worsen* sequence, not ordinary back-and-forth.
- **It's mild** ([[epistemic-guardrails]]): severity is `low` — the harm is typically a somewhat worse deal, recoverable by walking away. The counter (re-decide, get it in writing, be ready to leave) is reliable.

## Evidence & debates

Graded **`established`**: Cialdini et al. (1978) demonstrated the low-ball effect [1]; it has meta-analytic support (Burger & Caputo, 2015; Pascual et al., 2016 — weighted r ≈ .16, OR ≈ 2.47 across ~4,700 participants) and identified moderators (public commitment, small added cost, deferred target) [1]. The commitment-consistency mechanism is well-established. The honest note is the same as its siblings: a real, replicated, but *modest* effect — and the line from legitimate (good-faith) price changes must be drawn at the commit-then-withhold structure, not at any price change.
