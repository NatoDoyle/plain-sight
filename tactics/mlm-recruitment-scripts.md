# MLM Recruitment Scripts

> The pitch that signs you up as a distributor: a friend or relative messages you about an "opportunity" to "be your own boss," shows screenshots of income and a laptop-on-a-beach lifestyle, and reframes every hesitation as fear. It runs on trust borrowed from a real relationship and a dream that, for the overwhelming majority, doesn't pay. The recruiter usually believes it too — they were recruited the same way.

## Definition

MLM recruitment scripts are **the standardized persuasion patterns used to enroll new participants into a multi-level-marketing "downline" — leveraging the recruit's personal relationships, an income/freedom dream, and manufactured urgency, while obscuring that most participants lose money**. This is the *recruitment pitch* — the entry tactic — distinct from the full business-model arc ([[mlm-lifecycle]], which covers retention, sunk cost, and exit). Its defining feature is **warm-market mining**: the recruiter is told to "make a list of 100" people they know and approach friends, family, and social-media contacts first, so the pitch arrives wrapped in **existing trust** [1]. The diagnostic — the file's center — is **where the income structurally comes from (recruitment vs. real retail sales to outside customers) and whether the income claims are honest** ([1][4]), not the existence of network selling or a friend's enthusiasm (*legitimate small business*).

## Variants & aliases

- **The warm-market approach / "list of 100"** — recruiting personal contacts first, using relationship trust as the prospecting asset (industry training folklore, widely attested; not a regulator term) [1].
- **The "hunbot" DM / "Hey hun"** — a copy-paste cold or lukewarm direct message ("Hey hun! I noticed you're such a go-getter / an entrepreneur…") opening a recruitment pitch on social platforms; a lexicalized, journalism-documented phenomenon [5].
- **Income & lifestyle theater** — rented luxury cars/mansions/jets in photos, "laptop lifestyle," convention success-story testimonials, screenshots of "commissions" — selected success displayed against an unmentioned ~99% who don't profit [1][2].
- **The evasive frame** — "It's not sales," "This isn't an MLM," "I just want to share an opportunity" — recruitment that won't name itself.
- **The "no" reframe** — treating any hesitation as fear, negativity, or "lack of vision," with FOMO and "ground-floor opportunity" urgency.

## How it works

The primary hook is **hope/greed** ([[hope-greed]]): the dream of financial freedom, being your own boss, and passive/residual income. What makes the pitch land is **liking-similarity and borrowed trust** ([[liking-similarity]], [[trust-mechanics]]): you're approached by a *friend or relative* you already like and trust, so the offer inherits that credibility — the defining lever of warm-market recruitment. **Social proof** ([[social-proof]]) supplies the evidence the dream is real — convention testimonials, income screenshots, "everyone's doing this" — selected examples standing in for a representative outcome. **Scarcity/urgency** ([[scarcity-urgency]]) closes it: "ground-floor," "the team is filling up," limited-time rank pushes ([[manufactured-urgency]] register). A small initial **buy-in** (a "starter kit") then opens a [[commitment-consistency]] foothold the broader [[mlm-lifecycle]] exploits. The covertness — recruitment that denies being recruitment, success theater that hides the base rate — is what turns ordinary salesmanship into manipulation.

## Recognition

### Behavioral markers

- An **old friend or distant contact** suddenly reconnects, warm, then pivots to an "opportunity."
- **Money framed without a clear product** — income, freedom, "being your own boss" lead; what you'd actually sell is vague.
- **Recruitment emphasized over retail** — building "a team," "your network," getting others to sign up.
- **Lifestyle proof** (cars, travel, screenshots) substituted for verifiable, after-expenses earnings.
- The opportunity **won't name itself** ("not an MLM," "not sales").

### Typical phrases

"Hey hun! You'd be *perfect* for this." · "Be your own boss / fire your boss." · "Passive income while you sleep." · "I'm building a team and thought of you." · "This isn't sales, it's sharing something I love." · "It's a ground-floor opportunity — the timing is now." · "The only thing stopping you is fear / a negative mindset."

### Felt-sense indicators

- Pressure to say yes **so you don't disappoint a friend** — the ask is tangled up with the relationship.
- Excitement about the *income dream* paired with vagueness about **what you'd actually do or sell**.
- Feeling that doubt is being recast as a **personal failing** ("you just don't have the vision").

### Escalation signs

Repeated follow-ups after a "no"; the friendship feeling conditional on your "yes"; pressure to buy inventory or a bigger "package" to "be serious"; being pulled toward team chats, hype events, and "mindset" content that discourages outside skepticism (the on-ramp to [[mlm-lifecycle]] / [[cults-high-control]] dynamics).

## Where it appears

Direct-sales / "network marketing" recruitment ([[sales]]) and, heavily, social media — Instagram/Facebook DMs and stories where warm-market and "hunbot" outreach happen ([[digital-platforms]]) [5].

## Typical sequences & co-occurrence

It is the **entry point** of the [[mlm-lifecycle]] (which owns the sunk-cost, unity-ingroup, and retention edges). It exploits [[hope-greed]], [[liking-similarity]], [[social-proof]], and [[scarcity-urgency]]; rides on [[manufactured-urgency]] for the close; and co-travels with [[love-bombing]] of new prospects and [[future-faking]] about the income to come.

## Counter-strategies

### In the moment

- **Separate the friend from the pitch** ([[detection-heuristics]]): you can love your friend and decline the business. "I'm not interested in the business, but I'd love to catch up" — and the right to say no to a friend is yours; a real friendship survives it.
- **Ask the retail question**: "What share of income comes from **retail sales to people who are *not* distributors**, versus from recruiting and distributors' own purchases?" This is the legal pyramid line in plain language ([4]).
- **Refuse FOMO**: a legitimate opportunity survives "let me think about it and look at the numbers."

### Structural / long-term

- **Demand and actually read the Income Disclosure Statement** ([[verification-rituals]]); compute **net, after-expenses, using the median** (not the headline averages, which a few top earners distort) — the FTC itself favors median [1].
- **Check the base rate**: across studies, most participants lose money or make nothing (see Evidence) — weigh the *typical* outcome, not the testimonial.
- **Look up the company** for FTC actions, pyramid litigation, and consumer reports before any buy-in.

### When to exit or escalate

If the friendship is being made conditional on joining, or pressure escalates to inventory loading, step back — and see [[mlm-lifecycle]] for exit and sunk-cost recovery. Suspected pyramid schemes can be reported to the **FTC (reportfraud.ftc.gov)**, the **SEC** (if framed as investment), and your **state attorney general**.

## Caveats & false positives

### Not this tactic when…

- **Legitimate direct sales and self-employment are real.** Single-level direct sales, franchising, and genuine reselling exist; not every network-based sale is a scam (*legitimate small business*). The diagnostic is **where the money structurally comes from** — retail sales to outside customers (legitimate) vs. recruitment and participants' own inventory purchases (the pyramid pattern) — not the presence of a network or enthusiasm.
- **A friend excited about a product is not a pyramid.** Ambition, side income, and selling to people you know are not, by themselves, manipulation ([[epistemic-guardrails]]). Concept-creep warning: don't label every enthusiastic seller a scammer.
- **The individual recruiter is usually not a villain.** Most participants are **themselves victims** who sincerely believe the pitch and were recruited the same way, by a trusted friend. Recognition targets the **structure and script**, not the person (CLAUDE.md: label behaviors, not people).

### Base rates & severity calibration

Individual success exists but is **rare**, and a visible success story is [[social-proof]], not a representative outcome — it is selected *against* a loss-or-no-profit base rate that runs from ~73% (AARP, nationally representative) to ~99% (Taylor's MLM-earnings subset) (see Evidence). Severity is **medium**: the harm is financial (lost buy-in, unsold inventory, time) and relational (strained friendships from selling to one's network) — real, sometimes large, but generally recoverable and not a safety matter. It preferentially reaches people under **financial stress** ([[scarcity-stress]]) or seeking **community and belonging** ([[loneliness-isolation]]).

## Evidence & debates

Graded `supported`, with the income reality being the load-bearing, well-evidenced core — but **attribution matters**:

- **The headline "~99.6% lose money" is Jon M. Taylor's figure, NOT an FTC finding** [2]. Taylor (*The Case (for and) Against MLM*, Consumer Awareness Institute, ~2011; submitted as an FTC public comment — which is why it's often *mis*attributed to the FTC) analyzed 350+ MLM pay plans; the **99.6%** loss rate comes from the **subset of ~30 MLMs for which he had average-earnings data** (after expenses, including dropouts), and his broader claim is that **fewer than 1% profit**. Cite it **with that qualifier**; do not generalize 99.6% to "all MLMs."
- **The FTC's own language is softer and has no single percentage** [1]: a "substantial majority" of pyramid participants lose money, and its 2024 staff report on 70 income-disclosure statements found **most participants made $1,000 or less per year**, with **at least 17 MLMs where most made no money at all**; the FTC favors **median** income as the honest metric. Note: the FTC's **2011 Business Opportunity Rule deliberately excluded MLMs**, so "it's a legal/registered MLM" is not the reassurance it sounds like.
- **AARP Foundation (2018, DeLiema & Shadel)** — a nationally representative complement — found **47% lost money and 27% made no money (≈73% combined)**, while ~90% joined hoping to make money [3].
- **Legal line (US; not legal advice):** the **Koscot** test (FTC, 1975) and **FTC v. BurnLounge** (9th Cir., 2014) hold that a pyramid exists where rewards flow **primarily from recruitment** rather than sales to **outside ("ultimate") users**; *FTC v. Amway* (1979) blessed MLMs that enforce real **buyback, inventory (70%), and retail-customer** safeguards [4]. The application is **genuinely contested** (e.g., the Herbalife dispute). The cult/BITE-model framing of high-pressure recruitment is an **advocacy/clinical lens**, useful for recognition but not scholarly consensus.
