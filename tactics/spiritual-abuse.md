# Spiritual Abuse

> Control exerted through God — using scripture, "God's will," divine authority, and the fear of spiritual consequences to make a person obey, stay, give, or submit. It happens not only in cults but inside ordinary congregations, marriages, and families. The hard part is the line: sincere faith, strict belief, and intense community are **not** abuse. The tell is coercion, exploitation, deception, and a punished exit — with God installed as the enforcer of one person's will.

## Definition

Spiritual abuse is **a systematic pattern of coercive and controlling behaviour in a religious context** — control effected through the *misuse* of religious belief, texts, and authority [1][2]. Working from Oakley and Kinmond's research and the definition since adopted by UK safeguarding bodies (Thirtyone:eight) and the Church of England, its characteristic features include: manipulation and pressure; control through the use of sacred texts or teaching; the claim that the abuser holds a **"divine" position** that must not be questioned; requirement of obedience; enforced accountability, secrecy, and silence; censorship of dissent as sin or "the enemy's work"; isolation and superiority/elitism; and control of a person's behaviour, money, sexuality, or relationships in God's name [1][2][3]. What distinguishes it from other control tactics is the **religious framing** — the suggestion that *God* is complicit, that resistance imperils one's soul, standing, or salvation. Unlike a full high-control group ([[cults-high-control]]), it occurs routinely in *mainstream* settings: one abusive marriage, one controlling parent, one congregation. Its underlying machinery is [[coercive-control]] operationalized through faith; that is where its strongest evidence base lives [1][6].

## Variants & aliases

- **Divine-position leadership** — a leader/parent/spouse presented as God's mouthpiece whose word carries God's authority; "touch not God's anointed" shields them from accountability [4].
- **Submission theology weaponized** — male-headship or "obey your elders" teaching used to override an adult's decisions and to keep a partner in an abusive marriage ([[emotional-blackmail]], and see Safety notes) [7].
- **Purity culture** — sexuality controlled through shame, girls framed as "stumbling blocks," bodies policed as sin ([[shame-leverage]]) [8].
- **Doubt-as-sin** — questioning reframed as rebellion, faithlessness, or spiritual attack, so critical thought becomes dangerous ([[thought-terminating-cliches]], [[certainty-needs]]).
- **Exit-as-catastrophe** — leaving framed as losing salvation, family, and community at once; shunning and excommunication as the enforcement ([[isolation-tactics]]).
- **Stewardship/tithing control** — financial extraction or control framed as obedience to God ([[financial-abuse]] adjacent).

## How it works

The levers are unusually strong because they are aimed at the soul. **Fear-exploitation** ([[fear-exploitation]]): the threat is ultimate — divine punishment, hell, loss of salvation — and it is attached to ordinary acts of autonomy, so a boundary feels like a sin. **Authority** ([[authority]]): a human being is installed as an unquestionable proxy for God, converting deference into obedience and making challenge feel like blasphemy [1][4]. **Guilt-leverage** and **shame-leverage** ([[guilt-leverage]], [[shame-leverage]]): sin, unworthiness, and purity language install a permanent, undischargeable debt and a fear of exposure. **Certainty-needs** ([[certainty-needs]]): doubt is recoded as the enemy's voice, so the relief of certainty is priced at obedience. The result is a person who cannot question, cannot set limits, and cannot leave without believing they are risking God, family, and eternity — which is precisely the entrapment that [[coercive-control]] aims for, here with a sacred enforcer.

## Recognition

> Read this section with the Caveats in hand: much of the language below is *also* ordinary devout speech. It becomes tactic-grade **only** alongside coercion, exploitation, and a punished exit — never because the words are religious or the belief is intense.

### Behavioral markers

- A leader, parent, or partner claims to speak *for God*, or holds a "divine position" that puts them beyond question [1][4].
- Dissent, questions, or doubt are reframed as sin, rebellion, faithlessness, or spiritual attack.
- Scripture is deployed to demand a *specific* behaviour — money, sex, staying, obedience — with your compliance framed as obedience to God.
- Confession and transparency are demanded of you but never reciprocated; secrecy ("don't air this outside the church family") is required.
- Belonging is conditional on compliance; exit is framed as spiritual catastrophe and enforced by shunning.

### Typical phrases

"God will punish you / you'll fall away if you leave." · "Submit to your husband / your elders — it's God's design." · "Questioning is the enemy's work — don't doubt your doubts." · "If you leave, you'll lose your family *and* your salvation." · "Touch not God's anointed." · "This stays in the church family — don't slander." · "Your suffering is a cross to bear; a godly wife endures." · "Rebellion is as the sin of witchcraft."

### Felt-sense indicators

- Fear of divine punishment attached to ordinary, reasonable choices.
- Guilt or shame that spikes specifically around one leader, teaching, or community.
- The sense that you can't leave without losing *everyone* you love — and God too.
- A blurring of "what God wants" and "what this person wants," until you can't tell them apart.

### Escalation signs

Enforced confession and monitoring; isolation from "worldly" or questioning friends and family ([[isolation-tactics]]); financial control framed as tithing or stewardship; sexual coercion framed as duty or submission ([[sexual-coercion]]); and shunning, excommunication, or the standing threat of it. The red-line escalations are **minors**, **coerced exit**, and **"submission"-theology used to keep someone in intimate-partner violence** — see Safety notes.

## Where it appears

Families (the controlling religious parent over a child or adult child — [[family-parents]]); marriages and intimate relationships (headship theology weaponized — [[intimate-relationships]]); and religious institutions and congregations short of a full cult ([[institutions]]). The boundary with [[cults-high-control]] is one of degree and completeness: spiritual abuse names the *pattern* wherever it appears, including inside otherwise-mainstream faith settings. The examples and phrases here are drawn from the (Christian-centred) research literature, but the mechanism is tradition-agnostic — the same coercion-through-the-sacred appears in other religions and in secular quasi-spiritual groups (guru, wellness, and "therapy" cults).

## Typical sequences & co-occurrence

It runs on [[conditional-love]] (approval and belonging rationed on compliance) and [[thought-terminating-cliches]] ("let go and let God," "doubt your doubts"), travels with [[isolation-tactics]] and [[we-are-family-rhetoric]] (the congregation as a family whose loyalty overrides your autonomy), and deploys the FOG of [[emotional-blackmail]]. Hardened and generalized, it **escalates to** [[coercive-control]] with a sacred rationale. **Distinguish from [[affinity-fraud]]**, which exploits shared faith to run a *financial scam* rather than to control a life — neighboring, sometimes overlapping, but a different aim.

## Counter-strategies

### In the moment

- **Separate the person's demand from "God's will"** ([[detection-heuristics]]): "That is your interpretation, not a fact I'm bound to obey." A human being is not an unquestionable proxy for God.
- **Don't debate scripture on their terms** ([[jade-avoidance]]): you are not obliged to out-proof-text a person using text as a weapon; decline the frame.
- **Name the pattern privately**: "I'm being shamed, pressured, and threatened with loss for asking a question — that's control, not care."

### Structural / long-term

- **Get an outside perspective** ([[detection-heuristics]]): a different congregation or tradition, a chaplain, or a secular counselor — and talk to people who left *and kept* their faith as well as those who left it entirely.
- **Protect independence first**: money, ID, and outside relationships secured *before* voicing dissent, because dissent is often what triggers the shunning ([[documentation-practices]]).
- **Remember the baseline**: healthy faith communities *tolerate questions and permit exit with relationships intact* — that is the measuring stick.

### When to exit or escalate

Treat leaving like leaving any high-control setting even when it is "just" a family or a single church: expect shunning and re-contact campaigns, plan the loss of community in advance, and line up alternative support first ([[cult-exit-support]], [[recovery-rebuilding]]). For minors, and for "submission"-theology intimate-partner violence, route to safeguarding and DV protocols — **not** just "talk to the pastor" (Safety notes).

## Caveats & false positives

### Not spiritual abuse when…

- **It is sincere, demanding, or unusual faith** (*legitimate religion*): strict doctrine, high commitment, intense community, hard teachings, and *freely-chosen* submission within a genuinely-held theology are **not** abuse. Intensity and strangeness are not the line — **coercion, exploitation, deception, and a punished exit** are. Church discipline that is proportionate, accountable, and leaves exit and relationships intact is not this tactic.
- **You simply disagree with the theology**: "I find this belief wrong or oppressive" is not the same as "this is abuse." Guard hard against anti-religious concept-creep — the steelman is real: formalizing "spiritual abuse" risks smearing whole communities merely for holding beliefs one dislikes [9].
- **Religion is helping, not harming**: faith is, in the research, a *dual* factor — for many survivors it is a source of resilience, meaning, and help-seeking, not a weapon [6]. Do not treat faith itself as the pathogen; treat its *coercive misuse* as the tactic.
- **The fear is self-generated** (scrupulosity / religious OCD): guilt and fear of divine punishment can come from a person's *own* internalized belief or an anxiety disorder, with **no controlling other** present — that is distress to treat with care (often clinically), not a tactic being run on them. This file's pattern requires a coercing person or system.

### Base rates & severity calibration

Most religious devotion — even strict, even all-consuming — is not abuse, and most clergy and communities are not abusers. Widely-circulated prevalence figures (e.g. "two-thirds of churchgoers experienced spiritual abuse") come from **self-selecting online surveys** and must be treated as illustrative, never as a population base rate. The tactic-grade signature is the conjunction: **coercion + exploitation + deception + punished exit**, with religious authority as the enforcer ([[epistemic-guardrails]]). One demanding sermon or one hard conversation about faith is rung-1 evidence of nothing. Graded `high` — controlling and, at the escalations above, dangerous.

## Safety notes

Spiritual abuse can carry hard safety edges: shunning and coerced exit (loss of family, housing, community); **intimate-partner violence sustained by "submission" theology** [7]; and **minors** subjected to control or worse. If you are being told God requires you to endure abuse, that "submission" means tolerating violence, or that leaving will cost you your children or your safety — that is a safety matter, not a theological one. Respond to the risk first, regardless of the belief attached to it.

- **Faith-setting safeguarding (UK):** Thirtyone:eight — thirtyoneeight.org.
- **Religious-abuse / cult exit and recovery:** ICSA — internationalculticstudies.org; Journey Free (Release & Reclaim) — journeyfree.org; and [[cult-exit-support]].
- **If there is intimate-partner violence:** treat as DV — **US** National Domestic Violence Hotline 1-800-799-7233; **UK** Refuge 0808 2000 247; protocol at [[dv-safety-planning]]. **A minor at risk:** child-safeguarding / **Childhelp 1-800-422-4453**; immediate danger **911 / 999**.

## Evidence & debates

Graded **`supported`**: there is peer-reviewed empirical work (Oakley, Kinmond & Humphreys 2018, *Journal of Adult Protection*, reporting the Bournemouth survey and the characteristics model [1]; AIFS 2024 on religious/spiritual abuse in IPV [6]), practitioner and case literature (Enroth's *Churches That Abuse* [4]), and institutional adoption (Church of England safeguarding manual; Thirtyone:eight position paper [2][3]) — with limited controlled-study breadth, which is the `supported` band. Three sub-points are flagged rather than absorbed: (a) whether spiritual abuse should be a **distinct category** is genuinely **`contested`** — critics argue it is already captured by [[coercive-control]] / emotional abuse and warn of religious-freedom overreach [9]; (b) prevalence figures rest on self-selecting samples and are not base rates; (c) "**Religious Trauma Syndrome**" (Winell) is a useful practitioner label but **`folk`** — not a DSM-5 or ICD-11 diagnosis [5]. The pattern is real and well-described; its boundary as a named category is where the honest dispute sits.
