# Stalkerware & Digital Monitoring

> Covert surveillance — secret apps, location trackers, account access — used to monitor and control a partner. The omnipresence is the point: when someone always knows where you are and what you said, you self-censor and stop trying to leave. **Critical safety note up front: checking for or removing monitoring can alert the abuser and escalate danger — read "Safety notes" before you touch the device.**

## Definition

Stalkerware (also "spouseware") and digital monitoring are **the use of technology to covertly surveil and control a partner or family member** — a core instrument of tech-facilitated [[coercive-control]]. It spans commercial spyware apps secretly installed on a phone (reading messages, calls, location, even camera/mic), misused location trackers and shared accounts, and account takeover. The defining anchor is research like Citizen Lab's *The Predator in Your Pocket* (2019) and the **Coalition Against Stalkerware** (founded 2019; EFF, Kaspersky, NNEDV, and others) [1]. The diagnostic — and the line from legitimate monitoring — is **covert, non-consensual surveillance used for control**, not the existence of location-sharing or child-safety tools (*consensual monitoring*).

## Variants & aliases

- **Covert spyware apps** — commercial monitoring apps (mSpy, FlexiSpy, and others) secretly installed during brief physical access to an unlocked phone; the icon is hidden and the installer gets a real-time dashboard [1].
- **Location tracking** — misused **Bluetooth trackers** (e.g., AirTags slipped into a bag or car — a documented stalking problem that prompted Apple/Google "unwanted tracker" alerts), phone location-sharing turned on covertly, or "Find My" via a shared account.
- **Account takeover / compromise** — knowing or guessing passwords to read email, messages, and cloud backups; shared family/cloud accounts used to monitor.
- **Smart-home / IoT surveillance** — cameras, thermostats, and devices the controlling partner administers to watch or intimidate.

## How it works

Monitoring manufactures **omnipresence and the fear of it** ([[fear-exploitation]]): the target learns the abuser somehow always knows — where they went, who they texted, what they searched — so they self-censor, abandon escape plans, and stop confiding in anyone, *without* the abuser issuing a single threat. It supplies the surveillance pillar of [[coercive-control]] and reinforces [[isolation-tactics]] (you can't safely reach the people who'd help). Its power is partly psychological even when the tech isn't there: the *belief* that you're watched is enough to control behavior, which is why an abuser may claim omniscience whether or not spyware is installed.

## Recognition

### Behavioral markers (lead with the behavioral tell, not the tech)

- **The abuser knows things they shouldn't** — private messages, your location, conversations, searches — that you never shared. *This behavioral tell is more reliable than any technical symptom.*
- They show up where you are, "coincidentally," repeatedly.
- They gave you the phone/device as a "gift" (possibly pre-installed) or had unexplained physical access to it.
- They know your passwords or insist on access "because we have no secrets."

### Technical signs (non-specific — see caveat)

- Battery drain, overheating, or data-usage spikes; settings changed (e.g., unknown-source installs enabled); an unfamiliar app or profile.
- **Honesty caveat:** these signs overlap heavily with benign causes (an aging phone, an OS update), and well-built stalkerware can be near-invisible — **absence of symptoms does not mean you aren't monitored**, and presence doesn't prove you are. Weight the *behavioral* tell above the technical one.

### Felt-sense indicators

- The creeping sense of being watched; self-censoring even when alone.
- Afraid to search for help or contact anyone on your own devices.
- A specific dread that you "can't get away" because they always find out.

### Escalation signs

Monitoring intensifying when you assert independence or try to leave; surveillance fused with [[isolation-tactics]], [[financial-abuse]], and threats into [[coercive-control]]; tracking used to locate a partner who has fled. Because stalking and surveillance are recognized intimate-partner-homicide risk factors, escalating monitoring around separation is a serious danger signal, not a nuisance.

## Where it appears

Intimate relationships and ex-partner stalking (the core arena — [[intimate-relationships]]) and families (controlling parents; monitoring of older relatives — [[family-parents]]). The same tools have legitimate uses (child safety, consented location-sharing), which is exactly the cover that covert abusive use hides behind.

## Typical sequences & co-occurrence

The surveillance pillar of [[coercive-control]] (declared there as a constituent), interlocking with [[isolation-tactics]] (declared there) and [[financial-abuse]]. Information gleaned by monitoring feeds [[gaslighting]] ("I know you're lying") and the location-finding that makes leaving dangerous.

## Counter-strategies

### Safety first — before you touch the device (read Safety notes)

- **Plan from a device the abuser cannot monitor** — a friend's phone, a library or work computer — because *anything you do on a monitored device (including searching for spyware or for help) may be seen* [2].
- **Do not remove or confront yet**: abruptly killing the monitoring can alert the abuser and **escalate danger**; sequence it with a safety plan.

### Structural

- **Get specialist tech-safety help**: NNEDV's **Safety Net project** (techsafety.org) and a domestic-violence advocate can guide safe assessment and a plan; **US:** National Domestic Violence Hotline 1-800-799-7233.
- **Harden security once it's safe**: new device or full factory reset, new accounts and strong unique passwords + MFA set up on a safe device, check for unknown trackers (phones now alert to unwanted Bluetooth trackers), review account access and recovery settings.
- **Preserve evidence** ([[documentation-practices]]): document what the abuser knew and how, before removing anything — it can matter for protective orders and prosecution.

## Caveats & false positives

### Not stalkerware/abuse when…

- **Consensual location-sharing** (*consensual monitoring*): partners who *mutually agree* to share location (Find My, Life360) are not abusing each other — the line is consent, transparency, and the freedom to turn it off without punishment.
- **Lawful parental monitoring of minor children**: parents monitoring a young child's device/location for safety is context-dependent and broadly legitimate (it shades toward control when applied covertly to teens to dominate rather than protect).
- **Disclosed employer monitoring of company devices**: monitoring an employer-owned device, with notice, is generally lawful — different from covertly bugging a person's own phone.
- **The line is secrecy + non-consent + control + power asymmetry**: "we share locations" by genuine mutual agreement is not stalkerware; *covert* surveillance, or "consent" extracted under pressure, is. Don't pathologize ordinary couple location-sharing or legitimate child safety ([[epistemic-guardrails]]).

### Base rates & severity calibration

Most people use location-sharing, find-my-phone, and parental controls benignly. The tactic-grade signature: **covert, non-consensual surveillance (secret apps, hidden trackers, account compromise) used to monitor and control**, recognized chiefly by the *behavioral* tell (the abuser knowing un-shareable things) plus secrecy and coercion — not by a single battery-drain symptom. Severity is `high`: it underpins coercive control and enables the stalking that is a documented lethality risk.

## Safety notes

`safety: dv-escalation` — and this is the file's most important content. **Checking for, or removing, monitoring can alert an abuser and escalate danger**, and may destroy evidence. So: **(1)** assume a possibly-monitored device is compromised and **use a separate, safe device** for any research, help-seeking, or planning; **(2)** safety-plan *first* with a domestic-violence advocate / NNEDV's Safety Net (techsafety.org), **before** removing anything; **(3)** do not tip off the abuser or confront them with "I know you're tracking me." Separation is the peak-danger window, and escalating surveillance around it is a serious warning sign. **US:** National Domestic Violence Hotline **1-800-799-7233** / thehotline.org; emergencies 911. Protocol: [[dv-safety-planning]].

## Evidence & debates

Graded `supported`: stalkerware and tech-facilitated abuse are well-documented by security researchers, NGOs, and regulators — Citizen Lab's *The Predator in Your Pocket* (2019), the Coalition Against Stalkerware (2019), NNEDV's Safety Net, and FTC enforcement (the 2021 SpyFone ban barred a stalkerware maker from the surveillance business) [1][3]. The safety-plan-first guidance is strongly and consistently sourced across DV-tech-safety organizations [2]. The honest nuances: recognition by technical symptoms is unreliable (lead with the behavioral tell), and the legitimate-vs-abusive line turns on consent and covertness, not the tools themselves — which is why the counter is safety planning and security hygiene, not symptom-spotting.
