# That's-Not-All & Decoys

> Two ways to steer a choice by dressing up the offer rather than improving the underlying value: **sweeten the deal before you can say no** (the "that's-not-all" bonus that triggers reciprocity), and **add a deliberately inferior option** so the one they want you to buy looks like the obvious pick (the decoy). Both work on how the offer is *framed*, not on what it's actually worth.

## Definition

This file covers two related but mechanistically distinct "offer-shaping" techniques:
- **That's-not-all (TNA)** — improving the deal (adding a bonus, or dropping the price) *before the target responds*, raising compliance via **reciprocity** ("they just gave me extra") and **anchoring/contrast** (the revised offer looks better than the first) (Burger, 1986) [1].
- **The decoy effect (asymmetric dominance)** — adding a third, clearly inferior option to a choice set so that a target option (which dominates the decoy) is chosen more often; it manipulates the **choice architecture / framing**, not the options' real value (Huber, Payne & Puto, 1982) [2].

Both exploit **reciprocity** and **framing-anchoring** rather than the merits, and both sit on the **persuasion–manipulation boundary** — genuine discounts, bonuses, and product tiers are normal commerce ([[puffery]] and ordinary assortment). The diagnostic is **a sweetener timed to short-circuit deliberation, or a decoy engineered purely to steer** — not the existence of a deal or a range of options.

## Variants & aliases

- **The TV-pitch sweetener** — "But wait — that's not all! Order now and we'll also include…"; the bonus piled on before you decide.
- **The pre-emptive price drop** — "And I'll knock off $20" stated before you've responded to the first price (vs. a discount you negotiated).
- **The decoy / asymmetric-dominance option** — a third choice no one's meant to pick, existing only to make the target look like a steal (Ariely's *Economist* print-only-priced-same-as-print+web example) [2].
- **Bundle fog** — options and add-ons structured so comparison is hard and the high-margin bundle looks best.

## How it works

**That's-not-all** wins by timing and reciprocity: by improving the offer *before* you answer, the seller makes it feel like an unsolicited gift (reciprocity pressure to accept) and resets the comparison so the new deal looks generous against the first anchor — and it lands in the *deliberation gap*, before you've thought it through [1]. **The decoy** works differently — there's no request and no reciprocity; it exploits how we judge options *relative to each other*. A clearly-worse third option gives the brain an easy comparison ("this dominates that"), and that easy win pulls choice toward the target option even though the decoy is irrelevant to what you actually want [2]. Both are about *constructing the frame* in which you choose, not about the underlying value.

## Recognition

### Behavioral markers

- A bonus or price cut added *before you respond* ("and that's not all…"), rather than in response to your hesitation or negotiation.
- A third option that seems pointless — clearly worse than one of the others — quietly making that other option look like the obvious choice.
- Offers structured so direct comparison is hard and one bundle is steered as the "smart" pick.

### Typical phrases

"But wait, there's more!" · "Order in the next 10 minutes and we'll also throw in…" · (pricing) a middle option that's almost as expensive as the top one, making the top look like a deal · "Most people choose the [target] — it's the best value."

### Felt-sense indicators

- Feeling you're getting a generous deal because extras were piled on (without checking the base value).
- Gravitating to an option mainly because it "beats" another option in the set, not because it fits your need.

### Escalation signs

Sweeteners timed with [[manufactured-urgency]] ("only if you order now"); decoys combined with fake [[scarcity-urgency]] or fabricated [[social-proof]] ("most popular"); bundle fog hiding the real per-unit cost ([[dark-patterns-social-urgency]] adjacency).

## Where it appears

Sales and direct-response marketing (the core arenas — [[sales]], [[advertising-marketing]]); pricing-page and menu design (the decoy/middle-option); subscription and bundle offers.

## Typical sequences & co-occurrence

TNA is a close cousin of [[door-in-the-face]] (both use reciprocity/contrast) and the broader [[reciprocity]] lever; the decoy is a [[framing-anchoring]] choice-architecture effect. Both pair with [[manufactured-urgency]] and [[scarcity-urgency]] in pressure closes, and decoys/bundle-fog overlap [[dark-patterns-social-urgency]] in digital pricing.

## Counter-strategies

- **Value the thing on its own** ([[detection-heuristics]]): ask "what is this worth / do I want this *for its function and total cost*?" — independent of the bonus piled on or the option it "beats." Sweeteners and decoys lose power once you evaluate the target on absolute terms.
- **Ignore the decoy**: if one option seems to exist only to flatter another, mentally delete it and choose between the options you'd actually consider.
- **Don't decide in the sweetener's moment** ([[verification-rituals]]): "let me think about it" defeats the deliberation-gap timing of that's-not-all; a real bonus survives a pause.
- **Check the base price, not the discount**: compute the actual total and per-unit cost rather than reacting to "and we'll also include…".

## Caveats & false positives

- **Genuine discounts, bonuses, and product tiers are normal** ([[puffery]]): a real sale, a legitimate freebie, or a sensible range of options (good/better/best) that aids choice is ordinary, fair commerce — not manipulation. The line is a sweetener *timed to bypass deliberation* or a decoy *engineered purely to steer*, not the existence of a deal or options.
- **Comparison shopping is good**: presenting options is helpful; the manipulation is a *deliberately inferior* option whose only purpose is to distort the comparison.
- **It's mild** ([[epistemic-guardrails]]): severity is `low` — the harm is a single sub-optimal purchase, easily countered by valuing the item on its own and pausing before deciding.

## Evidence & debates

Graded **`supported`**: That's-not-all is a genuine effect (Burger, 1986 — seven experiments implicating reciprocity and anchoring) but rests on a thinner literature than foot-in-the-door/door-in-the-face/low-ball, with no large meta-analysis, and it works partly by short-circuiting deliberation (so mindfulness reduces it) [1]. The decoy/asymmetric-dominance effect is well-established in the classic numeric-attribute paradigm (Huber, Payne & Puto, 1982) **but genuinely contested at the edges**: replications with naturalistic, non-numeric products have often failed (Frederick, Lee & Baskin, 2014; Yang & Lynn, 2014), with a rebuttal defending robustness (Huber et al., 2014) and recent real-world evidence finding it does occur — so it's real but **stimulus- and context-dependent**, not universal [2]. The honest synthesis: two real offer-framing effects, modest and bounded, on the persuasion side of the line except when engineered to bypass judgment.
